Big Picture: What’s Happening Here?

Only twice in Mark’s Gospel do we hear the voice of the Father. The first time, at the Jordan, he speaks to his Son: “You are my Son, whom I love; with you I am well pleased” (1:11). The second time is here, on a mountain, and this time he speaks to frightened disciples: “This is my Son, whom I love. Listen to him!” Everything else in this passage is what happens when they do. The Father says listen, and Mark immediately lets us hear what the beloved Son says next.

What he says is surprising. He does not keep talking about glory. Coming down the mountain, he talks about rising from the dead, and about why “the Son of Man must suffer much and be rejected.” At the bottom, he walks into an argument, asks what it is about, and then speaks to a desperate father: “How long has he been like this?” He says, “Bring the boy to me.” He commands an evil spirit to leave a child and never come back. And when his disciples ask why they failed, he says, “This kind can come out only by prayer.” Listening to the beloved Son, it turns out, means following his voice down the mountain, toward suffering, toward a hurting family, and toward prayer.

That is the shape of his glory. It is real. On the mountain his clothes become whiter than anyone in the world could bleach them, and Moses and Elijah stand talking with him. But this glory does not stay up there, out of reach. It comes down to a valley full of argument, helplessness, a boy who has been thrown into fire and water since childhood, and a father who can only say, “I do believe; help me overcome my unbelief!” The same Jesus who shines on the mountain takes that boy by the hand. If we want to see his glory, Mark says, look at both places.

Passage Commentary

Glory on the Mountain

Mark 9:2–4

“After six days Jesus took Peter, James and John with him and led them up a high mountain, where they were all alone.” Six days earlier, Jesus had told his disciples plainly that he would suffer, be rejected, be killed and rise again, and he had called anyone who wanted to follow him to take up a cross. Then he promised that some standing there would see the kingdom of God come with power (9:1). Now three of them are taken up a mountain to see something.

“There he was transfigured before them. His clothes became dazzling white, whiter than anyone in the world could bleach them.” Mark reaches for the most ordinary comparison he can find, a launderer’s work, and says this is beyond it. No human hands made this brightness. For a moment the disciples see what has been there all along, hidden in a carpenter from Nazareth who eats with tax collectors and sleeps in boats.

“And there appeared before them Elijah and Moses, who were talking with Jesus.” These are the two great figures of Israel’s story: Moses, through whom God gave the law on a mountain wrapped in cloud, and Elijah, the prophet who also met God on that mountain and was promised to return. They do not come to compete with Jesus or to replace him. They come to talk with him. Israel’s whole history of hearing God gathers around the one on whom it was always leaning.

And the timing matters. The disciples have just heard that Jesus must suffer and die, and they have no idea what to do with it. The transfiguration does not cancel that word. It shows them who is walking that road. The one who will be rejected and killed is not a failed Messiah or a tragic prophet. He is the glorious Son.

“Listen to Him!”

Mark 9:5–8

“Peter said to Jesus, ‘Rabbi, it is good for us to be here. Let us put up three shelters—one for you, one for Moses and one for Elijah.’” Mark adds, almost tenderly, “He did not know what to say, they were so frightened.” Peter is overwhelmed, and he does what frightened people often do in the presence of something holy: he looks for something to build. It is good to be here, he says, and he wants to stay. Shelters would keep the moment. They would also keep Jesus on the mountain, in glory, away from the road down.

God does not answer Peter’s proposal. He answers Peter. “Then a cloud appeared and covered them, and a voice came from the cloud: ‘This is my Son, whom I love. Listen to him!’” The cloud is the old sign of God’s presence, the cloud that covered the mountain when God spoke to Moses. And out of it comes the Father’s own voice, saying two things. First, who Jesus is: my Son, whom I love. Then what to do about it: listen to him. Not build for him, not manage him, not keep him in a place where he only shines. Listen to him, which means listening to everything he says, including what he has just been saying about suffering and rejection, the very words Peter had tried to correct (8:32).

Stay for a moment with the first thing the Father says. Before the command, there is love. My Son, whom I love. The Father does not begin by telling the disciples what they have done wrong. He begins by telling them what is true about his Son and how he feels about him. That is where every command of God begins: in his love for his Son, and in that love reaching toward us.

“Suddenly, when they looked around, they no longer saw anyone with them except Jesus.” Moses and Elijah are gone. The cloud is gone. The brightness is gone. What remains is Jesus, ordinary-looking again, standing with them. That is not a loss. It is the point. The law and the prophets bear witness, but the one to listen to is the one who is left. Glory fades from his clothes, and he is still the beloved Son.

Coming Down the Mountain

Mark 9:9–13

“As they were coming down the mountain, Jesus gave them orders not to tell anyone what they had seen until the Son of Man had risen from the dead.” The very first words the disciples hear after being told to listen to him are about resurrection, and about waiting. The glory they have seen cannot be understood yet. Told now, it would sound like a story about power and spectacle. Only after the cross and the empty tomb will anyone be able to understand what kind of glory this was.

“They kept the matter to themselves, discussing what ‘rising from the dead’ meant.” They are listening, but they do not understand. It is an honest picture of discipleship: obedient, and confused.

Then they ask about Elijah. “Why do the teachers of the law say that Elijah must come first?” They have just seen Elijah on the mountain, and the scholars’ expectation seems to be coming true. Jesus agrees: “To be sure, Elijah does come first, and restores all things.” But then he asks his own question: “Why then is it written that the Son of Man must suffer much and be rejected?” And he adds, “But I tell you, Elijah has come, and they have done to him everything they wished, just as it is written about him.” Mark has already told us what was done to John the Baptist: arrested, then beheaded to keep a promise made at a powerful man’s banquet (6:17–29). The forerunner was treated as the powerful wished. The Son of Man will be too.

So even coming down from glory, Jesus keeps talking about suffering. The restoration God promised has begun, but it has begun through a prophet in prison and a Messiah on his way to rejection. That is not a detour from God’s plan. It is what is written.

An Argument Around a Suffering Child

Mark 9:14–19

“When they came to the other disciples, they saw a large crowd around them and the teachers of the law arguing with them.” No cloud, no shining, no voice from heaven. Just a crowd, religious experts, an argument, and nine disciples who have failed.

Jesus does what the Father has just told the disciples to do with him. He listens. “What are you arguing with them about?” he asks. And the answer comes, not from the experts or the disciples, but from a man in the crowd: “Teacher, I brought you my son, who is possessed by a spirit that has robbed him of speech. Whenever it seizes him, it throws him to the ground. He foams at the mouth, gnashes his teeth and becomes rigid. I asked your disciples to drive out the spirit, but they could not.”

Notice what the argument was about. Not a doctrine. A boy. While the teachers of the law and the disciples argued, a child who could not speak for himself stood at the center of it, still suffering, and his father stood there carrying the weight of another failure. This is one of the oldest temptations of religious people: to turn someone’s suffering into a debate. We argue about causes and blame and who has the right answer, and the person in pain waits. Mark will not let us look away from the boy.

“‘You unbelieving generation,’ Jesus replied, ‘how long shall I stay with you? How long shall I put up with you?’” The words are sharp, and they fall on everyone: the disciples, the experts, the crowd. Jesus has come down from the mountain into faithlessness, and he feels its weight. But the rebuke does not end the scene. His next sentence does: “Bring the boy to me.” The argument has been about the boy. Jesus wants the boy himself. He moves the whole scene away from the debate and toward his own presence. Whatever anyone else has failed to do, the child will not be left at the center of their failure.

“Help Me Overcome My Unbelief!”

Mark 9:20–24

“So they brought him. When the spirit saw Jesus, it immediately threw the boy into a convulsion. He fell to the ground and rolled around, foaming at the mouth.” The nearer the boy comes to Jesus, the worse it looks. Sometimes that is how it is.

Then Jesus asks the father a question no one else seems to have asked: “How long has he been like this?” Jesus wants to know the boy’s story. He lets the father tell it. “From childhood,” he answers. “It has often thrown him into fire or water to kill him.” In those few words is a whole life: years of watching, of fear, of nights awake beside fire and water, of a family that has never been able to rest. The one the Father told us to listen to is himself a listener. He asks a desperate parent to tell him how long, and he hears the answer.

“But if you can do anything, take pity on us and help us.” Notice the word us. The father does not say him. He says us. This suffering has been carried by the whole family. Whatever has happened to the boy has happened to all of them.

“‘If you can’?” said Jesus. “Everything is possible for one who believes.” Jesus picks up the father’s if and hands it back to him. The question is not whether Jesus can. It is whether the father will trust him. And the father’s answer is one of the most honest prayers in Scripture: “I do believe; help me overcome my unbelief!”

This is not polished faith. It is not triumphant confidence. It is faith bringing its own unbelief to Jesus. The father does not wait until his trust is pure before he asks. He brings the whole contradiction, belief and unbelief together, years of disappointment and one last hope, and puts it in front of Jesus. And Jesus does not send him away to come back when his faith is stronger. Anyone who has ever prayed with half a heart, or prayed when hope was almost gone, can pray this prayer. It is enough to bring to him.

Taken by the Hand

Mark 9:25–27

“When Jesus saw that a crowd was running to the scene, he rebuked the impure spirit. ‘You deaf and mute spirit,’ he said, ‘I command you, come out of him and never enter him again.’” Never enter him again. Jesus does not only free the boy for today. He speaks to his future. Whatever has thrown this child into fire and water will not be allowed back.

“The spirit shrieked, convulsed him violently and came out. The boy looked so much like a corpse that many said, ‘He’s dead.’” For a moment, deliverance looks like death. The crowd draws the obvious conclusion.

“But Jesus took him by the hand and lifted him to his feet, and he stood up.” Down, deathlike, taken by the hand, lifted, standing. Mark has shown us this hand before: Jesus took Peter’s mother-in-law by the hand and helped her up (1:31), and he took a dead twelve-year-old girl by the hand and said, “Little girl, I say to you, get up!” (5:41). Here the crowd says, “He’s dead,” and Jesus lifts him, and he stands. Coming right after the disciples’ confusion about what “rising from the dead” meant, it is hard not to see a picture of what is coming. The Son of Man who will rise is already lifting the helpless from the ground.

The boy never speaks in this story. He is spoken about, argued over, brought, thrown down, called dead. But at the end he is not an object anyone is debating. He is a boy whose hand is in the hand of Jesus, standing on his own feet.

Only by Prayer

Mark 9:28–29

“After Jesus had gone indoors, his disciples asked him privately, ‘Why couldn’t we drive it out?’” It is an honest question, and they had reason to ask it. Earlier, Jesus had sent them out, and “they drove out many demons” (6:13). They had done this before. Why not now?

“He replied, ‘This kind can come out only by prayer.’” Jesus does not give them a technique or a better formula. He points them to dependence. Their past success may have become the problem: it is easy, once God has worked through us, to start trusting the experience instead of the God who gave it. Prayer is the posture of people who know the power is not theirs. It keeps ministry from becoming performance, and compassion from becoming one more thing we manage on our own.

So the passage that began with the Father saying “Listen to him” ends with the Son teaching his disciples to pray. Those belong together. To listen to the beloved Son is to be led, again and again, out of our own strength and into prayer.

Mark 9:2–29 In One Movement

The passage moves from a mountain to a valley, from dazzling white to a boy foaming on the ground, and the same Jesus is at the center of both. On the mountain, the Father names him and commands, “Listen to him!” Then Mark lets us listen. The beloved Son talks about rising from the dead and about suffering and rejection. He walks into an argument and asks what it is about. He calls a suffering child to himself. He asks a father how long, and hears the answer. He receives a prayer that is half belief and half unbelief. He commands evil to leave and never return, takes a boy who looks dead by the hand, and lifts him to his feet. He tells failed disciples to pray.

That is his glory. It is not a glory that has to be protected up on the mountain in shelters. It is a glory that comes down to mercy. The brightness Peter, James and John saw was the brightness of the one who would soon be rejected, killed, and raised, and who already, on the way there, was lifting the helpless from the ground.

Scripture Cross-References

Exodus 24:15–18

“For six days the cloud covered the mountain, and on the seventh day the LORD called to Moses from within the cloud.” The mountain, the cloud, the six days, and the voice all echo here. At Sinai God spoke the law. On this mountain the Father points to his Son and says, “Listen to him.”

Deuteronomy 18:15

Moses promised, “The LORD your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among you… You must listen to him.” With Moses himself standing there, the Father uses the same words. The prophet Moses spoke of has come, and he is more than a prophet. He is the beloved Son.

Malachi 4:5–6

God promised to send Elijah, and said, “He will turn the hearts of the parents to their children, and the hearts of the children to their parents.” Jesus says Elijah has come and “restores all things.” Then, at the foot of the mountain, a desperate father and his suffering son are given back to each other.

2 Peter 1:16–18

Years later, Peter would write, “We were eyewitnesses of his majesty… We ourselves heard this voice that came from heaven when we were with him on the sacred mountain.” The frightened man who wanted to build shelters became a witness who never forgot what he heard.

Galatians 4:6

“God sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, the Spirit who calls out, ‘Abba, Father.’” On the mountain the Father says “whom I love” about his Son. Through the Son, the Spirit brings that same love home to us, so that we too can call him Father.

The Shape of a Kingdom People

A community shaped by this passage listens before it builds. Peter’s instinct, to put up shelters and stay, is the instinct of every church that would rather preserve its best moments than follow Jesus into hard places. It is easy to build around worship that feels like the mountain: the music, the gatherings, the experiences we want to keep. The Father’s answer to all of that is the same: “Listen to him!” And when we listen, we find that the beloved Son keeps leading us down, toward suffering people and toward prayer. A church that only wants the mountain has not yet heard what he is saying.

Such a community refuses to argue around suffering. The teachers of the law and the disciples stood in a crowd debating, and in the middle of their debate stood a child who could not speak and a father who had run out of hope. That scene repeats itself constantly. Churches, neighborhoods, and whole nations argue about suffering people: about who deserves help, whose fault it is, what should be done with them, while the people themselves wait at the center of the argument without a voice. A kingdom people will hear Jesus say, “Bring the boy to me,” and will move toward the person in pain instead of standing around them debating. They will ask the kind of question Jesus asked, “How long has he been like this?”, and they will listen to the answer. Families who have carried suffering for years, who have watched their children endure what no child should, who have been failed by the people who were supposed to help, will find in such a church people who listen, stay, and bring them to Jesus.

Such a community will also make room for honest, mixed faith. The father’s prayer is not polished, but it is true, and Jesus received it. A church becomes healthier when people can say, “I do believe; help me overcome my unbelief!” without being shamed for the second half of the sentence. And it will remember that it cannot do this work in its own strength. The disciples had done it before and could not do it now. When a kingdom people faces suffering too deep for its programs and its experience, it will not pretend. It will pray. That prayer is not a last resort after everything else has failed. It is the first breath of a people who know that only Jesus can lift the helpless to their feet.

Gospel-Shaped Response

From this passage we can learn to move in a grace-first order. We begin with who God is and what he has done, because our identity is shaped by his character and work, not by our performance. When we forget those truths, unbelief grows in distorted emotions and patterns of life. Faithful response and mission then flow out of trust in the gospel rather than pressure to earn God’s favor.

Who is God?

1 · Character of God

  • God is the Father who loves his Son, and who speaks that love out loud before he gives any command.
  • God reveals his glory, and his glory comes down. The Son who shines on the mountain walks straight into a valley of suffering.
  • God listens. Jesus asks a desperate father how long his son has suffered, and hears his answer.
  • God is stronger than the evil that torments people, and he speaks to their future: “never enter him again.”

What has God done?

2 · Work of God

  • On the mountain, God showed three frightened disciples the glory of his Son, and told them to listen to him.
  • In Jesus, he walked the road of suffering and rejection that had been written, and was raised from the dead.
  • He received a father’s half-believing prayer, set a tormented boy free, and lifted him from the ground when everyone thought he was dead.
  • He taught his failed disciples to depend on him in prayer, without casting them off.

Who are we in light of what God has done?

3 · New Identity

  • We are people who have overheard the Father’s love for his Son, and who are brought into that love through the Son.
  • We are listeners before we are builders. Our first task is to hear the beloved Son.
  • We are people who may bring mixed faith to Jesus, because his mercy is stronger than our unbelief.
  • We are servants who do not possess spiritual power in ourselves. We belong to the Son who teaches us to pray.

What wrong behavior, harmful emotions, or false attitudes result when we forget these truths?

4 · Unbelief

  • We try to preserve our mountaintop moments instead of following Jesus down into costly mercy.
  • We argue around suffering people, about causes, blame, and who deserves help, while they wait at the center of our debate.
  • We hide our weak faith, pretend certainty, or shame ourselves and others for not being stronger.
  • We trust our past experience and our methods, and stop praying because we think we already know how this is done.

How would we live if we believed these truths?

5 · Faithful Response

  • We would listen to Jesus, especially when he speaks of suffering, resurrection, and dependence.
  • We would pray honestly, bringing both our faith and our unbelief into his presence.
  • We would ask the people who suffer to tell us their story, and listen to the answer, as Jesus did.
  • We would receive correction after failure without despair, because Jesus is still forming his disciples.

If we lived these truths by faith, how might they overflow into the real places and relationships God has given us?

6 · Mission

  • In our neighborhoods, we would move toward families who have carried suffering for years, and bring them to Jesus instead of debating them.
  • In public life, we would refuse to let suffering people be turned into arguments, and insist that they be seen, heard, and helped.
  • In our homes and friendships, we would become safe places for honest weakness, able to pray with others instead of offering quick fixes.
  • In the church, we would measure faithfulness less by visible success and more by listening, mercy, and dependence on Jesus.

Hearing the Father’s Voice

The Bible is God’s love letter to us. When we read it, we do not want accusation, fear, shame, or distance to distort what God has spoken. In this passage, we actually hear the Father speak, and the first thing he says is “whom I love.” He shows us his beloved Son, tells us to listen to him, and then lets us watch that Son come down the mountain to a suffering child. Here is what this passage sounds like when the Father says it to you.

My child, you heard what I said on the mountain: this is my Son, whom I love. I love him. And through him, by my Spirit, I have brought you into that same love; you may call me Father. Listen to him. You will find that he does not stay far above you in the light. He comes down to where you are, to the argument, the failure, the years of suffering no one else has asked you about. He asks you how long it has been, and he waits for your answer.

My child, if you have carried someone you love through fire and water for years, if you have been failed by the people who should have helped, if all you can say is “I do believe; help me overcome my unbelief!”, bring that prayer to my Son. It is enough. He will not send you away until your faith is stronger. He takes the helpless by the hand, and he lifts them to their feet.
