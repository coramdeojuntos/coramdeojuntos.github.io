Big Picture: What’s Happening Here?

Again and again in this passage, someone says who Jesus is. Roman soldiers hail him as king of the Jews. The written charge against him repeats the title. The chief priests and teachers of the law call him the Messiah, the king of Israel. At the end, a centurion calls him the Son of God. Every one of these names is true, and for most of this long day hardly anyone who says them means a word of it.

Beneath all the naming runs a single question: what would a real king do now? The people nearest the cross have their answer ready. Come down. Save yourself. Let us see it, and then we will believe. Even after Jesus has cried out in the dark, someone is still waiting to watch: let’s see if Elijah comes to take him down. Mark answers the question, but not with an argument. He answers it with the way Jesus dies. Jesus meets none of their conditions. He says nothing to anyone who taunts him. He speaks once, to God, in anguish, and then he dies with a loud cry. Only then does Mark take us to the temple, where a curtain is torn from top to bottom, and back to the centurion, who “saw how he died.”

Mark’s readers have known who Jesus is since the first line of the book (1:1). What this passage gives them is a clear sight of what those names mean. The King of the Jews is the one who did not save himself. The Son of God comes into sharpest focus at the moment of his death.

That is hard to receive, because the mockers’ instinct lives in us. We tend to decide what God is like by whether he gets us, or himself, out of trouble on our terms. This passage does not sneer at our need for rescue; the one who will not save himself here is the one who spent his life saving others. But it exposes the bargain the leaders offer — show us first, and then we will trust you — and it gives us something far better than the bargain: a King whose power is spent on others, whose dying is not calm heroism but anguished prayer, and who is seen most clearly in the very place everyone assumed would disprove him.

Passage Commentary

A Robe They Could Put On and Take Off

Mark 15:16–20

The soldiers lead Jesus into the palace, the Praetorium, and call together “the whole company of soldiers.” Pilate has just had him flogged (15:15). Now a room of armed men gathers around one beaten prisoner, and every prop they reach for is royal: a purple robe, a crown twisted together out of thorns, a salute — “Hail, king of the Jews!” — and men falling to their knees in homage. Mark does not let us mistake any of it for worship. Verse 20 names the whole scene: “when they had mocked him.” The honor is a costume, and the costume is a weapon. “Again and again they struck him on the head with a staff and spit on him.” The men who kneel are the men who strike.

The scene depends on a visible contrast: royal signs are laid on a prisoner who has been beaten and is about to be crucified. The purple, the crown, the salute, and the kneeling are used to make him ridiculous. The rest of the passage will overturn that contrast. Here Mark simply lets us watch what armed power does with a prisoner it can humiliate.

Jesus had told his disciples this was coming. On the road up to Jerusalem he said the Son of Man would be handed over to the Gentiles, “who will mock him and spit on him, flog him and kill him” (10:34). By the end of verse 20, three of those four things have happened, and the fourth is waiting at the end of the road. That does not make the soldiers’ cruelty any less their own; they chose it, and Mark records it without softening a line. But the scene is not outrunning Jesus. Nothing in it is news to him.

Then the costume comes off. “They took off the purple robe and put his own clothes on him.” The robe was theirs to give and theirs to take back. His kingship was never theirs to give, and it was not theirs to take. “Then they led him out to crucify him.”

Stay with this room for a moment, because many people have been in rooms like it. A prisoner who cannot defend himself, surrounded by guards who have the power to make him ridiculous, and who use it. If you have been mocked by people with power over you, stripped of your dignity in a cell or a holding room, treated as a joke by the ones guarding you, Jesus has been there. The King of the universe was dressed up and laughed at by soldiers. They did not take his kingship from him, and they cannot take your worth from you.

A Stranger Under Orders and a Drink Refused

Mark 15:21–23

Simon is the one bystander in this passage whom Mark names, and he tells us a good deal about him. He comes from Cyrene, a city on the north coast of Africa, a long way from Jerusalem. He is the father of Alexander and Rufus. He is “passing by on his way in from the country,” a man with a day of his own and somewhere to be. What Mark does say is the one thing that matters most about Simon here: “they forced him to carry the cross.”

The last time Mark spoke of carrying a cross, Jesus was inviting. “Whoever wants to be my disciple must deny themselves and take up their cross and follow me” (8:34). The men who heard that invitation, and who had all insisted they would die with him (14:31), are gone; “everyone deserted him and fled” (14:50). The man carrying the cross now did not want to. Soldiers took him off the road. Mark does not blur the two scenes, and neither should we. This is not a portrait of discipleship. It is a portrait of what Jesus meant when he said that the “rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them” (10:42): power that can reach into any passing life and conscript it. Jesus invited his followers to take up a cross. Rome compels a stranger to carry one. The distance between those two is the distance between two kingdoms. And notice who gets conscripted: a man from far away, coming in from the fields, with no say in the matter. Empires have always found it easy to put their heaviest loads on the backs of people from somewhere else.

“They brought Jesus to the place called Golgotha (which means ‘the place of the skull’).” Mark translates the name and moves on, giving us no landscape and nothing more. Then “they offered him wine mixed with myrrh, but he did not take it.” Mark does not say who prepared the drink or why Jesus turned it away. What we can notice is this: nearly everything in these verses is done to Jesus. He is led, robed and unrobed, struck, led out, brought. This refusal is the one thing he does. Mark lets it stand with no lesson attached, and we should resist attaching one — least of all a lesson about withholding relief from people in pain.

The Charge Written Against Him

Mark 15:24–27 [28]

“And they crucified him.” In English it is four words, and Mark gives no more. He does not describe the method, the body, or the pain, and he does not invite the reader to stare. That restraint is a kind of reverence and a kind of honesty. Whatever this death means, its meaning does not lie in the details of its cruelty.

What Mark does record is small and cold. “Dividing up his clothes, they cast lots to see what each would get.” The clothes the soldiers put back on him a little earlier have become prizes in a game played at the cross. Then Mark marks the hour: “It was nine in the morning.” He will mark it again at noon and at three. This day is not a blur. Each stretch of it is counted.

Then comes the charge. “The written notice of the charge against him read: the king of the jews.” It is the whole case against him in a handful of words. It was written as an accusation, and it was the truest sentence written that day.

“They crucified two rebels with him, one on his right and one on his left.” The charge and the company fit together: a man accused of being king of the Jews is crucified with rebels. That morning Pilate had released Barabbas, who was in prison “with the insurrectionists” (15:7). At his arrest Jesus had asked, “Am I leading a rebellion...?” (14:48). Now he is crucified between two rebels, and the juxtaposition sharpens the irony of the charge written against him. He is counted among criminals. If you have been counted that way, grouped with the guilty, treated as a threat because of where you were found or who you were found with, the Son of God hangs in that company too.

Readers who remember Mark 10 may feel a chill at those words, “on his right” and “on his left.” James and John once asked for those very places: “Let one of us sit at your right and the other at your left in your glory” (10:37). Jesus told them, “You don’t know what you are asking” (10:38). Mark makes no comment here, and we should not make the rebels into more than they are; before long they are insulting him too (15:32). But the echo is hard to unhear. Whatever glory the brothers imagined, the road to Jesus’ glory runs through this place, and the places at his right and left today are places no one would ask for.

Proof on Their Terms

Mark 15:29–32

Now the mockery gathers voices, and Mark keeps them distinct. “Those who passed by hurled insults at him, shaking their heads.” Their taunt — “You who are going to destroy the temple and build it in three days” — echoes the false testimony at the trial, where witnesses said something like it and “even then their testimony did not agree” (14:57–59). Language that could not establish the case there appears again here as a roadside taunt. The chief priests and the teachers of the law mock him too, but Mark notes that they do it “among themselves.” They talk about him, not to him. And “those crucified with him also heaped insults on him.”

Notice what the leaders concede. “He saved others,” they say. Their words concede what the whole Gospel has shown: a man with leprosy made clean, a dead girl raised, the tormented set free, the hungry fed. Then comes their verdict — “but he can’t save himself!” — and then the bargain: “Let this Messiah, this king of Israel, come down now from the cross, that we may see and believe.” Their theology fits in a sentence. A real Messiah would prove it by rescuing himself; seeing comes first and faith afterward; and they will name the sign. The passersby and the leaders, at least, agree on one thing: real power saves itself.

Mark has spent the whole Gospel teaching us to hear this differently. Jesus had said that “whoever wants to save their life will lose it, but whoever loses their life for me and for the gospel will save it” (8:35); that “even the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many” (10:45); and, at the last supper, over the cup, that this was “my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many” (14:24). The leaders have the facts exactly right and the meaning upside down. He saved others. He does not save himself. In Mark’s telling these are not two facts in tension. They are a single act, seen from both sides.

And “can’t” is their word, not Mark’s. Mark never tells us that Jesus had run out of power. The night before, in Gethsemane, he prayed, “Abba, Father,” and then, “everything is possible for you. Take this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will” (14:36). He is on the cross by Roman force, and Mark never pretends otherwise. But the deeper reason he stays is one the mockers cannot see: a will already given over to his Father, for the sake of the many.

That refusal belongs to Jesus alone, because the mission belongs to him alone. No one else gives a life “as a ransom for many.” His staying on the cross is not a pattern for anyone else to remain in harm’s way, and it gives no one permission to leave a suffering person there. The one who would not save himself is the one who spent his life saving others, and who told his own followers to flee when danger came (13:14). He stayed so that others could be saved. His staying was never meant to keep anyone else in danger.

Darkness and the Only Words He Speaks

Mark 15:33–36

“At noon, darkness came over the whole land until three in the afternoon.” Mark gives this darkness no cause and no caption. Readers steeped in the prophets have heard judgment in it, and mourning, and Mark does not choose between them for us. What he makes unmistakable is where it falls: three hours of darkness, and then a voice.

For hours now, people have talked at Jesus and about him: soldiers, passersby, chief priests, teachers of the law, men dying beside him. He has answered none of them. Now, for the first time in this whole passage, he speaks, and not to them. “Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani?” Mark gives us the words as they sounded and then translates them: “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?”

This is anguish, and it should be heard as anguish. The words are the opening line of Psalm 22, the lament of a righteous man suffering unjustly, but Jesus is not reciting a text to make a point. He is praying it, from inside the darkness. Yet notice what he still says: my God. In Gethsemane he called him “Abba, Father.” Here the address has changed, but it is still an address, and it is still “my.” Even in this forsakenness, he cries to God. The one thing he does not do is stop speaking to him.

This cry is more than the feeling of abandonment. Jesus is entering real forsakenness as he bears the judgment of sin and gives his life for others. The darkness is not calm heroism; it is the terrible depth of the saving work he has chosen to undergo. And yet, even here, he says, “My God.” Forsaken, he still addresses God as his God. The cry does not mean that Jesus has ceased to be the Son, lost his divinity, or stopped trusting God. It means that the Son has gone into the depth of judgment rather than coming down from the cross.

What follows is almost unbearable. “When some of those standing near heard this, they said, ‘Listen, he’s calling Elijah.’” They take his prayer and turn it into a call for rescue. “Someone ran, filled a sponge with wine vinegar, put it on a staff, and offered it to Jesus to drink.” Mark does not say whether this was kindness or cruelty, and he does not say Jesus drank. He records only what the man said: “Now leave him alone. Let’s see if Elijah comes to take him down.” Even the drink has become part of the waiting, and the waiting has become a show.

Mark’s readers know something the bystanders do not. Coming down from the mountain where he was transfigured, Jesus told his disciples that Elijah had already come, “and they have done to him everything they wished” (9:13). No prophet is on his way to lift Jesus down. The rescue they are waiting to watch is not the rescue that is happening.

How He Died

Mark 15:37–39

“With a loud cry, Jesus breathed his last.” Mark gives that cry no words. Jesus does not come down. Elijah does not come. No one takes him down. Every condition set at the cross goes unmet, and he dies. The only taking-down Mark records comes afterward, when Joseph of Arimathea takes down the body to bury it (15:46).

Then Mark turns from the cross to the temple. “The curtain of the temple was torn in two from top to bottom.” He names no one who tore it. “From top to bottom” invites the inference many readers have drawn — that this tearing came from above and not from any human hand — and Mark leaves it there. Earlier, passersby had mocked Jesus as the man who would destroy the temple. Now, at his death, something happens in the temple. Christians have read the torn curtain as the way into God’s presence thrown open, and as a sign of judgment on the temple whose stones, Jesus said, would not be left one on another (13:2). Mark does not choose between those readings, and neither of them needs to become contempt for the temple that Jesus, quoting Scripture, had called God’s house, “a house of prayer for all nations” (11:17). What the curtain does make plain is that this death is not a private tragedy at Golgotha. It reaches the temple.

Mark’s readers may also remember that his Gospel opened with a tearing. At Jesus’ baptism “he saw heaven being torn open,” and a voice said, “You are my Son” (1:10–11). Now, near the end, a curtain is torn, and a human voice says “Son of God.”

“And when the centurion, who stood there in front of Jesus, saw how he died, he said, ‘Surely this man was the Son of God!’” Mark is careful about what the centurion saw. He was not at the temple; he was standing in front of Jesus. He did not see a rescue or a descent. Mark tells us only that he saw “how he died,” and the dying Mark has just shown us is a cry to God, a loud cry, and a last breath. The leaders had demanded to see him come down and then believe. This man saw him stay, saw him die, and spoke.

Mark does not tell us how much the centurion understood, and it may have been far less than the reader understands. Nor does Mark tell us what became of him, and we should not supply a conversion he does not narrate. But the sentence stands where Mark put it. Readers have heard Jesus called God’s Son since the book’s first line (1:1): from heaven (1:11; 9:7), from spirits that cried out when they saw him (3:11; 5:7), and in a hostile question from the high priest, which Jesus answered, “I am” (14:61–62). Now a human voice says it plainly, with no question and no sneer, and the voice belongs to a Roman officer at the execution. That does not excuse what Rome did that day. It means the truth about Jesus was spoken aloud in the very place the violence was done.

So this is where Mark brings Jesus’ names into their sharpest focus: not in a rescue, and not in the spectacle the leaders asked for, but in the way he dies — mocked, robbed of his clothes, counted with rebels, taunted, crying out to God, and giving his life. Nobody at the cross got the sign they demanded. One man looked at what was in front of him and saw the Son of God.

Mark 15:16–39 In One Movement

From the Praetorium to Golgotha, the names keep coming. The soldiers use the royal title as a joke, the written charge uses it as an accusation, and the leaders turn Messiah and king of Israel into a dare with a bargain attached: come down, and we will believe. Jesus meets none of it. He is robed and unrobed, led out, crucified, gambled over, counted with rebels, and taunted by people passing, by people in charge, and by men dying beside him. When at last he speaks, it is to God, and what he says is a question these verses leave unanswered. Then he dies, and Mark brings the truth into focus: a curtain torn from top to bottom, and a Roman officer who has been standing in front of him saying “Son of God.” The names were true all along. What changes here is not who he is but what those names are shown to mean. The King of the Jews is the one who did not save himself, because he had come to give his life as a ransom for many. Mark leaves the darkness unexplained and the cry unanswered within these verses, and the promise that he would rise after three days (8:31) waits beyond the tomb. But the reader has now seen what the leaders demanded to see, and it looks nothing like what they expected. It looks like a man dying for others, and it is the King.

Scripture Cross-References

Psalm 22:1

“My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Why are you so far from saving me, so far from my cries of anguish?” Jesus’ cry takes up the opening line of this psalm, the lament of a righteous man suffering unjustly. The psalm moves on toward hope, but Mark puts only its first line on Jesus’ lips, and the hope further on does not cancel the anguish of that line.

Isaiah 50:6; 53:12

“I did not hide my face from mocking and spitting.” And: “he poured out his life unto death, and was numbered with the transgressors. For he bore the sin of many.” Mark does not quote Isaiah here, but the soldiers’ spit, the rebels on either side, and the “many” of Mark 10:45 belong to the servant’s story.

Amos 8:9

“I will make the sun go down at noon and darken the earth in broad daylight.” In the prophets, darkness at noon is a sign of God’s judgment joined to mourning. Mark does not explain the three hours of darkness, but this helps us sense why Christians have heard both in them.

Hebrews 13:12–13

“Jesus also suffered outside the city gate to make the people holy through his own blood. Let us, then, go to him outside the camp, bearing the disgrace he bore.” Golgotha was outside, among the condemned. Those who follow him go out there too, to stand with the people the city has put outside.

Mark 8:34–35

“Whoever wants to be my disciple must deny themselves and take up their cross and follow me.” At Golgotha the one man carrying the cross has been forced to. The invitation of 8:34 still stands, but it is an invitation; Rome’s conscription is its opposite.

The Shape of a Kingdom People

A people who belong to this King cannot use his name to lord it over anyone. The passage puts domination on full display: a company of soldiers entertained by one beaten man, a passerby taken off the road and made to carry the cross, a public place where anyone could stop and add an insult. Jesus’ kingship is shown in giving his life, not in taking anyone else’s. So whatever authority his people hold — in a church, a household, a workplace, a classroom — it carries no license to humiliate, coerce, or conscript. Simon’s forced carrying is not a model of service; it is a warning about power. A leader who presses others to carry burdens for the leader’s own plans and calls it cross-bearing stands much closer to the soldiers than to the King.

Mockery in this passage is contagious. It begins with soldiers and spreads to people passing by, to religious leaders, even to men dying the same death. A kingdom people learns to be the place where it stops: refusing to laugh along when someone weaker is made ridiculous, refusing to repeat a slander because it makes a good line — the way failed false testimony echoes again as a roadside taunt — and guarding the dignity of people whom power finds easy to despise: prisoners, detainees, the poor, the sick and disabled, foreigners, people on the losing side of a system. When the powerful make sport of the weak, in a holding cell or a public speech, a kingdom people will not laugh along. It will go to them, as Hebrews says, “outside the camp,” and bear some of the disgrace with them. Telling the truth about this passage belongs here too. Mark names particular actors: soldiers, a governor, chief priests and teachers of the law, passersby, rebels. A church that reads him faithfully will not excuse Rome because a centurion spoke well at the end, and it will never again blame the Jewish people as a whole for what particular leaders did. Each of us can instead find our own instincts among the mockers — the contempt for weakness, the demand for proof — and bring them to the one they mocked.

Because the one who stayed on the cross is the one who “saved others,” his people are rescuers. His refusal to save himself was his own unrepeatable work for the many. It is no rule for anyone else. The same Jesus told his followers to flee when danger came (13:14) and prayed that the cup might be taken from him (14:36). So a church shaped by this passage helps people get out of abusive homes, calls the doctor, seeks legal protection for the vulnerable, and supports escape, and it never tells anyone that enduring harm is holier than being safe. Pain is not holy in itself, and no one’s suffering earns anything from God. There is also suffering that cannot yet be taken away. There the bystanders’ posture — “let’s see” — is the wrong one: watching, waiting for a sign, treating someone’s agony as a test of whether God will show up. A kingdom people stays near instead, without demands and without diagnoses, and lets people pray the words Jesus prayed — “why have you forsaken me?” — without rushing to correct them, explain them, or treat the question as a failure of faith.

And a kingdom people points to Jesus the way Mark does. The truest thing said about him that day came from a man who had watched him die. We do not need to make more of the centurion than Mark makes of him. But our witness will ring truest when it looks less like a display of power and more like a life given away, because that is how the King was seen.

Gospel-Shaped Response

From this passage we can learn to move in a grace-first order. We begin with who God is and what he has done, because our identity is shaped by his character and work, not by our performance. When we forget those truths, unbelief grows in distorted emotions and patterns of life. Faithful response and mission then flow out of trust in the gospel rather than pressure to earn God’s favor.

Who is God?

1 · Character of God

  • God is the one the Son still calls “my God,” even in the dark. The anguish of the cry does not make him any less Jesus’ God, and it does not make him any less ours.
  • God shows what his Son’s kingship means by the way the Son gives himself. The clearest portrait of the King is the way he died.
  • God’s purpose runs beneath this whole day without excusing anyone in it. Jesus named the mocking, the spitting, the flogging, and the killing in advance (10:34), and every one of them remained the choice of the people who did it.

What has God done?

2 · Work of God

  • In Jesus, God has given a life as a ransom for many and poured out covenant blood for many (10:45; 14:24).
  • Jesus stayed where the taunts told him to come down. The mockers said he could not save himself; Mark has shown us a Son who had already said to his Father, “not what I will, but what you will.”
  • At his death the curtain of the temple was torn from top to bottom, a sign Mark leaves open and Christians have long received as the way to God thrown open.
  • The truth that he is the Son of God was spoken aloud not at a moment of triumph but at his death, by someone who had watched him die.

Who are we in light of what God has done?

3 · New Identity

  • We are among the “many” for whom he gave his life. We do not have to prove ourselves to be his, any more than he had to prove himself by coming down.
  • We are people who may say “my God” with a question still in our mouths. Lament belongs to the Son’s own prayer, so it belongs to his people.
  • We are subjects of a King who refused to dominate, which makes us servants of one another rather than lords over one another (10:42–45).

What wrong behavior, harmful emotions, or false attitudes result when we forget these truths?

4 · Unbelief

  • We set conditions for trust: come down, fix this, show me, and then I will believe. That is the leaders’ bargain, not the Son’s prayer.
  • We treat weakness, our own or someone else’s, as evidence that God cannot be present. The soldiers’ mockery turns visible weakness into a reason for ridicule; the passage asks whether we do the same.
  • We join the laughter when someone is made ridiculous, or repeat a charge we know to be false because it makes a good line.
  • We use whatever power we have to make others carry what we will not carry, and we call it service.

How would we live if we believed these truths?

5 · Faithful Response

  • We would look at how Jesus died long enough for it to change what we think power is for.
  • We would pray honestly in the dark, calling God ours even while the “why” stays open.
  • We would receive rescue, care, medicine, and protection without guilt, and help others receive them.
  • We would worship him as King now, without waiting for a spectacle to make it reasonable.

If we lived these truths by faith, how might they overflow into the real places and relationships God has given us?

6 · Mission

  • In families, churches, and workplaces, we would ask what the people under our authority are being made to carry, and lift what should never have been laid on them.
  • We would stand beside people who are being mocked, humiliated, or harmed — helping them reach safety where safety can be found, and staying near when suffering cannot yet be removed.
  • We would speak of Jesus the way Mark does: here is who he is — look at how he died.

Hearing the Father’s Voice

The Bible is God’s love letter to us. When we read it, we do not want accusation, fear, shame, or distance to distort what God has spoken. In this passage, the Father shows us that his Son is the true King, made known not by coming down from the cross but by giving his life there for many. Here is what this passage sounds like when the Father says it to you.

My child, you have heard the voices at that cross telling my Son to come down and save himself. He did not come down. He stayed, and he stayed for the many, and you are among them. If you have been mocked by those with power over you, counted among criminals, stripped of your dignity, know that my Son was all of these things, and he is still the King. What they did to him did not make him less my Son, and what has been done to you does not make you less my child. You do not have to set terms before you trust me, and you do not have to prove yourself to be mine. Look at how he died. That is what his kingship looks like, and that is how much you are loved.

My child, when the dark comes and you cannot see why, you may say what my Son said: my God, why? Bringing me that question is not unbelief. I do not promise that every darkness will lift when you ask. But his staying was his gift to you, not a chain I place on you; where help and safety can be found, receive them. And where your sorrow cannot yet be taken away, keep calling me yours.
