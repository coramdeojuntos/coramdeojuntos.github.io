Devotional commentary

The Gospel of Mark

Twenty-five devotionals for reading Mark slowly, with your Bible open

How each devotional is shaped

These devotionals are an aid, not a replacement for the Bible. Start with your Bible open: read the passage itself first, slowly, before you read anything here. Then let the devotional walk beside you like a wise companion, pointing out connections and details that are easy to pass by. Stay in the text as you read. When the devotional points to something, look back at the passage and see it there for yourself. And read prayerfully: stop when something strikes you, and talk to God about it.

Each devotional is meant to be read slowly, over several days. You might take a week with a single one. Read the Big Picture first. Then work through the Passage Commentary a little at a time, maybe one section a day, only five or ten verses, because there is so much there to notice and pray through. Keep a notebook nearby. The later sections gather everything together and become more practical, so they are best read once you have lived with the passage for a while.

Every devotional follows the same shape:

Big Picture: What's Happening Here?
The doorway into the passage. It helps you see what kind of moment you're stepping into, what it shows us about Jesus, and why it matters for us.

Quick Reference Verse Map
A simple map of the passage's movements, so you can see the whole before looking closely at each part.

Passage Commentary
The heart of each devotional. We walk through the passage together, slowing down where Jesus is especially beautiful and noticing details that are easy to pass by.

In One Movement
A step back, so the whole passage can be heard at once: how its scenes come together, and what they say together that no single scene says alone.

Scripture Cross-References
A few other passages that shed light on this one. Each shows that this moment belongs to the whole story of God, and then sends you back to Mark.

The Shape of a Kingdom People
The good news doesn't only save individuals; it forms a people who are sent. This section asks what kind of community the passage makes, and how its life together becomes good news for the neighborhood and the world, especially for those who are poor, overlooked, or far from home.

Gospel-Shaped Response
Six questions that help us respond to the passage in the order grace teaches: first who God is and what he has done, then who we are because of it, and only then how we live and how that overflows to others. The questions also help us notice what grows in us when we forget these truths, so that our response comes from trust and not from pressure.

Hearing the Father's Voice
The Bible is God's love letter to his children, and if we read it and never hear the Father say "I love you," we are listening to some other voice, whether accusation, fear, or shame. This section is there to help us hear him.

It is written in the Father's own voice, but it is not Scripture, and it is not a new word from God. It is a pastoral meditation: the truths of the passage you have just read, spoken to you the way a loving Father would say them. Everything in it grows out of the passage and the rest of Scripture, and the only words in quotation marks are Scripture itself.

Think of it as practice. You can do the same with any passage you read. Ask what it shows you about who God is and what he has done, and then listen for how he is saying "I love you" through it.
