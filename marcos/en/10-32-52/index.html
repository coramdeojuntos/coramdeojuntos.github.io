The Son of Man Who Stops on the Way to the Ransom

Big Picture: What’s Happening Here?

Near the end of this passage Jesus stops walking, and the moment only means something if we have watched how he has been walking. At the start he is out in front on the road to Jerusalem, ahead of disciples who are astonished and followers who are afraid, and he tells the Twelve exactly what is waiting there: he will be handed over to the chief priests and the teachers of the law, condemned, given to the Gentiles, mocked, spat on, flogged, and killed — and three days later he will rise. Nothing in the next two scenes slows him. Not a request for thrones, not a quarrel among his friends. Then, on the way out of Jericho, a blind man at the side of the road starts shouting, and the one who has been walking ahead of everyone stands still.

Hold those two facts together, because they belong to the same person. Between them Mark sets the sentence the whole passage leans on: “For even the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many.” Jesus is walking toward that ransom. It is his alone. No follower pays any part of it, and no follower can turn it into a credential. Yet the one walking toward it has room, on the way, for Bartimaeus. Bartimaeus does not illustrate the ransom. He meets the Servant who is on his way to it.

Mark does not make these scenes say the same thing. The brothers ask for places of honor; the ten bristle; Bartimaeus asks for his eyes. What joins them is the Lord they are all speaking to, and what he does with each request tells us what his greatness is made of. Somewhere in this passage most of us will find our own voice — in the wish to be close to Jesus and to count for something there, in the irritation when someone else seems to get further ahead, or in a cry for mercy that the people around us would prefer we kept to ourselves. Before Mark asks anything of us, he shows us the one walking in front, and tells us what he is walking toward.

Passage Commentary

Jesus Walks Ahead

Mark 10:32–34

“They were on their way up to Jerusalem, with Jesus leading the way.” In one sentence Mark lets us see the shape of the whole company: Jesus in front, the disciples astonished, those following afraid. He does not explain either reaction, and we do not need him to. What matters is the arrangement. The only person on this road who knows exactly what waits at the end of it is the one setting the pace.

Then, for the third time, Jesus takes the Twelve aside and tells them. The earlier warnings (8:31; 9:31) spoke of rejection, killing, and rising. This one is fuller than either. It names the city — “We are going up to Jerusalem” — and it lays out a sequence: delivered over to the chief priests and the teachers of the law, condemned to death, handed over to the Gentiles, mocked, spat on, flogged, killed. When Mark later tells these events, he tells them with the same care about who did what. Particular people with particular power will do this to Jesus. The prediction leaves no room for blaming a whole people, and no room for pretending that what is done to him is anything but cruelty.

Notice the “we.” Jesus includes the Twelve in the journey even while he describes what will happen to him alone. And notice the last sentence: “Three days later he will rise.” The resurrection is part of the prediction from the beginning. Jesus walks toward the suffering knowing that it is not the end of the road. That does not make the spitting or the whip less terrible; Mark will show us, in Gethsemane, how heavy the cup was for him (14:33–36). It means the one walking ahead is not being dragged. He is going somewhere, on purpose, to do something.

Seats Requested, a Cup Offered

Mark 10:35–40

Mark moves straight from the prediction to the request, and the placement does its own work. Jesus has just spoken of mockery and flogging; James and John come to him with a request that has no limit built into it: “We want you to do for us whatever we ask.” There is no need to make villains of them. They believe Jesus will have glory, and they are right about that (8:38; 14:62). They want to be beside him when it comes. What they do not yet know is the road to it.

Jesus neither signs the blank page nor tears it up. He asks, “What do you want me to do for you?” It is the question of someone willing to hear a request all the way through, and it lets them say plainly what they want: “Let one of us sit at your right and the other at your left in your glory.”

His answer is honest in two directions. “You don’t know what you are asking.” Then a question of his own, about a cup he will drink and a baptism he will undergo. Their reply is two words — “We can” — and later, when Mark shows us what that cup costs Jesus, he is on the ground in Gethsemane asking his Father to take it from him (14:36). Whatever that cup holds, no one should agree to it lightly. Yet Jesus does not withdraw his friends from it. “You will drink the cup I drink and be baptized with the baptism I am baptized with.” He promises them a real share in his road, suffering included, though never a share in what his suffering alone would accomplish.

What Jesus will not do is hand out the places of honor they ask for. “To sit at my right or left is not for me to grant. These places belong to those for whom they have been prepared.” The places are real, and they already belong to someone. What Mark makes plain is what Jesus refuses to become. Whatever lay behind the request, it asked him to hand out positions to those closest to him, and he will not do it — not even for these two. The one person in the scene with real authority declines to spend it on status. That refusal is itself the first lesson in the greatness he is about to describe.

Only once more does Mark place anyone at Jesus’ right and left. At the cross two rebels are crucified with him, “one on his right and one on his left” (15:27). After Golgotha it is hard to reread the brothers’ request without feeling what it actually looked like, on that day, to be beside Jesus.

Not So with You

Mark 10:41–45

When the ten hear about the request, they become indignant with James and John. What follows shows that the trouble is not confined to two brothers. Earlier on this same journey the whole group had argued on the road about which of them was the greatest (9:33–34). So Jesus calls all of them together. The correction is for everyone.

He begins with the world they know. “You know that those who are regarded as rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them, and their high officials exercise authority over them.” This is a description of how power ordinarily works: the higher you rise, the more people exist to serve you. Jesus is not ranking nations. He is naming a pattern every society recognizes, including the ones that think of themselves as devout. He sees it clearly, and he does not pretend it is anything but what it is. If you live under people who lord it over you, who use their position to push you down, treat you as there to serve them, or decide your future without asking you, Jesus has named what they are doing. He does not call it the way things must be. He calls it the way his people must never be.

Then: “Not so with you.” Jesus does not tell his followers to stop wanting to be great. He tells them where greatness lives among them. “Whoever wants to become great among you must be your servant, and whoever wants to be first must be slave of all.” The ambition is turned around and pointed toward other people’s good, and the reach is complete — of all — so that no one falls beneath this kind of attention or outside it.

It matters who is being addressed. These words are spoken to people who want to be great, about how they will use whatever standing they gain. They restrain the powerful. They are never an instruction to the vulnerable to accept being lorded over, and they cannot be made into one without contradicting the very sentence that forbids lording it over anyone. Jesus has shown only moments before that serving someone does not mean doing whatever they ask.

Then comes the sentence on which everything turns: “For even the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many.” The first word, “For,” carries the argument. Jesus does not ground this new way of life in an ideal, or in the disciples’ ability to live up to it. He grounds it in himself. The next word, “even,” sharpens it. The Son of Man is the one Jesus has spoken of as coming in his Father’s glory (8:38), the one he will say is to sit at the right hand of the Mighty One (14:62). If anyone had the right to be served, it was him. He came to serve.

And he came to do something none of his followers could imitate. A ransom is the price paid to set someone free, and here the price is his own life. What the sentence says is enough to steady the whole passage: the service of Jesus ends in a gift that belongs to him alone, given for many. A few verses back, the disciples asked, “Who then can be saved?” and heard, “With man this is impossible, but not with God” (10:26–27). Now the Son of Man says what he is going up to Jerusalem to do.

That is why this sentence lifts those who hear it instead of flattening them. If it held out only an example — be like Jesus, give everything — it would leave us admiring him and failing him. It holds out something done for us. The disciples are called to serve because they have first been served, at a cost none of them could meet. Their service will never complete his ransom, and it can never become a way of earning a place he has already secured. It can only answer him.

Jesus Stops

Mark 10:46–52

Jericho is the last stop Mark names before the approach to Jerusalem, and he has the whole procession on its way out — Jesus, his disciples, a large crowd. At the edge of all that motion sits a man who is not moving: “a blind man, Bartimaeus (which means ‘son of Timaeus’), was sitting by the roadside begging.” Mark gives us his name and his father’s. He states his circumstances plainly — he cannot see, and he lives on what passersby give him — and makes nothing more of them. There is no hint that his blindness is a judgment, and we should not reduce it to a lesson or a symbol. It is his life, and he wants it changed.

He hears before he sees. “When he heard that it was Jesus of Nazareth, he began to shout, ‘Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me!’” No one in Mark has called Jesus Son of David before. Soon those going ahead of Jesus and following him into Jerusalem will shout about “the coming kingdom of our father David” (11:10), and Jesus himself will ask how the Messiah can be David’s son when David calls him Lord (12:35–37). Mark does not tell us how much Bartimaeus understood when he used the title. He lets it ring out on the approach to the city where it will matter most, and he tells us what the man asked for: mercy.

“Many rebuked him and told him to be quiet.” Mark gives no reasons. He records the effect: a poor man with a need is told to keep it to himself. It is what crowds have always done to people at the side of the road. Their need is inconvenient, their timing is bad, their voices are too loud, and the procession has somewhere important to be. And Bartimaeus refuses. “He shouted all the more.” Mark lets us feel his determination: loud, persistent, unwilling to be managed out of the way.

If you have been told to be quiet, that your need is too much, that this is not the time, that people like you should wait at the edge, hear what Bartimaeus did, and hear what Jesus did next. And if you have ever been part of the crowd, embarrassed by the person shouting, eager to move things along, notice whose side Jesus takes. The one pushed to the edge may be the one who sees Jesus most clearly.

“Jesus stopped.” After everything this passage has shown us, those two words are astonishing. The one who has been walking ahead of everyone toward Jerusalem, who has just said that he came to give his life for many, stands still for one voice from the side of the road. His errand is the largest there has ever been, and it does not make one shouting man too small to hear. Mark does not call this service, and he does not need to. He lets us watch the Son of Man of verse 45 in Jericho, and we see that on the road to the ransom there is time for a beggar’s cry. Bartimaeus does not illustrate the ransom. He meets the Servant who is on his way to it.

Jesus says, “Call him,” and the crowd’s part in the scene turns over. The voices that said “be quiet” now say, “Cheer up! On your feet! He’s calling you.” Mark does not claim the crowd became better people; he shows that Jesus’ word changed what they were carrying. Bartimaeus throws his cloak aside, jumps to his feet, and comes. Mark catches the speed of it and leaves it there.

Then comes the question James and John heard: “What do you want me to do for you?” It might seem obvious what a blind man would ask for. Jesus asks anyway, and lets him say it in his own words — a courtesy the crowd had not extended. The same question has now drawn out two very different requests. Bartimaeus answers at once: “Rabbi, I want to see.”

“Go,” said Jesus, “your faith has healed you.” Faith here has a shape we have just watched: hearing about Jesus, crying out to him, refusing to be silenced, coming when he called. It was not a quantity Bartimaeus produced or a technique he performed. It was trust that brought him to Jesus, and Jesus is the one who healed. Mark tells us what happened to this man on this road. He does not promise that everyone who trusts Jesus and asks for sight or health will receive it in this life, and many who have trusted him with everything are still waiting. Their faith is not smaller for it.

“Immediately he received his sight and followed Jesus along the road.” Jesus had told him to go; he could have gone anywhere. He follows — onto the road Jesus has been walking since verse 32, the road up to Jerusalem. It is enough to see where the passage leaves him: no longer beside the road but on it, walking behind the one who stopped for him.

Mark 10:32–52 In One Movement

Jesus sets out ahead of everyone, knowing what Jerusalem holds and saying so, down to the spitting and the third day. Two friends ask him for glory, and he offers them his cup while declining to hand out the places they want. Ten more take offense, and he gathers them all to hear that among his people greatness bends toward others, because he himself came to serve and to give his life as a ransom for many — a gift none of them can pay toward or add to. Then, with Jerusalem close, he stops for a man the crowd wanted quiet, asks him what he wants, and gives him sight. None of this finishes the story. The cup is still ahead; the disciples have not yet been shown to understand; the road still climbs toward the city. But a man who could not see is walking on it now, behind the one who is going there to give himself. The brothers’ request, the ten’s indignation, the beggar’s cry — every voice in this passage has reached the same Jesus, and he has answered each of them as the Servant who is also the Son of Man.

Scripture Cross-References

Isaiah 53:10–12

Isaiah’s servant of the Lord suffers on behalf of others and accomplishes for them what they could not accomplish themselves. Read beside it, Mark 10:45 is the announcement of a saving work, not just a model of admirable behavior.

Psalm 49:7–9

The psalm reckons honestly with a ransom for a human life that is beyond anyone’s means to pay. Set beside it, Jesus’ saying in Mark 10:45 answers the disciples’ question from a few verses earlier — “Who then can be saved?” — with the one gift that could meet that price: the Son of Man’s own life.

Daniel 7:13–14

Daniel’s vision gives the title Son of Man its horizon of authority and rule, the same horizon Jesus draws on when he speaks of the Son of Man coming on the clouds of heaven (Mark 14:62). That is what makes Mark 10:45 so startling. The figure with the greatest claim to be served says he came to serve.

Jeremiah 31:7–9

When the prophet pictures God bringing his scattered people home, he names “the blind and the lame” among the returning company, together with expectant mothers: the weakest of them, coming back not as conquerors but as people God has saved, on a level path. Bartimaeus on the road to Jerusalem, seeing and following, fits that promise. God’s gathered people were always meant to include those others leave at the roadside.

Mark 14:24, 36

At the supper Jesus speaks of his blood “poured out for many”; in Gethsemane he prays, “Take this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will.” The “many” of the ransom saying and the cup James and John accepted so quickly both return here, showing the cost of what Jesus announced on the road and the willingness with which he went on to give it.

The Shape of a Kingdom People

“Not so with you” is one of the most practical sentences Jesus ever spoke. It was said to a small group of friends who had just discovered they were rivals, and it was said about power: who decides, who gets waited on, who sits closest. Jesus did not tell them to stop wanting to be great. He told them what greatness would look like among them — more influence meaning more responsibility for everyone else’s good, the person at the front making sure no one is left behind. That shape shows up in ordinary places long before it shows up anywhere public: in whose schedule bends, whose voice is heard in a meeting, how those with authority in a church or a household treat the people with the least of it.

The same sentence stands guard against its own misuse. It forbids lording it over others, so it can never become a tool for doing so. A leader who demands silent submission in the name of servanthood has turned Jesus’ words upside down. Christian leadership cannot be baptized domination. And the one who spoke them showed, in the same scene, that serving is not the same as granting every request: he told James and John plainly what was not his to give. A people shaped by this passage can serve with honest limits, name harm when they see it, and refuse to ask anyone to remain in danger as proof of faithfulness. Jesus went to his cross deliberately and for others; his road does not make injury holy or ask the vulnerable to absorb abuse.

“Not so with you” also faces outward. Jesus spoke it about the rulers of the nations, and his people live among such rulers still. A church shaped by this passage will not admire or imitate those who lord it over others, will not lend them its blessing, and will not stay quiet while they push the weak down. Its own life together, where the powerful serve and the least are heard, becomes a visible alternative in a world that assumes power exists to be served. Its neighbors should be able to look at it and see a different kind of kingdom.

What keeps this service from turning anxious or proud is the word “for.” We serve because the Son of Man first served us and gave his life as a ransom for many. His people never add to that ransom, and they never stand on their service as if it had earned their place. That frees them to serve without keeping accounts, since the one account that mattered has already been settled by someone else.

And then there is the road out of Jericho. Communities, like crowds, develop a sense of which voices are welcome, and people whose needs are loud, persistent, or badly timed are often the first to be told to wait. Jesus stopped. A church that follows him learns to stop too: to notice who is being hushed, whether in a congregation, a town meeting, or a public debate about people who are not in the room, to pass along Jesus’ call to them rather than our verdict about them, and to ask what they want before deciding what they need. Bartimaeus was nobody’s project. He was a man with a name, a voice, and a request, who ended up walking the same road as everyone else. Blind and disabled brothers and sisters belong on that road fully now. We can pray boldly with one another for healing, as Bartimaeus asked, while refusing to treat an unhealed body as a verdict on anyone’s faith. The road Jesus leads his people on runs through a cross toward resurrection, and there is room on it for all of them.

Gospel-Shaped Response

From this passage we can learn to move in a grace-first order. We begin with who God is and what he has done, because our identity is shaped by his character and work, not by our performance. When we forget those truths, unbelief grows in distorted emotions and patterns of life. Faithful response and mission then flow out of trust in the gospel rather than pressure to earn God’s favor.

Who is God?

1 · Character of God

  • God gives before he requires anything. The Son of Man came to serve and to give his life; he did not wait to be served first.
  • God does not hand out places of honor as favors. Even when two of his closest friends asked, Jesus would not grant them the seats they wanted.
  • God hears the voice others want quiet. On his way to Jerusalem, Jesus stopped for a man the crowd was hushing.

What has God done?

2 · Work of God

  • In Jesus, he walked knowingly toward being handed over, mocked, flogged, and killed, and named the resurrection beyond it.
  • He gave his life as a ransom for many — a price no one else could pay, and one he paid alone.
  • He stopped on the road out of Jericho, had Bartimaeus called, asked him what he wanted, and gave him his sight.

Who are we in light of what God has done?

3 · New Identity

  • Our standing rests on a ransom we did not pay and cannot add to.
  • Because our place near Jesus is not something we have to win, we are free to let greatness run toward other people’s good.
  • We are heard. No one in his company is too loud, too needy, or too inconvenient for him to stop for.

What wrong behavior, harmful emotions, or false attitudes result when we forget these truths?

4 · Unbelief

  • We come to Jesus with blank-check requests, hoping he will serve our plans for distinction.
  • We become indignant when others seem to get ahead, because we are still keeping score by rank.
  • We lord it over people in small ways — at home, at work, in church — using position to be served.
  • We join the many who tell someone to be quiet when their need is loud, awkward, or inconvenient.
  • We turn service into a way of earning standing, and it sours into resentment or pride.
  • We read an unhealed body, our own or someone else’s, as a verdict on faith.

How would we live if we believed these truths?

5 · Faithful Response

  • We would bring Jesus our real requests and let him answer them truly, even when the answer is “You don’t know what you are asking.”
  • We would use whatever influence we have to carry responsibility for others rather than to secure comfort for ourselves.
  • We would serve freely and with honest limits, remembering that the one who came to serve also said no.
  • We would keep crying out for mercy when others tell us to hush.
  • We would follow Jesus on the road he walked, knowing it passes through the cross to resurrection.

If we lived these truths by faith, how might they overflow into the real places and relationships God has given us?

6 · Mission

  • In families, workplaces, and churches, we would practice “Not so with you,” measuring authority by the people it serves.
  • We would notice who is being shushed, in a meeting, a congregation, a neighborhood, or a nation, and stop, listen, and pass along Jesus’ call.
  • In public life, we would refuse to admire or bless those who lord it over the weak, and we would stand with the people they push down.
  • We would ask neighbors what they want before deciding what they need, especially those whose disabilities lead others to speak for them.
  • We would let our shared life be a visible alternative to domination, pointing past ourselves to the Son of Man who gave his life for many.

Hearing the Father’s Voice

The Bible is God’s love letter to us. When we read it, we do not want accusation, fear, shame, or distance to distort what God has spoken. In this passage, the Father shows us that his Son came not to be served but to serve and to give his life as a ransom for many, and that on the way to that gift he stopped for a man others wanted silenced. Here is what this passage sounds like when the Father says it to you.

My child, you do not have to win a place near my Son. The place you most need was never something to be bargained for or seized as a prize; he secured it himself when he gave his life as a ransom, and he gave it freely. So you can set down the long work of making yourself great. Let him serve you first, and then serve others without fear of being made small. And if you live under people who lord it over you, hear me: that is not my way, and it is not the last word over your life. My Son came to serve you, not to use you.

My child, when voices around you, or inside you, tell you to be quiet, remember that my Son stood still on the way to Jerusalem for one man who would not stop asking for mercy. Your cry does not interrupt his great work; it was never outside it. Bring him what you truly want, without making your need look smaller than it is, and never mistake being silenced or used for being faithful. In my Son, you are not forgotten on the roadside. You are called, healed, and invited to follow on the way.
