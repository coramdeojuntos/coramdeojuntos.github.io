Authority in Open Hands

Read Mark 6:1–29 in your Bible first, slowly. Then let this devotional walk beside you, a little at a time, over several days.

Big Picture: What’s Happening Here?

Almost everyone in this passage is holding something. The people of Jesus’ hometown hold a settled knowledge of him: his trade, his mother’s name, his brothers’ names, the sisters who live among them. King Herod holds a prison, an executioner, a hall full of officials and commanders, and half a kingdom to promise. The Twelve hold a staff. Mark lets us notice how short that last list is, and then shows us that the Twelve are the ones through whom demons are driven out and the sick are healed.

The authority at work in these verses is real, and it is never anyone’s possession. It belongs to Jesus. He gives it to the Twelve for their errand, and they carry it the way a messenger carries a message: with no means of enforcing it and no power to make anyone receive it. His hometown can hear him and still take offense. A village can refuse to welcome the ones he sends. A king can listen to God’s prophet with real interest and still order his death. Jesus never forces the matter, and he never stops. He keeps teaching, he heals even where he is dishonored, and he sends.

Mark also keeps showing us people who hear. The hometown hears and is amazed. The Twelve are warned that some places will not listen. Herod hears about Jesus, and we learn that he used to like listening to John. More than once in this chapter, hearing — even amazed, even admiring hearing — stops short of receiving, and the one response the Twelve went out preaching was repentance. Mark then sets John’s death inside the Twelve’s mission, so that fruitful work and costly witness stand side by side, and neither is allowed to become the measure of faithfulness. The chapter is crowded with John, Herod, and a banquet, but its center is the one whose name set it all in motion.

Passage Commentary

Amazed, and Still Offended

Mark 6:1–6a

Jesus comes home with company. Mark mentions that his disciples are with him, and it matters: they are about to watch the people who have known Jesus longest decide what to do with him, not long before they are sent to towns that will have to decide the same thing. It is the Sabbath, the place is the synagogue, and Jesus does what he has done in synagogues since the first chapter: he teaches, and “many who heard him were amazed.” No one in the room is bored, and no one denies the wisdom or the works. Their first questions are good ones — “Where did this man get these things?” “What’s this wisdom that has been given him?” “What are these remarkable miracles he is performing?” Someone who asked them honestly and followed them through would be on the way to the truth. The questions are not the problem.

The problem is where the questions come to rest. “Isn’t this the carpenter? Isn’t this Mary’s son and the brother of James, Joseph, Judas and Simon? Aren’t his sisters here with us?” None of this is false. He had worked with his hands; his family was part of their town. Their knowledge of Jesus was accurate as far as it went, and it went only as far as they had already seen. What Mark tells us is the outcome: “And they took offense at him.” Amazement had not become welcome. People can be astonished by Jesus and still decide that what they already know about him is the last word. Notice what they think they know: his trade, his family, the town he came from. They dismiss him because of where he comes from and what kind of work his hands have done. Many people know what that feels like, to be judged by an accent, a job, a family name, a country of origin, before anyone has listened to what they have to say. The Son of God was dismissed that way too.

Jesus answers with a saying: “A prophet is not without honor except in his own town, among his relatives and in his own home.” He is interpreting what has just happened to him, and he names a pattern: honor withheld nearest home. The saying explains his rejection. It does not hand out credentials. Being dismissed by the people closest to us does not make any of us a prophet, and the reader of Mark already knows that Jesus is more than one (1:1, 11).

Then Mark writes a sentence he makes no attempt to smooth: “He could not do any miracles there, except lay his hands on a few sick people and heal them.” Both halves belong to it. Mark says “could not,” and it would be dishonest to quietly change it to would not so that it sits more comfortably. He does not explain how the town’s unbelief and Jesus’ inability are related, and we should not pretend he has. But neither does he let the inability stand alone. In a town that had just taken offense at him, Jesus put his hands on a few sick people, and they were healed. Unbelief in that place did not switch off his compassion. Mark does not say those few had more faith than their neighbors, and nothing in this scene tells anyone why a particular person is or is not healed today. It describes a town’s response to Jesus. It is not a verdict on anyone’s sickbed.

“He was amazed at their lack of faith.” The scene opened with the town amazed at Jesus, and it closes with Jesus amazed at the town. Mark simply lets him be astonished and does not stop to explain what that means for how the Son of God knows things. What he wants us to see is where the astonishment falls: on people who had heard the wisdom, seen the works, and still not received him.

Sent Out Under His Authority

Mark 6:6b–13

“Then Jesus went around teaching from village to village.” The rejection in his hometown does not bring anything to an end. He does not wait for the town to reconsider, and he does not answer offense with offense. He goes on doing what Mark has shown him doing from the start, moving through the villages with the word (1:38–39). The first thing this passage teaches about Jesus’ authority is that refusal does not stop it.

Then he widens its reach. “Calling the Twelve to him, he began to send them out two by two and gave them authority over impure spirits.” Mark told us long ago why Jesus appointed the Twelve: “that they might be with him and that he might send them out to preach” (3:14). Now the sending begins, and every verb in the sentence belongs to Jesus. He calls; he sends; he gives. The authority is real, and demons will be driven out. It is also entirely his. They did not earn it, buy it, inherit it, or grow into it. They received it, for this errand, from the one who sent them. Mark notes that they go in pairs and does not tell us why; what we can say is that no one in this sending goes alone.

The instructions that follow are strikingly sparse. “Take nothing for the journey except a staff—no bread, no bag, no money in your belts. Wear sandals but not an extra shirt.” They may walk, and lean on a staff as they walk. What they may not bring is the means to support themselves. The effect is plain: the Twelve arrive in each village needing something from the very people they have come to serve. “Whenever you enter a house, stay there until you leave that town.” They stay where they are first received and do not move from house to house. They are guests. Here is the shape of Jesus’ authority in the hands of his messengers: power over impure spirits, and no power at all over whether anyone will offer them a meal.

It would be easy to turn these instructions into a rule for all ministry, or into a romance of going without. Mark gives them as the shape of this particular sending, and dependence is not the same thing as destitution. Nor does being a guest give anyone a claim on someone else’s home. The Twelve are to receive, not demand. What carries over is the posture, not the packing list: those who go in Jesus’ name go as people who have been given something, and who must also let themselves be given to.

Notice, too, what this means for the villages. Jesus sends his messengers as strangers who arrive with nothing and need a place to stay. Whether a town welcomed them or turned them away was not a small matter; Jesus told them to shake the dust off their feet where they were refused. How a community treats the needy stranger at its door has always mattered to God. Christians who hear this passage should think carefully about the strangers who arrive at their own doors today with very little in their hands.

Jesus also prepares them for refusal. “And if any place will not welcome you or listen to you, leave that place and shake the dust off your feet as a testimony against them.” This is a serious sentence. Turning away the messengers Jesus has sent is not a neutral act, and the gesture says so. But look at what the messengers actually do. They leave. They do not force their way into a house, stay on to wear the town down, call down harm, or punish anyone. The testimony stays behind them, and enforcing it is not their work. The authority Jesus gives can free a person from an impure spirit. It cannot make a village welcome the one who brings it, and he does not ask them to try.

“They went out and preached that people should repent. They drove out many demons and anointed many sick people with oil and healed them.” Mark reports real fruit, and we lose something if we shrink it. Their message was a call to turn, the same summons John and Jesus had preached before them (1:4, 15). And the word came with deeds: people troubled by demons set free, sick people anointed and healed. Mark does not explain the oil, turn it into a technique, or promise that everyone anointed afterward will recover in the same way. He tells us what happened on this journey, with this authority, from this sender. The Twelve’s hands held very little, and a great deal of good passed through them.

A Name Reaches the King

Mark 6:14–16

“King Herod heard about this, for Jesus’ name had become well known.” Twelve travelers with no money in their belts have carried Jesus’ name far enough for a king to hear it. What reaches the king is that name, and the rest of the chapter unfolds because of it. The story that follows is about John and Herod, but it begins with Jesus. His name is traveling into a world where rulers imprison prophets, and Mark wants us to see that world clearly.

People are trying to account for the power at work in him. Some say, “John the Baptist has been raised from the dead, and that is why miraculous powers are at work in him.” Others say, “He is Elijah.” Still others, “He is a prophet, like one of the prophets of long ago.” These are respectful guesses. Nobody here thinks he is only a carpenter. But each one fits Jesus into a category already on the shelf, and Mark reports them without endorsing any of them. The same three answers will surface again much later, just before Peter says something more (8:28–29).

Herod’s own answer is different in kind: “John, whom I beheaded, has been raised from the dead!” The sentence tells us more about Herod than about Jesus. It is not faith in resurrection. It is the voice of a man whose own act has come back to meet him. Mark does not name guilt here; in a moment he will name fear. And to explain Herod’s words, he turns back to tell us what Herod did.

A Prisoner Speaks, a King Listens

Mark 6:17–20

“For Herod himself had given orders to have John arrested, and he had him bound and put in prison.” Mark states Herod’s power in its plainest terms: he can have a man seized, tied, and locked away. The cause, Mark says, was “Herodias, his brother Philip’s wife, whom he had married,” and what John had been saying to Herod about it: “It is not lawful for you to have your brother’s wife.” John was not airing a general complaint about the times. He named one concrete wrong, to the man responsible for it, and he appealed to God’s law rather than to his own preference. Mark told us near the beginning that John had been put in prison (1:14). Now we learn why.

John’s word carried authority, but not the kind Herod had. It could not unlock the prison, shield him from the king, or make Herod do anything at all. John could say what was true. What Herod did with it was Herod’s. And John was locked up for saying it. If you or someone you love has been jailed, threatened, or silenced for telling the powerful the truth, or for standing up for people the powerful were harming, John stands in that line with you, and so does the one he prepared the way for.

Herodias, Mark says, “nursed a grudge against John and wanted to kill him. But she was not able to.” Her hostility is real, and Mark does not soften it. But he is careful about who holds the power here. She could not act on her wish, “because Herod feared John and protected him.” The order that put John in prison was Herod’s, and so, in the end, will be the order that kills him.

Then Mark gives us one of the most searching portraits in his Gospel. Herod feared John. He protected him. He knew him “to be a righteous and holy man.” “When Herod heard John, he was greatly puzzled; yet he liked to listen to him.” Every one of those responses is real, and none of them is repentance. Herod is not a cartoon villain here. He is drawn to the truth, disturbed by it, and not willing to be rid of it. He also keeps its speaker in chains. Hearing can stop short of receiving, and Herod shows how far short it can stop: with respect, protection, fascination, even enjoyment, and the thing John named still left in place.

That is worth taking to heart, but not in a way that turns every honest struggler into Herod. Being puzzled by God’s word is not the problem; almost everyone who takes it seriously is puzzled by it at times. What Mark exposes is a way of hearing that keeps the truth at the level of interest — admired, protected, even enjoyed — while what it names goes untouched. The Twelve went out calling people to repent. Herod had that call within his own walls, and liked hearing it, and did not repent.

A Banquet, an Oath, and a Tomb

Mark 6:21–29

“Finally the opportune time came.” Mark has just told us what Herodias wanted, and now the opening arrives. It is Herod’s birthday, and he gives a banquet for “his high officials and military commanders and the leading men of Galilee.” Everyone whose opinion matters to him is in the room. Herodias’ daughter comes in and dances, and “she pleased Herod and his dinner guests.” Mark tells us nothing more about the dance, and the passage is better served if we say nothing more either. What he does tell us is what Herod says next: “Ask me for anything you want, and I’ll give it to you,” and then, under oath, “Whatever you ask I will give you, up to half my kingdom.” It is an extravagant promise, made in front of the most important men in his world by a king presenting himself as able to give almost anything.

Now follow the sequence, because Mark keeps each person distinct. The girl goes out and asks her mother, “What shall I ask for?” Herodias answers, “The head of John the Baptist.” Then Mark writes, “At once the girl hurried in to the king with the request: ‘I want you to give me right now the head of John the Baptist on a platter.’” The mother names the head. The daughter adds the urgency and the platter. Mark gives us their words and actions and no window into their hearts, and we should not invent one — neither a scheming type nor a helpless pawn. Each of them did what Mark says she did.

“The king was greatly distressed, but because of his oaths and his dinner guests, he did not want to refuse her.” This is the hinge of the account. Herod’s distress is real, and so is his choice. Mark does not say the guests demanded anything, and he does not say the oath left Herod no option. He says Herod did not want to refuse. The man with officials, commanders, a prison, and half a kingdom to promise did not want to say no in front of that room. His power was enormous, and it was not free. It answered to a promise made before an audience, and to the audience itself.

Set him beside the Twelve. They went out with a staff and sandals, and they were free to walk away from any town that would not have them. Herod had everything a ruler could want gathered in one hall, and he would not walk away from a single sentence he had spoken over dinner. Mark is not teaching that having little is good and having much is evil. He is showing two very different relationships to power: authority received from Jesus and carried with open hands, and power held tightly for the sake of how one looks.

“So he immediately sent an executioner with orders to bring John’s head. The man went, beheaded John in the prison, and brought back his head on a platter. He presented it to the girl, and she gave it to her mother.” Mark tells it without flourish, and so should we. The order is Herod’s. The killing is the executioner’s. The head passes to the daughter, and from her to her mother. No one in the chain is invisible, and no one’s part excuses anyone else’s. John, who had kept speaking, says nothing in the scene of his death. Nothing here is beautiful. A righteous man was killed so that a king would not have to refuse a request in front of his guests.

“On hearing of this, John’s disciples came and took his body and laid it in a tomb.” The passage ends with one more hearing, and this one turns straight into action. They come. They take him. They lay him down. Mark gives them no speeches, and we are not told what they felt, but we can see what they did. At the banquet, John’s head had been asked for, carried in on a platter, and handed on. His disciples came and treated his body with the care owed to a person. Care for a body that violence has broken is not a small thing, and Mark lets it be the last thing in the story.

Step back and notice where Mark has placed all this. The Twelve are sent out (6:7–13); John’s death is told; then Mark resumes: “The apostles gathered around Jesus and reported to him all they had done and taught” (6:30). The Twelve come back. John does not. Mark sets fruitful mission and costly witness inside the same frame and will not let either one become the measure of faithfulness. The messengers are not promised safety, and they are not told that suffering proves anything. Even the next meal in Mark’s story will look nothing like Herod’s banquet: in a remote place, Jesus has compassion on a crowd and feeds it (6:34–44).

A reader who knows how Mark’s Gospel goes on will also sense another shadow over this scene: a ruler under pressure, a righteous man put to death, a body laid in a tomb (15:15, 42–46). Later Jesus will say that Elijah has come “and they have done to him everything they wished” (9:13), and after this chapter it is hard to hear those words without thinking of John. But the likeness has limits. John was beheaded; Jesus will be crucified. John’s death is not a ransom for anyone; Jesus’ will be (10:45). John’s disciples laid him in a tomb, and Mark says no more; Jesus’ tomb will be found empty (16:6).

Mark 6:1–29 In One Movement

Mark follows one authority through this chapter and watches the different ways people meet it. It belongs to Jesus. It speaks with a wisdom his neighbors cannot deny and still will not welcome. It heals a few sick people in a town that has taken offense. It keeps teaching when it is refused. Then it goes out with twelve ordinary people who carry little more than a staff, who cannot compel a single welcome, and through whom demons are driven out, the sick are healed, and villages are called to repent. None of it is theirs, and none of it is forced on anyone. Across the same chapter, people keep hearing without receiving: a town amazed and offended, places that will not listen, a king who likes listening to a righteous man and orders his death anyway. John spoke God’s truth plainly to power and could not control what power did with it. Herod seemed to hold everything, would not let go of his promise and his standing, and a prophet’s head was the price. The Twelve come back and report what they have done and taught, and John’s disciples lay his body in a tomb. Mark lets neither the fruit nor the cost become the measure. The center of the chapter is not the king who would not say no. It is the teacher who could be refused and kept on giving himself, whose authority everyone else can only receive, carry, or reject.

Scripture Cross-References

Leviticus 18:16; 20:21

These Torah passages provide the scriptural background for John’s charge that it was “not lawful” for Herod to have his brother’s wife. John was not inventing a standard or voicing a private grievance. He pressed on a king a word God had already given to Israel, which is part of what made his speech authoritative and part of why that authority was not his own.

2 Kings 5

Naaman arrives with money and letters from his king, is told through the prophet’s messenger simply to go and wash in the Jordan, and leaves in a rage that the remedy asks nothing worthy of a man like him. His servants have to plead with him to do the simple thing. The story does not explain every hearer in Jesus’ hometown, but it shows how God’s help can offend people when it arrives in a form that does not match their expectations or honor their standing.

Ezekiel 2:3–5

God sends Ezekiel to a stubborn, rebellious people to speak in the Lord’s name, and tells him in advance that whether they listen or refuse, they will know a prophet has been among them. The prophet’s task is faithful speech, not control of the response. Jesus builds the same limit into the Twelve’s instructions (Mark 6:11), and John lived within it before Herod.

Amos 7:12–15

When Amos is told to go back to his own land, earn his bread there, and stop prophesying at the nation’s sanctuary, he answers that he was no prophet by trade; the Lord took him from his flock and sent him to prophesy to Israel. His right to speak before the powerful came entirely from being sent, which is the only authority the Twelve carry as well.

Mark 9:11–13

Coming down from the mountain, Jesus says, “Elijah has come, and they have done to him everything they wished, just as it is written about him,” and sets that suffering beside the Son of Man’s. He does not name John there, but after Mark 6 the reader can hardly miss him. The connection is real, and so is the difference: the forerunner’s death prepares the way for Jesus’ suffering without being the same death or doing the same work.

The Shape of a Kingdom People

The hometown’s mistake lies closest to those who have known Jesus longest. The communities most at risk of it are rarely the ones that have never heard of him. They are the ones that have heard of him all their lives, who know the stories and can finish his sentences, and who have quietly concluded that nothing he says will surprise them. A kingdom people is willing to be astonished again, and then to go further than astonishment. Questions belong here; Jesus’ neighbors asked good ones. What a church must not do is let familiarity settle the questions before they have been brought to him.

Whatever authority a church or ministry carries is received, not owned. The Twelve’s commission was their own and cannot simply be claimed by later leaders, but its shape still teaches us. Those who serve in Jesus’ name answer to him, and that means they remain answerable: to his word, to the people they serve, and to correction. Religious office does not make anyone immune from criticism, and being criticized does not make anyone a prophet. A people shaped by this passage will also be willing to be guests: to receive hospitality with gratitude, to honor the limits of those who host them, and never to confuse dependence with a claim on someone else’s home or money.

When people will not receive the message, this passage allows no coercion. The Twelve left; they did not pressure, shame, or punish. That frees a church from two opposite temptations: forcing a response, and reading rejection as proof that we were right. Refusal is serious, and the dust testified to that, but the testimony served the message, not the messenger’s wounded pride. The same restraint shapes how we pray for the sick. We can ask boldly, remembering that the Twelve anointed many and saw them healed, and we can refuse to lay blame on anyone who is not healed. Mark gives us no warrant for such blame.

John shows that faith can speak to public wrong, concretely and to the person responsible. He also shows what such speech requires. It named a real wrong, it rested on what God had already said, and it did not depend on winning. Christian public witness that hopes to stand where John stood must be just as careful about truth, evidence, and accountability, and must never become a way of humiliating opponents or baptizing a side. Truthful speech also refuses easy scapegoats. Mark will not let blame settle on a mother and a daughter so that the man who gave the order goes free, and neither should we. Herod himself is a warning closer to home than we might like. Few of us will ever command an executioner, but many of us know the pull of a room whose approval we cannot bear to lose. A kingdom people learns to say no in front of the guests.

And when violence has done its work, this passage asks for something quieter. John’s disciples came, took his body, and laid it in a tomb. Churches that have buried people killed by violence know how much that simple act carries. Honoring the dead and staying with the grieving are part of our witness, not a pause in it.

Gospel-Shaped Response

From this passage we can learn to move in a grace-first order. We begin with who God is and what he has done, because our identity is shaped by his character and work, not by our performance. When we forget those truths, unbelief grows in distorted emotions and patterns of life. Faithful response and mission then flow out of trust in the gospel rather than pressure to earn God’s favor.

Who is God?

1 · Character of God

  • God is the source of the authority at work in this passage. Jesus teaches with a wisdom his neighbors cannot account for, and the power the Twelve exercise over impure spirits is power he gives them.
  • God does not withdraw mercy the moment he is dishonored. In a town that took offense at Jesus, a few sick people still felt his hands and were healed, and he went on teaching in the next village.
  • God does not force a welcome to his word. He lets his word be refused, treats that refusal as serious, and sends his messengers on rather than compelling a welcome.
  • God’s truth does not bend to power. The word John spoke to a king rested on what God had already said, and a king’s banquet could silence the speaker without changing what was true.

What has God done?

2 · Work of God

  • God came in Jesus to his own hometown and taught in its synagogue, and when the people would not receive him, he kept teaching.
  • Jesus called twelve ordinary people, gave them his authority, and sent them out. Through them people heard the call to repent, demons were driven out, and many sick people were healed.
  • Through John, God confronted a ruler with a concrete wrong, and that costly faithfulness prepared the way for Jesus’ own road (9:12–13).
  • Jesus went on to redefine authority itself. He came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many (10:45), a ransom no messenger of his could ever have paid.

Who are we in light of what God has done?

3 · New Identity

  • Everything we carry in Jesus’ name was received first. It begins with what he gives, and it remains his.
  • We are sent people who are also guests: dependent on God, often on the kindness of others, and not in charge of how anyone responds.
  • We are hearers invited past amazement into receiving, people for whom Jesus is not merely interesting but Lord.
  • We belong to the one who sends us, so our standing is settled neither by the size of the welcome nor by the depth of the opposition.

What wrong behavior, harmful emotions, or false attitudes result when we forget these truths?

4 · Unbelief

  • We shrink Jesus to what familiarity says we already know about him, and stop expecting him to say anything we have not heard before.
  • We admire truth, enjoy hearing it, even defend it, while refusing to let it touch the thing it names. Herod shows where that road can end.
  • We treat entrusted authority as possession: demanding deference, resisting correction, or taking from those who host us as though we were owed.
  • We try to force a response when people refuse, or we read their refusal as proof that we were right.
  • We let a room’s approval or our own standing outweigh what we know is true, and let someone else pay the cost of protecting our reputation.
  • None of this turns honest questions, doubt still being worked through, fear of powerful people, or an illness that has not been healed into unbelief. The unbelief this passage exposes is hearing that will not receive and power that will not let go.

How would we live if we believed these truths?

5 · Faithful Response

  • We would take our questions about Jesus all the way to him, and let amazement become trust and, where it is needed, repentance.
  • We would carry whatever authority we have been given with open hands: serving under Jesus, answerable to his word and to others, never treating ministry as property.
  • We would receive hospitality gratefully and respect the limits of those who offer it.
  • We would leave people free to refuse, without contempt, and keep going.
  • We would pray for the sick with real expectation and with no blame for anyone who is not healed.
  • We would say no when conscience requires it, even when the people watching matter to us.

If we lived these truths by faith, how might they overflow into the real places and relationships God has given us?

6 · Mission

  • In neighborhoods and workplaces we would join word and deed: an honest call to turn to God, together with real care for people’s bodies and burdens.
  • In public life we would name concrete wrongs truthfully and accountably, checking our facts, refusing vengeance, and resisting the pull to make John’s courage a banner for our own side.
  • In families and communities that know us too well, we would keep bearing witness without forcing it, trusting that rejection there does not end what Jesus is doing.
  • Where violence has struck, we would stay with the grieving and honor the bodies and names of those who were harmed.
  • In all of it we would go where Jesus sends and leave the welcome, the results, and the cost in his hands.

Hearing the Father’s Voice

The Bible is God’s love letter to us. When we read it, we do not want accusation, fear, shame, or distance to distort what God has spoken. In this passage, the Father shows us that the authority to heal, to set free, and to call people to repentance belongs to his Son, and that the Son shares it with dependent messengers who are never asked to control how anyone responds. Here is what this passage sounds like when the Father says it to you.

My child, what my Son sends you to carry is his, not yours, and it never depended on your standing, your reputation, or the welcome you receive. If you have been dismissed because of where you come from, your work, your family, your accent, remember that his own town said, “Isn’t this the carpenter?” They did not see who he was, and they do not see all of who you are. You go as one who has been given something, and you may go as one who needs to be given to. When someone will not listen, you do not have to force them, and you do not have to read their refusal as a verdict on you. My Son was refused in his own town, and still he laid his hands on a few who were sick there, and then went on teaching.

My child, I do not measure you by the size of the harvest or by the depth of the wounds. Some of those my Son sent came back and told him all they had done, and one faithful servant was killed for telling a king the truth; neither your success nor your suffering is the measure of my love for you. If you have been locked away or threatened for speaking truth to those in power, I have not forgotten you in that prison. You do not need to win a room to belong to me. Speak what is true, receive what I give, and leave with me what you cannot control.
