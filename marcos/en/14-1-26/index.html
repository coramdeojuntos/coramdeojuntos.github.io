Before They Take Him, He Gives Himself

Big Picture: What’s Happening Here?

For two days in Mark’s story, everyone around Jesus seems to be deciding what to do with him. The chief priests and the teachers of the law decide he must die — quietly, and not during the festival. At a dinner in Bethany, some of the guests decide what the perfume poured over his head was worth, and what the woman who poured it deserves to hear. One of the Twelve goes to the chief priests, comes away with a promise of money, and starts watching for his moment. Read quickly, the passage feels like a net closing around him.

Read it slowly and you notice who keeps speaking. Jesus receives the woman’s gift and tells her critics to leave her alone. He says what her act means and promises that the whole world will hear of it. He sends two disciples into the city with directions that prove exactly right. He tells the Twelve that one of them will betray him, and he does not pretend the betrayer is merely playing a part. Then, with everything against him already in motion, he takes bread, gives thanks, breaks it, and hands it to them: “Take it; this is my body.” Before anyone lays a hand on Jesus, Jesus places himself in their hands.

That is where this passage is going, and it needs to be held with care. What is done to Jesus is real, and so is the guilt of those who do it. He is not above the pain of it; a few verses on, he will be on the ground in Gethsemane, “overwhelmed with sorrow to the point of death.” But Mark will not let his death be told only as something that happened to him. The one being handed over is the one who gives himself — at a table already shadowed by “one of you,” to friends who will scatter before morning, with his eyes on a day when he will drink the fruit of the vine new in the kingdom of God. Many of us know what it is to be on the receiving end of other people’s plans. Most of us, if we are honest, also know what it is to decide quietly what Jesus is worth to us. This passage has good news for both.

Passage Commentary

Death Planned Around Him

Mark 14:1–2

Mark opens with a calendar and a conspiracy. Passover and the Festival of Unleavened Bread are two days away — the feast in which Israel remembered the night God brought them out of slavery — and “the chief priests and the teachers of the law were scheming to arrest Jesus secretly and kill him.” Mark is specific about who. These are particular leaders with a history in his story. They began looking for a way to kill Jesus after he drove the traders out of the temple courts (11:18), and they backed away from arresting him once already because they were afraid of the crowd (12:12). Nothing here indicts a people. It exposes a use of power.

Their one recorded sentence shows what that power is thinking about: “But not during the festival,” they said, “or the people may riot.” No one asks whether Jesus has done anything wrong. The questions are timing, secrecy, and public reaction. Power that has already settled on its outcome tends to talk this way. It prefers the dark, counts the risk, and waits for a quieter moment. Decisions about vulnerable people are still often made this way: quietly, behind closed doors, by people who never ask whether the ones they are deciding about have done anything wrong. If decisions about your life have been made like that, know that the Lord of this passage was the subject of one too.

Mark lets us see what the plotters could not. And notice what festival it was. Passover was the feast of a people set free from slavery, the night God heard the cry of the oppressed in Egypt and brought them out. Everything that follows — the supper, the arrest, the trial — takes place during the very festival they meant to avoid. He does not stop to explain how their timetable came apart; he sets the calendar beside their plans and moves on. But notice where the story is heading. At the center of that festival there will be a table, and at that table it will be Jesus, not the men scheming against him, who says what his death means.

A Costly Gift Received and Defended

Mark 14:3–9

The scene shifts to Bethany, to the home of Simon the Leper, with Jesus reclining at the table. A woman comes in. Mark never gives her a name or a line of dialogue, and he does not tell us her history or what was in her mind. He tells us what she brought and what she did with it: an alabaster jar of very expensive perfume, pure nard. She broke the jar and poured the perfume on his head. By the time anyone thinks to object, it is done.

Then the objection comes. “Some of those present” begin saying indignantly to one another, “Why this waste of perfume? It could have been sold for more than a year’s wages and the money given to the poor.” The arithmetic is not foolish. A year’s wages is a great deal, and the poor are real; Jesus is about to say so himself. But look at what the complaint became: “they rebuked her harshly.” A good cause was turned into a stick, and the one it came down on was the woman in the room.

Then Jesus steps in. “Leave her alone,” he says. “Why are you bothering her?” It is worth noticing that he speaks up at all. In a few days this same man will stand before the high priest and before Pilate and say almost nothing to defend himself (14:61; 15:5). Here, for her, he speaks. Then he gives the room a verdict it did not expect: “She has done a beautiful thing to me.” To me. Before Jesus gives himself away in this chapter, he lets himself be given to. He receives her love as his own, and he will not let it be scolded.

His next words have been misused so often that they deserve a slow reading. “The poor you will always have with you, and you can help them any time you want. But you will not always have me.” Jesus is not waving the poor away. He assumes his followers will go on helping them, and he leaves that door standing open — any day, every day. What he refuses is the idea that care for the poor has to be paid for by shaming this woman. The contrast he draws is about time. The poor will still be there tomorrow. He will not; he is about to die. A church that quotes this verse to excuse neglect has kept the first half of Jesus’ sentence and dropped the second.

“She did what she could.” Jesus measures her gift by what was in her hands, not by the objectors’ arithmetic, and not by some standard everyone else must now meet. The sentence defends her; it does not set a price for the rest of us. Then Jesus names what her gift was for: “She poured perfume on my body beforehand to prepare for my burial.” This is the first time in the passage that Jesus himself speaks of his death, and he speaks of it as something to be honored — a body worth preparing. Did she know? Mark does not say. Perhaps she sensed what was coming; perhaps she did not. Either way, the meaning of the act comes from Jesus. Whatever she understood when she came through the door, he knows what her gift is for.

Mark will make that word beforehand ache. After the crucifixion, three women buy spices “so that they might go to anoint Jesus’ body” (16:1), and when they reach the tomb there is no body to anoint. In Mark’s telling, the only anointing Jesus’ body ever receives is this one, given in Bethany while he was still at the table. She came early. They came, in the best possible sense, too late.

Then a promise: “Truly I tell you, wherever the gospel is preached throughout the world, what she has done will also be told, in memory of her.” Notice what Jesus takes for granted. There will be a gospel; it will go to the whole world; and it will be the story of a man who died and was buried — the very burial she has just prepared him for. Carried along inside that story will be what she did. Jesus writes a woman who was publicly scolded into the public telling of the good news. The order of the scene matters. She gives to Jesus. Jesus receives her gift and honors it. In a few days he will give himself, and her act will be told wherever that story goes. Her perfume saves no one, and his death is not a repayment. But the one who is about to give everything is not too proud to be given to, and he does not forget who loved him.

Handed Over from Within the Twelve

Mark 14:10–11

“Then Judas Iscariot, one of the Twelve, went to the chief priests to betray Jesus to them.” Mark sets this directly after Bethany, and the two scenes sit side by side: a woman whose name we never learn pours out something costly on Jesus; a man whose name the whole world knows goes to arrange his handing over. Mark lets us see the contrast.

He tells us very little, and every piece counts. Judas is “one of the Twelve” — one of those Jesus called up a mountainside and appointed “that they might be with him” (3:14). He goes to the chief priests; they did not come looking for him. The men who, two verses earlier, were wondering how to seize Jesus without a riot “were delighted to hear this and promised to give him money.” And Judas “watched for an opportunity to hand him over.” From here to the cross, Jesus will be passed from one set of hands to another: to the chief priests, to Pilate (15:1), to the soldiers to be crucified (15:15). The handing over begins here, from inside the circle.

What Mark does not tell us is why. A motive would make Judas easier to file away as a kind of person we are not, and Mark does not give us one. Money is in the scene, but he never says it was the reason. We are left looking at the act itself: someone who had eaten with Jesus, walked with him, and been sent out by him to preach (3:14; 6:7), arranging to hand him to people who wanted him dead. The silence is uncomfortable, and it should be. It keeps Judas from becoming a type we can pin on someone else, and it keeps the weight where Mark puts it — on what was done.

Jesus Prepares the Place

Mark 14:12–16

Mark dates the next scene by a sacrifice: “On the first day of the Festival of Unleavened Bread, when it was customary to sacrifice the Passover lamb.” The disciples ask the natural question — “Where do you want us to go and make preparations for you to eat the Passover?” — and Jesus answers with directions so specific they read like a map. Go into the city. A man carrying a jar of water will meet you. Follow him. Speak to the owner of the house, and you will be shown “a large room upstairs, furnished and ready.” Then Mark’s quiet sentence: “The disciples left, went into the city and found things just as Jesus had told them.”

Jesus’ word held, as it held when two disciples were sent for a colt and found it just as he said (11:1–6). On a day when enemies are working in secret and a friend is watching for his moment, Jesus is also making preparations, through the people who follow him. The Teacher calls it “my guest room.” The Passover will be his table. Twice now in this chapter someone has prepared something for Jesus: a woman, his body for burial; two disciples, a room for the meal. Both point toward his death: one to his burial, the other to the table where he will give himself.

Betrayal Named at the Table

Mark 14:17–21

Evening comes, and Jesus arrives with the Twelve. They recline, they eat, and in the middle of the meal he says it: “Truly I tell you, one of you will betray me—one who is eating with me.” A moment later, answering their questions, he draws it closer still: “It is one of the Twelve,” he says, “one who dips bread into the bowl with me.” One of you. Eating with me. One of the Twelve. Sharing my bowl. Each phrase draws the circle tighter. The betrayal in this passage does not come from beyond the walls. It comes from someone near enough to share a dish.

“They were saddened, and one by one they said to him, ‘Surely you don’t mean me?’” Protest, fear, honest uncertainty may all be in that question. But look at what Mark records. Each one asks about himself. No one points across the table. When Jesus names betrayal, that is the fitting first question: the one about me. And it can be asked here, in the presence of someone who already knows the answer and has not stopped eating with them.

Notice, too, what Jesus does not do. He does not name Judas to the others, and he does not pretend not to know. He says enough to be unmistakable to one man and not enough to expose him to the room. Then he speaks a sentence that holds together two things we usually pull apart: “The Son of Man will go just as it is written about him. But woe to that man who betrays the Son of Man! It would be better for him if he had not been born.”

The first half says that Jesus’ death is no accident. The Son of Man is not only taken; he goes, along a road Scripture has already marked, as he has been telling his disciples since he first said the Son of Man “must suffer many things” (8:31). The second half says that God’s purpose does not make betrayal innocent. Few sentences in Mark are more severe than “better for him if he had not been born.” Jesus does not soften it, and we should not. Nor should we go past it; Mark leaves the last word about Judas where Jesus leaves it. What we are given is both truths at once — a purpose that betrayal cannot derail, and a guilt that purpose does not erase. Jesus does not treat betrayal as a small thing because he saw it coming.

The One Being Handed Over Gives Himself

Mark 14:22–26

“While they were eating” — the same meal, at the same table, with one of you still in the air — “Jesus took bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke it and gave it to his disciples.” Readers of Mark have watched these hands before. In two remote places with hungry crowds, Jesus took the loaves, gave thanks, broke them, and gave them to his disciples to hand out (6:41; 8:6). The motions are the same. But this time the bread is not multiplied. It is named: “Take it; this is my body.”

Set that sentence beside everything else in the passage. The chief priests want to seize him secretly. Judas is watching for the opportunity to hand him over. Before the night is out, a crowd with swords and clubs will take hold of him (14:43–46). But before any other hand closes on him, he has already opened his own. Others are arranging to take him; he says, “Take it.” The one being handed over is the one who gives himself. The betrayal will still happen, and it will still be betrayal. It will not be the only thing that is true about his death, and it will not be the deepest.

Then the cup. “Then he took a cup, and when he had given thanks, he gave it to them, and they all drank from it.” Mark tells us that they all drank before he tells us what the cup means. All of them — at a table where Jesus has just said “one of you,” and only a little before he tells them, “You will all fall away” (14:27). Before morning, “everyone deserted him and fled” (14:50). The cup does not wait for their faithfulness. It comes first, into the hands of people who are about to fail, and that is the only order in which anyone has ever received it.

“This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many.” These words reach a long way back. Mark has dated the day by the customary sacrifice of the Passover lambs, at the feast that remembered blood on the doorposts and a people brought out of slavery. “Blood of the covenant” then recalls Sinai, where the covenant between God and Israel was sealed with sacrificial blood on the altar and on the people. Now, at the Passover table, the meal of a people God brought out of slavery, Jesus lifts a cup and says the covenant blood is his own. The freedom God gave at the first Passover is being given again, deeper and wider, through his death. This table is a freedom meal. Everyone who has lived under someone else’s power, worked without fair pay, been treated as property, or waited for a God who hears the cry of the oppressed, is invited to it. And “poured out for many” gathers up what he told them on the road to Jerusalem — that he came “to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many” (10:45). What he said then, he now puts into their hands. At a table of twelve he says many. The gift is larger than the room.

Christians have understood Christ’s presence at his table in different ways, and still do, and this short scene does not settle every question they have asked. What Mark makes unmistakable is enough to live on. The gift is bodily: bread broken, a cup passed. It is received: “Take it” is a word obeyed with hands and mouths, not only with thoughts. It binds: this is covenant blood, the kind that makes a people belong to God. And it comes from Jesus himself, given by the one who is about to be handed over.

Then a third “Truly I tell you.” The first, at Bethany, promised that a woman’s gift would be told throughout the world. The second, at this table, announced betrayal. Now the third: “I will not drink again from the fruit of the vine until that day when I drink it new in the kingdom of God.” See where the betrayal saying stands — between a promise about the gospel’s future and a promise about God’s kingdom. It is real and it is terrible, but it is surrounded. Jesus looks through the night ahead and past his death to a day when he will drink again, new, in the kingdom of God. What he has just given is not the end of him. He carries it forward into a promise.

“When they had sung a hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives.” Mark does not tell us what they sang, only that they sang, Jesus among them, on the night of his betrayal. Then they walk out, and it is worth following a few steps past the edge of this passage, because Mark does not want us to mistake what we have just seen for calm mastery. At Gethsemane Jesus will be “deeply distressed and troubled.” He will fall to the ground and pray that if possible the hour might pass from him: “Abba, Father … Take this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will” (14:33–36). The one who handed his friends a cup will be on his face asking his Father about the cup before him. That is no contradiction; it is the price of the gift. He gave himself with his eyes open, and he went on giving through the anguish.

Mark 14:1–26 In One Movement

Two days before Passover, men who fear a crowd decide to kill Jesus quietly. In a house in Bethany a woman breaks a jar and pours its perfume over his head and is scolded for it, and Jesus turns the scolding into a promise that the world will hear what she did. One of the Twelve goes to the chief priests and comes away with a promise of money and a reason to watch. On the day the lambs are sacrificed, two disciples find a room exactly where Jesus said it would be. At the table he tells them one of them will betray him, and each of them asks whether it is him. And then — with the betrayal arranged and the desertion only hours off — Jesus does the one thing no one else in the story can do. He gives himself. Bread broken: “this is my body.” A cup they all drink: “my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many.” He will be handed over; he has already given. He looks past his death to the day he will drink it new in the kingdom of God, sings a hymn, and walks out toward Gethsemane, where the cost of what he has just given will press him to the ground.

Scripture Cross-References

Exodus 12:13

“The blood will be a sign for you on the houses where you are, and when I see the blood, I will pass over you.” Passover remembered the night the LORD brought Israel out of slavery. Mark dates Jesus’ meal by the day “it was customary to sacrifice the Passover lamb” (14:12), and at that meal the blood Jesus speaks of is his own.

Exodus 24:8

Moses “took the blood, sprinkled it on the people and said, ‘This is the blood of the covenant that the LORD has made with you.’” When Jesus calls the cup “my blood of the covenant,” he recalls Sinai. And at a table of disciples whose promises will soon fail, the covenant rests on his blood rather than on the strength of their pledges.

Deuteronomy 15:11

“There will always be poor people in the land. Therefore I command you to be openhanded toward your fellow Israelites who are poor and needy in your land.” Jesus’ words about the poor echo this command. Read beside it, “you can help them any time you want” is an expectation that his followers will keep giving.

Isaiah 53:12

“He poured out his life unto death… For he bore the sin of many.” Mark does not quote Isaiah at the table, but blood “poured out for many” has the same shape: one life given for the sake of many.

Mark 16:1–6

Three women buy spices “so that they might go to anoint Jesus’ body,” and find instead a young man saying, “He has risen! He is not here.” The only anointing Mark ever records for Jesus’ body is the one given at Bethany “beforehand.”

The Shape of a Kingdom People

Start at Bethany, because the first thing this passage asks of a community is whether it can hold two kinds of love without setting them against each other. The woman’s costly devotion to Jesus and the ongoing care of the poor both receive his blessing in the same breath. The trouble in that room began when concern for the poor was aimed at a person. A church shaped by this scene keeps its hands open to the poor in the ordinary weeks — “any time you want” means the help is always in season — and pays attention to what keeps people poor as well as to who is hungry tonight. It also refuses to make lavishness a test of faith. “She did what she could” honors one woman’s gift. It does not hand anyone else a quota, and it gives no one license to audit another person’s devotion.

Then watch what Jesus does with his voice. He spends it defending someone others were scolding, and he makes sure the world will remember what she did. Kingdom people learn to do the same: to speak up for those being shamed in public, especially when the people doing the shaming have good arguments, and to keep a long memory for the faithfulness of people who never make the record under their own names. When the church tells the gospel to the world, it tells the story of Jesus’ death and burial, and it lets the love of overlooked people travel with that story as part of the news about him — never as an advertisement for anyone’s budget.

Finally, the table. Jesus ate with twelve men, told them one of them would betray him, and then gave them the bread and the cup. A community formed at that table is a people brought out of slavery, and it cannot be at peace with anyone else’s. It will notice the people in its own town who are trafficked, exploited, paid less than they were promised, or kept quiet by fear, and it will treat their freedom as part of the meaning of the meal. A community formed at that table can also afford to be honest about betrayal and failure inside its own walls. It does not pretend that closeness makes people safe from one another, and it does not hurry past real harm or ask those who have been betrayed to treat it as small; Jesus named betrayal plainly and spoke woe over it. Nor does such a community spend its life scanning faces for the traitor. When it hears this passage, its first question is the disciples’ question about themselves. And because the cup came before anyone had proven faithful, these people come to the table to receive rather than to perform — humble about their own reliability, patient with one another’s weakness, and hopeful, because the one who gave them the cup has promised to drink it new in the kingdom of God.

Gospel-Shaped Response

From this passage we can learn to move in a grace-first order. We begin with who God is and what he has done, because our identity is shaped by his character and work, not by our performance. When we forget those truths, unbelief grows in distorted emotions and patterns of life. Faithful response and mission then flow out of trust in the gospel rather than pressure to earn God’s favor.

Who is God?

1 · Character of God

  • God’s giving does not wait for our faithfulness. In Jesus he places bread and a cup in the hands of people who will desert him within hours.
  • God’s purpose is not derailed by betrayal, and it never makes betrayal innocent. “As it is written” and “woe” come from the same mouth in the same breath.
  • God binds himself to a people by covenant, and at this table the covenant blood is his Son’s own.
  • God notices what others dismiss. He defends a woman while she is being scolded and makes her act part of the gospel the world will hear.

What has God done?

2 · Work of God

  • In Jesus he gave himself — “my body,” “my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many” — before anyone laid a hand on him.
  • He received costly love and would not let the one who gave it be shamed.
  • He named betrayal truthfully, and then gave them the cup, and they all drank.
  • He walked toward Gethsemane and the cross in anguish and obedience, and carried his gift forward into the promise of God’s kingdom.

Who are we in light of what God has done?

3 · New Identity

  • We are receivers first. Our life with Jesus begins with his hands, not ours: “Take it.”
  • We are a covenant people, joined to God by Jesus’ blood rather than by the strength of our pledges.
  • We are people whose love for Jesus, small or costly, he receives and does not forget.
  • We are people with a future, living toward the day he will drink it new in the kingdom of God.

What wrong behavior, harmful emotions, or false attitudes result when we forget these truths?

4 · Unbelief

  • We treat Jesus as something to be managed or priced — welcome in some seasons, too expensive in others, worth exactly what he seems to deliver.
  • We keep our love for him measured and safe, more afraid of what the people around us will say than eager for what he will call beautiful.
  • We turn good causes into weapons, using concern for the poor to shame someone’s devotion, or using devotion to excuse our own neglect of the poor.
  • We come to believe our reliability is the price of the table, so that failure drives us away from the grace Jesus gives there — or we scan the room for the traitor instead of asking the question about ourselves.
  • We let betrayal have the last word, either by assuming the worst thing done to us defines our story, or by excusing what was done because God can bring good out of it.

How would we live if we believed these truths?

5 · Faithful Response

  • We would come to Jesus’ table to receive, bringing our failures instead of hiding them, because the cup came first to people who were about to fall away.
  • We would give Jesus what is in our hands, gladly and without comparing, and let “she did what she could” free us from both showing off and shrinking back.
  • We would keep helping the poor as an ordinary, habitual part of loving Jesus, never as a rival to worship.
  • We would hear “one of you” as a summons to honest self-examination rather than a cue to accuse.
  • We would sing on hard nights, looking toward the day he promised.

If we lived these truths by faith, how might they overflow into the real places and relationships God has given us?

6 · Mission

  • We would tell the gospel as the story of a man who gave himself for many, and let the forgotten faithfulness of ordinary people travel with it.
  • We would speak up for people being shamed in public — at work, at home, in church — especially when their critics sound reasonable.
  • We would keep open hands toward the poor near us and take seriously the conditions that keep them poor, since “any time you want” includes today.
  • We would build tables where people who have failed can come and receive, and refuse to use Judas as a label for the people we oppose.

Hearing the Father’s Voice

The Bible is God’s love letter to us. When we read it, we do not want accusation, fear, shame, or distance to distort what God has spoken. In this passage, the Father shows us that while others were plotting, pricing, and arranging to hand over his Son, the Son was giving himself, his body and his blood of the covenant, poured out for many, to people who could not yet hold on to him. Here is what this passage sounds like when the Father says it to you.

My child, in the days when so many were deciding what to do with my Son, he was giving himself. Hours before he was seized, he broke bread and said, “Take it.” He passed a cup to friends who would scatter before morning, and they all drank. He did not wait for them to be steady, and he does not wait for you to be steady before he gives. If others have been deciding your life in rooms you cannot enter, hear me: I set this table at the feast of freedom, and I set a place for you. Come to him with empty hands. What he gives is his own, and it does not depend on how well you have held on.

Do not think it was easy for him. He sang with his friends and walked out into the night, and there he fell to the ground and asked me whether the cup could pass, and then he chose my will. I saw what it cost him. I see what betrayal and failure have cost you, and I do not call it small. But betrayal did not have the last word over what his death means, and it does not have the last word over you. Look past this night to the day he promised, when he will drink it new in my kingdom.
