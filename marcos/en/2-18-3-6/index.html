The Lord of What We Keep

Big Picture: What’s Happening Here?

Every question in this passage comes from people who take God seriously. Why don’t your disciples fast? Why are they doing what is unlawful on the Sabbath? Even the silent watching in the synagogue is organized around a holy day. No one here thinks devotion is foolish, and Jesus never suggests that it is. He does not call fasting empty, set Scripture aside, or announce that the Sabbath has had its day. What he does, each time, is move himself into the middle of the question. The guests cannot fast while he is with them. The Sabbath was made for humanity, so the Son of Man is its Lord. And when the question comes to a man with a shriveled hand, Jesus answers with a question of his own and then a word that makes the hand whole.

So the issue underneath these scenes is not whether holy practices are good. Mark assumes they are. The issue is whom they belong to and what they are for, and Jesus answers both at once. They belong to him: the bridegroom whose presence makes a fast out of place, the Son of Man whose lordship reaches even the day God set apart. And they are for life. In his hands the Sabbath becomes a reason to defend his companions and to give a man back the use of his hand. His lordship does not make God’s gifts heavier. It gives them back their shape.

The pressure in this passage builds slowly, and it matters where it finally lands. At the beginning, Mark reports only a question about fasting; the accusation and the plot come later. But by the last scene some are watching Jesus closely in order to accuse him, his question is met with silence, and the Pharisees go out to plan his death. Nothing in that sequence required anyone to stop fasting or to stop honoring the Sabbath. That is what makes it searching for anyone who loves God’s gifts. Disciplined devotion is not the danger here; Jesus expects his people to fast. The danger is that holy things can be guarded carefully by a heart that is resisting the Lord they belong to. Jesus has already said the bridegroom will be taken away. By the end of the passage, a plan to kill him has begun.

Passage Commentary

While the Bridegroom Is with Them

Mark 2:18–20

Mark begins with a plain fact: “Now John’s disciples and the Pharisees were fasting.” These are two different circles of devout people, and one of them had gathered around the prophet who baptized Jesus and announced that someone more powerful was coming after him. The question itself is fair. In Israel’s life, fasting was a serious way of bringing the whole self before God, in sorrow over sin or in desperate need, a way of saying with the body that we depend on him. When earnest people are fasting and the followers of a new teacher are not, it is reasonable to ask why.

Jesus answers with a picture anyone in Galilee could see: a wedding. “How can the guests of the bridegroom fast while he is with them?” Nobody fasts at a wedding. That is not because fasting is bad but because it would misread the day. What makes the day is a person: “so long as they have him with them.” Jesus does not argue about whether fasting has value. He changes what fasting is measured by. The question is no longer simply what devout people are doing this week. It is where the bridegroom is. Israel’s prophets had pictured the LORD himself as a husband to his people, and here Jesus, in his own story, takes the bridegroom’s place. He does not explain the echo. He lets it sound.

Then, before anyone has threatened him, Jesus says something no one else has raised: “But the time will come when the bridegroom will be taken from them, and on that day they will fast.” This is the first shadow to fall across Mark’s story, and Jesus is the one who names it. Notice that he does not do away with fasting. He promises it. His followers will fast. Their life with him will know the feast and, later, the fast, and both will be ways of answering him: joy because he is here, longing and grief because he has been taken. And Israel’s prophets had already said what kind of fast God wants: “to loose the chains of injustice… to set the oppressed free… to share your food with the hungry and to provide the poor wanderer with shelter” (Isaiah 58:6–7). The fasting Jesus’ followers will do is meant to look like that. The disciples’ not fasting was never a claim that devotion had gone out of date. It fitted his presence, just as their fasting will fit his absence.

Two Things That Will Not Hold

Mark 2:21–22

Jesus follows with two more pictures from ordinary household life, and each makes its own point. A patch of unshrunk cloth sewn onto an old garment will shrink and pull away, “making the tear worse.” New wine poured into old wineskins will swell until the skins burst, and then “both the wine and the wineskins will be ruined.” In the first picture the garment ends up worse off. In the second the wine is lost along with the skins. Nobody in either picture despises the old coat or the old skins. The problem is a combination that cannot hold, and the damage runs both ways.

Jesus gives no key to the pictures, and it would be unwise to hand out labels he never gives. Where they sit tells us enough. He has just said that his presence changes what fasting means. Now he warns that he cannot simply be stitched onto whatever people already expected. He is not an accessory to an existing pattern of life, something devout that leaves everything else as it was. Where he arrives, life has to take a shape that fits him, or something tears.

That warning reaches us as easily as it reached anyone in Galilee. Most of us would be glad to have Jesus as a patch, covering the worn place in a life we have otherwise arranged to our satisfaction. Then comes the last line: “No, they pour new wine into new wineskins.” The answer to new wine is not no wineskins at all. Wine still needs something to hold it. Jesus is not setting fresh life against every form, discipline, and practice. He is saying that the life he brings needs forms fitted to it. The devotion he creates will have a shape, and it will be shaped around him.

Holy Bread, Hungry Men, and the Lord of the Day

Mark 2:23–28

“One Sabbath Jesus was going through the grainfields, and as his disciples walked along, they began to pick some heads of grain.” That is all Mark describes. The Pharisees object, and they put the objection to Jesus about his disciples: “Look, why are they doing what is unlawful on the Sabbath?” The charge is about the day. We should feel how much the Sabbath mattered. It was a gift at the heart of Israel’s covenant with God, a sign of that covenant, and keeping it faithfully was a way of showing loyalty to him. A Sabbath question was never a small one.

Jesus answers by sending his questioners back into their own Scriptures. “Have you never read what David did when he and his companions were hungry and in need?” Then he tells the story in a sentence: “In the days of Abiathar the high priest,” David entered the house of God and ate the consecrated bread, “which is lawful only for priests to eat. And he also gave some to his companions.” Jesus does not pretend the bread was ordinary. He names the restriction plainly. What he points to is a story Scripture itself tells, in which holy bread fed hungry men. Two things stand together in that story, and neither should be dropped. There is need: David and those with him were hungry. And there is David himself, who went in and gave to those with him. Jesus is standing with his own companions now, taking the question about what they did onto himself.

Then come two sentences that belong together. “The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath.” The day is a gift. It was made, made for human beings, the way a meal is made for the hungry or a house for the people who will live in it. God’s first seventh day was his own delight in a finished, good work, and the rest he gave his people was an invitation to share it. People were never created to be the servants of a day. The day was created to serve them.

And notice whom the day was made to serve. When Moses repeated the Sabbath command, he named the people most likely to be denied rest: “your male or female servant… nor any foreigner residing in your towns, so that your male and female servants may rest, as you do.” Then he gave the reason: “Remember that you were slaves in Egypt” (Deuteronomy 5:14–15). From the beginning, the Sabbath was God’s protection for workers who could not stop on their own, for servants, for foreigners, for people with no power to demand a day off. If you work without rest, for wages that never let you stop, in a job where the foreigner is the one who never gets the day, hear this: God made a day for you. The Lord of the Sabbath is on your side.

But Jesus does not stop there, as though human need were the final word. “So the Son of Man is Lord even of the Sabbath.” That small word so joins the gift to its Lord. Because the Sabbath was made for human good, it matters a great deal who has the right to say what that good is. Taken alone, the first sentence could be bent into do whatever you need. Taken alone, the second could sound like bare authority overruling everyone. Together they say something better: the day belongs to someone, and the one it belongs to knows what it was made for. The Son of Man, the same name Jesus used when he claimed “authority on earth to forgive sins” (2:10), now claims lordship over the day God gave as a sign of his covenant. The word even shows how large the claim is. And the first thing we see this Lord do with it is defend his companions.

Stand Up in Front of Everyone

Mark 3:1–4

“Another time Jesus went into the synagogue, and a man with a shriveled hand was there.” Mark tells us very little about him: a man, a hand, a synagogue, a Sabbath. The tension in the room comes from somewhere else. “Some of them were looking for a reason to accuse Jesus, so they watched him closely to see if he would heal him on the Sabbath.” Notice where their eyes are fixed. They are watching Jesus, not the man. For them the man has become the place where a charge might be found. It is an old and ugly habit: to use a vulnerable person as a test case, a symbol in someone else’s argument, while no one asks what he needs. Whole groups of people are still treated that way, talked about and fought over in public while they stand in the middle of the room unseen. And their watching depends on the likelihood that Jesus will heal, so in a strange way it testifies to the kind of teacher he is. Earlier in this passage people asked him questions. Here no one asks anything. They watch.

Jesus could have waited until the gathering was over, or found the man somewhere quieter. Instead he says, “Stand up in front of everyone.” He brings the man out of other people’s calculations and into the center of the room, where he has to be seen as himself and not as a test case. Jesus also makes his own action unmistakably public. Whatever happens next will happen in full view, and he chooses that openness knowing the room is full of watchers.

Then he turns to them: “Which is lawful on the Sabbath: to do good or to do evil, to save life or to kill?” He asks what is lawful. He is not brushing the law aside; he is asking what it is for. And he frames the choice so that no comfortable middle ground remains. No one would have said the Sabbath was for doing evil. But the question presses: on the day God made for human life, is it lawful to restore this man? Nothing suggests the man was dying, and Jesus is not pretending there is an emergency. He is asking which side the Sabbath is on. “But they remained silent.” What we know is that a plain question about good and life, asked in the synagogue on the holy day, received no answer.

A Hand Restored, a Plot Begun

Mark 3:5–6

“He looked around at them in anger and, deeply distressed at their stubborn hearts…” Mark gives us both in a single sentence, and we should hold on to both. Jesus is angry. This is not mild disappointment, and it should not be softened into something more comfortable. He looks around at people who were waiting for a charge while a man who could be restored stood in front of them. But the same sentence says he is deeply distressed at their stubborn hearts. His anger is not cold. It is the anger of someone who is grieved by what hardness does and grieved for the hard.

Then look at what his anger does next. It does not humiliate them or strike back at them. He turns to the man: “Stretch out your hand.” In Jesus, anger held together with grief moves toward restoration. That is worth remembering whenever our own anger claims to be righteous. His anger, in this scene, ends in a man made whole.

“He stretched it out, and his hand was completely restored.” There is no touch, only a word, a man doing what the word told him, and a hand made new. Mark says completely. This is a body being mended, not a lesson being illustrated. The hand mattered to Jesus because the man mattered to him. It is not a promise that every body will be mended in this life; nothing here makes that promise. But we can see from it what the Son of Man’s lordship is for. It is on the side of life.

Then comes the scene’s last line: “Then the Pharisees went out and began to plot with the Herodians how they might kill Jesus.” Mark names who went out and whom they joined. Religious leaders who cared about holy days and the political allies of King Herod do not usually share a table, but they found common cause against the man who healed on the Sabbath. Religion and political power have often joined hands against those who threaten their control. What he does is set two things side by side. Jesus asked whether the Sabbath was for saving life or for killing, and he answered his part of that question with a restored hand. The other answer is given as they leave. Mark adds no comment. He does not need to.

The passage opened with Jesus speaking of a day when the bridegroom would be taken away. It closes with the first plan to kill him. He had already spoken of being taken, and he healed in the open anyway. Much later in Mark he will say that the Son of Man “did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many” (10:45). Here, near the start of that road, we can already see what kind of Lord walks it: one who restores a man’s hand in full view even as opposition gathers around him.

Mark 2:18–3:6 In One Movement

Holy things appear in every scene: a fast, the Scriptures, consecrated bread, the Sabbath, the synagogue. Jesus treats each of them with respect, and he will not let any of them stand apart from him. The fast waits on the bridegroom. The Scriptures open onto David and his hungry companions. The day made for humanity answers to the Son of Man. And the question of what that day is for is settled in a man’s body, when a hand that did not work is made whole at a word. Around Jesus, meanwhile, the scrutiny tightens, from questions to close watching to silence to a plan to kill, and none of it requires anyone to stop keeping holy things. That is both the sobering heart of the passage and its beautiful one. God’s gifts were never meant to be kept apart from their Lord. And their Lord is the kind who uses his authority to defend his companions and to restore a man in front of everyone, and who walks, with anger full of grief, toward the day he himself will be taken away.

Scripture Cross-References

Hosea 2:19–20

“I will betroth you to me forever; I will betroth you in righteousness and justice, in love and compassion.” The prophets pictured the LORD as a husband to his people. When Jesus casts himself as the bridegroom whose presence makes fasting out of place, he speaks in language Israel had used for God’s own covenant love.

1 Samuel 21:6

“So the priest gave him the consecrated bread.” This is the account Jesus sends his critics back to: David and his hungry companions fed with holy bread. Jesus answers a Sabbath charge from inside Israel’s Scriptures and places his own companions beside David’s.

Genesis 2:2–3

“On the seventh day he rested from all his work. Then God blessed the seventh day and made it holy.” The seventh day begins as God’s own delight in a finished, good work. When Jesus says the Sabbath was “made for man,” he reaches back to that beginning: the day is a gift.

Deuteronomy 5:14–15

“…nor any foreigner residing in your towns, so that your male and female servants may rest, as you do. Remember that you were slaves in Egypt.” The Sabbath command names the servant and the foreigner by name. The day made for humanity was made especially for those who could not rest on their own.

Isaiah 58:6–7

“Is not this the kind of fasting I have chosen: to loose the chains of injustice… to share your food with the hungry and to provide the poor wanderer with shelter?” Jesus promises that his followers will fast. Isaiah tells us what God wants that fasting to look like.

The Shape of a Kingdom People

A people shaped by this passage will not be embarrassed by holy practices, and they will not be satisfied with them either. Jesus expects his followers to fast. He speaks of the Sabbath as a gift made for human beings. He argues from Scripture. A church that throws away discipline in the name of freedom has not understood him. But a church can also keep every practice running and slowly forget whom it is for. The test this passage offers is not whether a practice is old or new, strict or relaxed. It is whether the practice is an answer to Jesus. Our feasting should sound like joy that the bridegroom has come. Our fasting should sound like longing for him, and it should look like Isaiah’s fast: chains loosened, the hungry fed, the wanderer given shelter. Our rest should be received as a gift from the one who made it, not claimed as a reward we earned. And because the Sabbath was made for servants and foreigners too, a church that keeps it will care whether the people who clean its buildings, pick its food, and serve its tables ever get a day of rest themselves.

The passage also speaks to everyone who holds some authority over holy things: pastors, elders, parents, anyone who keeps the rules of a community. The Lord of the Sabbath used his authority to answer for his companions and to restore a man in public. That is what authority over God’s gifts is for. It is not a reason to loosen every standard, as if need settled every question; Jesus still asked what was lawful. But when a rule we keep has become our reason not to do plain good to the person standing in front of us, his question returns to us: which is lawful, to do good or to do evil?

A community formed here will also make room for the man in the middle. Jesus did not treat him as a test case or a sermon illustration. He called him to stand in front of everyone as himself. Churches honor that when people with disabilities belong as full members, contributing and not only being served, seen as people and never used as lessons. And because this passage tells of a real restoration without promising every reader the same, such a community will never treat a body that has not been healed as a failure of faith. Christ’s people can pray for healing, give thanks when it comes, and still hold that no one’s worth depends on it.

Finally, a people shaped here will learn two kinds of watchfulness. The first is over our anger. Some things in this world should anger Christians, and Jesus shows that anger need not leave love behind: his was full of grief, and its next act was restoration. Anger that turns into contempt or retaliation has left his side. The second watchfulness is over our own hearts. The danger in that synagogue was not that people valued the Sabbath. It was that holy practice became a vantage point for watching someone closely in order to accuse him. Christians can do the same, watching one another’s practices for a fault. Later in Mark, Jesus will ask his own disciples whether their hearts are hardened. The fitting response is not suspicion of others but honesty about ourselves, and a return to the Lord to whom every holy thing belongs.

Gospel-Shaped Response

From this passage we can learn to move in a grace-first order. We begin with who God is and what he has done, because our identity is shaped by his character and work, not by our performance. When we forget those truths, unbelief grows in distorted emotions and patterns of life. Faithful response and mission then flow out of trust in the gospel rather than pressure to earn God’s favor.

Who is God?

1 · Character of God

  • God gives before he asks. The Sabbath was made for human beings as a gift, and his commandments came to a people he had already brought out of slavery.
  • God delights to be with his people. Jesus pictures his presence as a wedding, the kind of joy in which fasting would be out of place.
  • God’s authority is on the side of life. The Son of Man is Lord even of the Sabbath, and he uses that lordship to defend his companions and restore a man’s hand.
  • God is not indifferent to hardened hearts. In Jesus we see anger at hearts closed against the good, joined with grief over those same hearts.

What has God done?

2 · Work of God

  • In Jesus, the bridegroom has come to be with his people, and his presence changes what their devotion means.
  • He has opened the Scriptures to show that the Sabbath was made for human life, and he has claimed that day as its Lord.
  • He restored a man’s hand with a word, in the open, knowing he was being watched.
  • He walked toward the day he would be taken away. The Son of Man came “to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many” (10:45).

Who are we in light of what God has done?

3 · New Identity

  • We are guests of the bridegroom. Our practices are responses to a person, not attempts to earn a place with him.
  • We are companions he answers for. When our lives are questioned, we are not left to defend ourselves alone.
  • We are the people for whom the Sabbath was made. Rest is a gift we receive, not a reward we deserve.
  • We are a people who can both feast and fast, because both answer to him.

What wrong behavior, harmful emotions, or false attitudes result when we forget these truths?

4 · Unbelief

  • We keep holy practices while forgetting whose they are, until fasting, rest, or Bible reading becomes a way of measuring ourselves instead of turning toward him.
  • We use holy things as a place to stand while we watch others closely for a fault, instead of as a place from which to seek their good.
  • We go quiet when the good in front of us is plain, because doing it would be inconvenient or costly.
  • We swing the other way and treat freedom as permission to look down on God’s gifts. We forget that Jesus promised his people would fast and that new wine still needs wineskins.
  • We let anger harden into contempt for those we judge hard-hearted, and we lose the grief that kept Jesus’ anger on the side of restoration.

How would we live if we believed these truths?

5 · Faithful Response

  • We would feast with gladness that he has come. When we fast, we would fast as people longing for him rather than performing for anyone.
  • We would receive rest as a gift and let it be a day for life: worship, delight, mending, and doing good.
  • We would bring hard questions about rules and need to Jesus’ own question, asking what doing good looks like here, without pretending that need alone settles everything.
  • We would let our anger at harm stay joined to grief and move toward restoration.

If we lived these truths by faith, how might they overflow into the real places and relationships God has given us?

6 · Mission

  • In homes and workplaces, we would use whatever authority we hold the way the Lord of the Sabbath used his: to protect and restore the people under it, including making sure that those whose work serves us have rest.
  • We would make our churches places where people with disabilities belong as full members, seen as people and never used as illustrations.
  • To friends who think faith is only a set of rules, we would show a Lord whose authority serves life. We would do this not by mocking discipline but by practicing it gladly with him at the center.
  • When we meet hostility, we would answer without retaliation: with plain questions, honest grief, and good aimed at restoration.

Hearing the Father’s Voice

The Bible is God’s love letter to us. When we read it, we do not want accusation, fear, shame, or distance to distort what God has spoken. In this passage, the Father shows us that his gifts are given for our life, and that his Son, the Lord of all we keep, uses his authority to restore rather than to crush. Here is what this passage sounds like when the Father says it to you.

My child, I did not give you my good gifts to weigh you down. I made rest for you. I named the servant and the foreigner in my command so that no one would be left out of it, and if you have worked without rest, I made that day for you too. I have given you my Son to stand at the center of everything you keep. When you feast, rejoice that he has come to you. When you fast, fast toward him, not to win my attention; you already have it. Bring your practices to him, and let him give them their shape.

My child, when you feel watched, talked about, or used as someone else’s argument, or when your body does not do what you wish it would, remember the man my Son called to stand in front of everyone. He was not a lesson to my Son. He was a person, and he was seen. You are seen that way too. I do not promise that every hurt will be mended now. But my Son’s authority is on the side of your life. He did not come to be served. He came to serve, and he gave his life for you.
