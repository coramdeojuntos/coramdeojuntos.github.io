The Kingdom Comes as Seed

Big Picture: What’s Happening Here?

By the end of Mark 3, something has become hard to explain. Jesus came into Galilee announcing that “the kingdom of God has come near” (1:15), and since then he has driven out demons, healed the sick, and forgiven sins. Yet his own family has set out to take charge of him because they think he is out of his mind, and teachers of the law who came down from Jerusalem have decided that his power comes from the prince of demons. If God’s reign has really arrived in this man, how can people look straight at it and see madness, or Satan? Mark places the longest stretch of Jesus’ teaching we have yet heard directly after that scene, and it reads like an answer given almost entirely in pictures of seed.

Seed is a strange image to choose for a kingdom, and an honest one. It is alive, and it is small. Once it is sown it disappears into the ground. It can be eaten before it sprouts, scorched after it sprouts, or strangled while it grows. A farmer who scatters seed is not displaying a harvest; he is letting go of something valuable into ground he does not fully command. In this chapter, that is how God’s reign comes among people — through a word to be heard, not through a display so overwhelming that hearing would be unnecessary. This is not because divine power is missing. A chapter earlier Jesus answered his accusers with the picture of someone who ties up the strong man and plunders his house (3:27). But here beside the lake, the kingdom comes as seed rather than spectacle.

So the chapter holds together two things we tend to pull apart. The word’s reception is uneven, and Jesus treats that with great seriousness: the word can be taken away, can wither, can be choked, and he warns his hearers to “consider carefully what you hear.” And the kingdom’s future is not hostage to anyone’s misunderstanding or anyone’s management. The seed grows while the farmer sleeps. What is hidden is meant to come into the open. The smallest seed becomes a plant with branches wide enough for birds to rest in its shade. Running between those two truths is a quiet mercy. The people who receive Jesus’ explanation are not the ones who understood the parable. They are the ones who did not — and before he explains anything, he tells them the secret of the kingdom has already been given to them.

Passage Commentary

A Story About Sowing, Told from a Boat

Mark 4:1–9

“Again Jesus began to teach by the lake.” The crowd is so large that he gets into a boat and sits in it out on the lake, “while all the people were along the shore at the water’s edge.” Mark tells us nothing about why they came or what they believed. He gives us a seated teacher, a strip of water, and a very large number of people within reach of his voice, and he tells us that Jesus “taught them many things by parables.” Whatever else this chapter is, it begins as public teaching, offered to everyone standing at the edge of the lake.

The first word is a command: “Listen!” Then comes a story about a farmer who “went out to sow his seed.” Notice who is telling it and what he is doing while he tells it. Jesus sits in a boat before a crowded shore and casts words toward the people gathered there. He tells a story about sowing while he himself is speaking the word. Mark never says the farmer is Jesus, and we should not make him say it. But in a few moments Jesus will explain that “the farmer sows the word” (4:14), and Mark will sum up the whole scene by saying that Jesus “spoke the word to them” (4:33). The parable is taking place even as it is told. Every person on that shore is, at that moment, somewhere the word is landing.

The story is plainly told, and it rewards attention before we hurry on to the explanation. Some seed falls along the path, “and the birds came and ate it up.” Some falls on rocky places, where “it sprang up quickly, because the soil was shallow,” and then the sun scorches the plants and they wither “because they had no root.” Some falls among thorns, which grow up and choke the plants, “so that they did not bear grain.” And some falls on good soil, where it “came up, grew and produced a crop, some multiplying thirty, some sixty, some a hundred times.” The three losses are not one loss told three times. One seed never gets started. One starts fast and cannot last. One grows for a while and is crowded out. Even the good soil does not produce one uniform result. Jesus gives no proportions and no seed counts, and this is not a forecast of how many people will believe. It is a picture of what actually happens when living seed meets different ground.

Then he says it again, as a summons rather than a story: “Whoever has ears to hear, let them hear.” The parable does not finish itself. It waits to be received. Jesus is not ranking bodies here. The hearing he calls for is the welcome of a word — letting it in and keeping it — which no sensory ability guarantees and no disability rules out. A Deaf believer reading this chapter is as fully addressed by that summons as anyone who stood on the shore that day.

A Secret Given and a Severe Word

Mark 4:10–12

When Jesus is alone, “the Twelve and the others around him” ask him about the parables. Mark does not say only the Twelve. The circle is wider and less tidy than that. He has just shown us a similar picture: Jesus’ mother and brothers “standing outside,” a crowd “sitting around him,” and Jesus saying, “Whoever does God’s will is my brother and sister and mother” (3:31–35). Now the language of around and outside returns. Yet Mark gives no hint that sitting in the right place or asking the right question is what earns what comes next.

What comes next is a gift, spoken before any explanation: “The secret of the kingdom of God has been given to you.” Has been given. After the severe word about those outside, Jesus turns back to the parable and tells these same people that they have not understood it. So whatever this secret is, it was not handed over as a reward for comprehension. Mark does not spell out its contents in a sentence. The chapter around it suggests that the secret concerns God’s reign actually arriving, now, through Jesus and his word — truly present, but in a form that can be looked at and missed. These learners have been let in on that before they can explain it.

Then comes one of the hardest sentences in the Gospel. “But to those on the outside everything is said in parables so that, ‘they may be ever seeing but never perceiving, and ever hearing but never understanding; otherwise they might turn and be forgiven!’” These are words from the commissioning of the prophet Isaiah, and Jesus does not soften them. Mark writes “so that.” What is at stake is turning and forgiveness — the forgiveness Jesus had spoken of so widely a chapter earlier, when he said that “people can be forgiven all their sins” (3:28). How should we hear this? Is Jesus describing a judgment that confirms a refusal already made? Is he speaking the way the prophets sometimes spoke, naming a foreseen outcome as though it were the aim? Do the parables expose what hearers already want? Mark does not stop to tell us, and it would be dishonest to pretend he does. The sentence is meant to be felt as severe, and it is.

Some things can be said with confidence, and they matter. “Outside” is not a people group. It is not Israel, not the Jewish people, not Jesus’ relatives, not the crowd as a whole, and not everyone who is slow to understand. Isaiah spoke these words as Israel’s own prophet, and Mark will later show Jesus pressing almost the same questions on his own disciples: “Do you have eyes but fail to see, and ears but fail to hear?” (8:18). No reader of Mark 4 gets to stand comfortably inside and point out. And this hard word is not the chapter’s last word; a few sentences later Jesus will say that hidden things are meant for the open. That does not make 4:12 gentler, and it does not explain it. It means we hear its warning — that people can see and hear and still not turn — from the lips of one who said that preaching was the reason he had come (1:38).

What Happens to the Word

Mark 4:13–20

“Don’t you understand this parable? How then will you understand any parable?” The gift has already been spoken before this rebuke. Gift and rebuke sit side by side, and neither cancels the other. Jesus does not pretend these learners understand, and he does not send them away for failing to. He explains. This is what grace before comprehension looks like: the gift is real before the understanding is, and the understanding still matters enough to be rebuked and taught.

His explanation opens with a sentence that governs everything after it: “The farmer sows the word.” The seed is never faulted. The same word goes out in every case, and in every case people hear it. Follow the verb through the explanation: those along the path hear, those on rocky ground hear, those among thorns hear, those on good soil hear. The difference is not whether the word reaches people. It is what becomes of the word once it has.

Along the path, “as soon as they hear it, Satan comes and takes away the word that was sown in them.” Mark does not let us shrink that into a lesson about poor concentration. After a chapter in which Jesus was accused of serving Satan, we are reminded that the kingdom arrives in contested territory and that there is an enemy who wants the word gone. But notice what Jesus names and what he does not. He names a theft of the word. He does not give us a way to detect the enemy behind every question, doubt, wandering mind, mental-health struggle, or change of subject — in ourselves, and still less in other people. The fitting response to the reality of an enemy is not suspicion of one another. It is holding on to the word, and to the one who ties up the strong man.

On rocky ground, people “hear the word and at once receive it with joy.” The joy is not the problem; joy is a fitting way to receive good news. The problem Jesus names is that “they have no root,” so “they last only a short time.” And the pressure that exposes it is specific: “When trouble or persecution comes because of the word, they quickly fall away.” Hearing Jesus can bring trouble rather than remove it. That is sobering, and it is also a kindness to be told in advance. The suffering is not the failure; falling away under it is. Frightened believers are not rocky ground for being frightened, and persecution is never evidence that God has let someone go. Before this very day is over, Jesus’ disciples will be terrified in a boat (4:40–41), and he will still be in the boat with them.

Among thorns, people hear, “but the worries of this life, the deceitfulness of wealth and the desires for other things come in and choke the word, making it unfruitful.” These are three pressures, each with its own texture. The worries of this life are the ordinary cares of keeping a life going, and they can quietly take over the room the word needs. Jesus is not diagnosing anxiety disorders or depression here; someone who needs medical care for anxiety has not thereby shown that the word is being choked. Wealth is called deceitful because it promises what it cannot give — safety, standing, enough. Later in Mark a man runs up to Jesus, and Jesus looks at him and loves him, and the man goes away sad “because he had great wealth” (10:21–22). The warning is real; it is also not a way to read anyone’s soul from their bank balance, rich or poor. And “the desires for other things” are often desires for good things, grown so tall that nothing else gets the light. Thorns do not kill quickly. The seed in this ground actually grows. It simply never bears grain.

Then the good soil: people “hear the word, accept it, and produce a crop—some thirty, some sixty, some a hundred times what was sown.” Mark does not tell us what the crop consists of, and it is worth resisting the urge to fill that silence with what our world counts — income, influence, output, attendance, platform. The nearest picture Mark has given may be the one that closes chapter 3, where Jesus’ family is made up of those who do God’s will. Later, Jesus will promise those who leave things behind for him “a hundred times as much in this present age … along with persecutions” (10:30). That is a different image, but it is a reminder that in Mark abundance and hardship are not opposites. The yields here differ, and all of them are harvest.

This explanation invites honest self-examination: which of these pressures is at work on the word in me? It does not invite us to sort the people we love into soils. The very learners who heard this explanation will fail to understand again, and Jesus will keep teaching them. He gives none of us the task of pronouncing which ground a brother, a child, a neighbor, or an opponent is.

A Lamp Belongs on Its Stand

Mark 4:21–25

“Do you bring in a lamp to put it under a bowl or a bed? Instead, don’t you put it on its stand?” The question is almost comic. Nobody lights a lamp in order to hide it. And after the severe sentence about those outside, this is what we most need to hear: “For whatever is hidden is meant to be disclosed, and whatever is concealed is meant to be brought out into the open.” The concealment in this chapter is real, but it is not the goal. The kingdom is not a club for people with inside information. What is hidden now is on its way into the open.

Mark does not tell us exactly what the lamp is — Jesus himself, his word, the kingdom — and we do not need to settle it to see where the saying points. Mark’s own story later offers a glimpse of how hiddenness can serve disclosure. Coming down a mountain, Jesus tells three disciples not to tell anyone what they have seen “until the Son of Man had risen from the dead” (9:9). Some hiddenness in Mark is for a season. That glimpse does not explain everything about the lamp, but it shows that silence and disclosure can both belong to God’s timing. Nor is this saying a demand that we expose every private corner of our lives. The lamp concerns God making his kingdom known, not a duty to be constantly on display.

“If anyone has ears to hear, let them hear.” The summons returns, and then Jesus sharpens it: “Consider carefully what you hear.” He goes on: “With the measure you use, it will be measured to you—and even more. Whoever has will be given more; whoever does not have, even what they have will be taken from them.” In Mark, this measure saying is tied directly to hearing. The measure is the attention and welcome we bring to his word. Mark does not name what the one who “has” possesses, and it is better not to supply a word he left out; in this setting it seems to concern what has been heard and received. Two things stand out. There is a warning: a word neglected does not stay put, and even what seemed to be held can slip away. And there is a surprising excess — “and even more.” The one who hears is not repaid in exact proportion. More is given. Even the increase is a gift.

Growth the Farmer Cannot Explain

Mark 4:26–29

“This is what the kingdom of God is like.” For the first time in the chapter, Jesus states the comparison outright. “A man scatters seed on the ground. Night and day, whether he sleeps or gets up, the seed sprouts and grows, though he does not know how. All by itself the soil produces grain—first the stalk, then the head, then the full kernel in the head. As soon as the grain is ripe, he puts the sickle to it, because the harvest has come.”

Two things are true of this farmer, and the parable needs both. He acts. He scatters the seed, and when the time comes he harvests; the grain does not cut itself. And the growth is not his. He does not cause it, cannot hurry it, and does not understand it. It goes on while he is asleep. This parable does not say that every sowing succeeds; the story of the four kinds of ground has just told us otherwise. What it says is that the life of God’s kingdom is not in the farmer’s hands. That rules out two familiar mistakes at once. God grows it, so I needn’t sow — but the man sows. If I sow the right way, growth is guaranteed — but the man does not even know how growth happens. His not-knowing is not a flaw to be corrected with a better method. It is the honest position of anyone who serves a kingdom that belongs to God.

Mark will show us another sleeper before this day is out. That evening, in the same boat, Jesus is “in the stern, sleeping on a cushion” while a squall rises and his friends are sure they are going under (4:38). Mark draws no line between the two scenes, and the connection should not be made to carry more than it can. But the farmer’s rest in the parable is not laziness. It is what trust looks like when the growing belongs to someone else.

The stages — stalk, then head, then full kernel — tell us that growth takes ordinary time and passes through forms that do not yet look like harvest. They are not a chart that every believer or church must move through on schedule. And the harvest is real. The parable ends not with endless waiting but with ripe grain, a sickle, and a harvest that “has come.” Mark gives no timetable and adds no further detail. He gives a direction: the kingdom God has begun is heading toward harvest.

The Smallest Seed and the Largest Garden Plant

Mark 4:30–32

“What shall we say the kingdom of God is like, or what parable shall we use to describe it?” It is lovely that Jesus asks the question aloud, as though no single picture could quite hold what he means. Then he chooses one of the least impressive things available: “a mustard seed, which is the smallest of all seeds on earth. Yet when planted, it grows and becomes the largest of all garden plants, with such big branches that the birds can perch in its shade.” There is no need to take this to a botanist, whether to defend Jesus or to correct him. The point lives in the contrast he draws — a beginning so small it is almost nothing, and an outcome out of all proportion to it.

Think about what the kingdom looked like to the people in these chapters. A teacher sitting in a boat. A few fishermen and others he had called. A family convinced he had lost his mind. Opponents sure his power came from the prince of demons. Jesus does not answer by pointing to anything impressive. He says, in effect, that this is what the beginning of God’s reign looks like, and that it will not stay this size. If you belong to people who are small in the world’s eyes, a congregation that meets in a rented room, a community no one in power takes seriously, a family with almost nothing, hear this: God’s kingdom has always begun in places like yours. Its smallness now is not a measure of what God is doing.

The branches and shade carry an echo of Israel’s prophets. Ezekiel pictured God planting a tender sprig that becomes a splendid cedar, where “birds of every kind will nest in it; they will find shelter in the shade of its branches,” and added, “I the LORD bring down the tall tree and make the low tree grow tall” (Ezekiel 17:23–24). Birds of every kind. Shelter in the shade. God lifting up the low. Look, though, at what the size is for in Jesus’ telling: branches big enough for birds to perch in their shade. This is not a promise that any church, movement, or institution will grow large or come to dominate the culture around it. The kingdom’s greatness is God’s to bring about, and in Jesus’ picture it looks like shelter: a place where those who have nowhere to land can come and rest in the shade. If you are looking for that kind of place, this is the kingdom Jesus is describing.

As Much as They Could Understand

Mark 4:33–34

“With many similar parables Jesus spoke the word to them, as much as they could understand.” Mark closes the scene by describing how Jesus taught. There were many more parables than the ones we have been given, and he spoke them in a measure fitted to his hearers — a teacher paying attention to what people could take in. The same parables that conceal are also, in the same moment, the way he reaches people where they are.

“He did not say anything to them without using a parable. But when he was alone with his own disciples, he explained everything.” This describes this scene and this stage of his teaching; later in Mark, Jesus will speak about his coming suffering very plainly (8:32). And the difference between public parables and private explanation is not a line between clever people and slow ones. The disciples were the ones who had not understood; that is why they needed everything explained. Nor did explanation turn into mastery. Before the night is over they will be asking one another, “Who is this?” (4:41), and several chapters later Jesus will still be asking them, “Do you still not understand?” (8:21). Disciples still live here: given more than we have grasped, and taught by someone who has not given up on us.

Mark 4:1–34 In One Movement

Sitting in a boat on the lake, Jesus scatters a word over a crowded shore and tells his hearers what becomes of a word when it is sown: it is taken away, it withers, it is choked, and it yields a harvest far greater than what was sown. He gives the secret of the kingdom to people who have not understood the story, speaks a severe word over those who see without perceiving, and then insists that nothing is hidden except to be brought into the open. He warns that how people hear matters, and promises that more will be given. Then he shows them the kingdom itself: seed that grows while the farmer sleeps, and a seed so small it hardly seems to count becoming a plant with shade enough for birds. The whole chapter says that God’s reign has come among us as seed rather than spectacle — alive and real, genuinely vulnerable in how it is received, and not governed by anyone’s misunderstanding or anyone’s management. The word can be lost to a hearer; the kingdom is not lost. So Jesus asks for careful, receiving, fruitful hearing, and he gives his hearers something to rest in: the life of the kingdom was never theirs to control. He keeps speaking, he keeps explaining, and what God has begun is heading for harvest.

Scripture Cross-References

Isaiah 6:1–10

Before Isaiah was sent, he saw the Lord enthroned in holiness, cried out that he was a man of unclean lips, and had his lips cleansed with a coal from the altar; only then did he offer himself to be sent. The message he received included the words Jesus quotes in Mark 4:12 about people who hear without understanding. The severe word came through a man who had just received mercy himself. In Mark, the line those words draw runs through how people hear, not through ancestry.

Ezekiel 17:22–24

“Birds of every kind will nest in it; they will find shelter in the shade of its branches… I the LORD bring down the tall tree and make the low tree grow tall.” The branches and shade of Jesus’ mustard plant carry this hope: God planting something tiny that becomes shelter for every kind, and lifting up the low.

Luke 12:32

Jesus tells his “little flock” not to be afraid, because their Father is pleased “to give you the kingdom.” The kingdom is given rather than achieved, and its coming does not depend on the number of those waiting for it. The gift of Mark 4:11 and the smallness of Mark 4:31 meet in one sentence.

James 1:18–21

James speaks of new birth “through the word of truth” and then urges believers to “humbly accept the word planted in you.” James carries the same grace-first direction: God’s word comes to us before our response to it, and we are called to receive what we did not create. Good soil does not make the seed; it receives it.

Mark 8:17–21

Jesus asks his disciples, “Do you have eyes but fail to see, and ears but fail to hear?” and then, “Do you still not understand?” Language shaped like Mark 4:12 is turned toward the insiders. The secret given in 4:11 has not become mastery, and Jesus is still teaching them.

The Shape of a Kingdom People

A community shaped by Mark 4 is made up of people who have been given more than they understand. That should make it a humble place, and a patient one. When the Twelve and the others around Jesus came with their bewildered question, they were met first with a gift, then with a rebuke, then with an explanation — and not with dismissal. A kingdom people can receive questions the same way, without treating them as unbelief, and can keep learning together over years instead of pretending to a mastery nobody in Mark possesses. Because the hearing Jesus summons is the welcome of a word rather than a test of ears or speed, such a people will take care that his word actually reaches everyone in the room: those who are Deaf, those with cognitive disabilities, those who learn slowly, those who cannot yet put their faith into fluent words. Understanding still matters — Jesus rebuked his learners for lacking it — but no one earns a place by having more of it.

This community will also name the pressures Jesus names, without shame and without flattening them. It will stand with people who suffer because of the word, near or far, rather than wondering what they did wrong. It will take the worries of this life seriously as a spiritual pressure while treating anxiety and depression with compassion and fitting care, never as proof that someone’s faith is being strangled. It will speak honestly about the deceitfulness of wealth, without flattering the rich or romanticizing the poor, since neither a large income nor an empty account tells us where anyone stands with God. And it will be sober about Satan without growing suspicious of one another, since Jesus named the enemy’s theft of the word but never told us to diagnose each other.

A kingdom people will also look like the mustard plant’s shade. In a world full of people with nowhere to land, migrants, the homeless, people pushed out of one place after another, a church shaped by this chapter will make room in its branches. It will not need to be large to do it. It only needs to be the kind of place where “birds of every kind” can find shelter.

Finally, a kingdom people will sow without trying to manage the harvest. It will speak of Jesus generously, on every kind of ground. In Jesus’ own story seed is lost on the path, on rock, and among thorns, and the story never blames the farmer for it. A small congregation need not despair over its size, and a large one has no reason to boast about its own; the kingdom’s growth is God’s reality, not a forecast for our projects. And none of this becomes an excuse for passivity. The farmer in the parable still scatters seed, still rises each morning, and still puts in the sickle when the grain is ripe.

Gospel-Shaped Response

From this passage we can learn to move in a grace-first order. We begin with who God is and what he has done, because our identity is shaped by his character and work, not by our performance. When we forget those truths, unbelief grows in distorted emotions and patterns of life. Faithful response and mission then flow out of trust in the gospel rather than pressure to earn God’s favor.

Who is God?

1 · Character of God

  • God reigns, and in Jesus his reign has truly come near. The one who ties up the strong man is not weak; in this chapter he chooses to come as seed rather than spectacle, through a word that must be heard.
  • God gives before we grasp. “Has been given” comes before “Don’t you understand?” He does not wait for our comprehension before he is generous.
  • God intends to be known. Whatever is hidden is meant to be disclosed; concealment is never his final purpose.
  • God’s kingdom has a life we do not supply. The seed grows while the farmer sleeps, in ways no one can explain or manage.

What has God done?

2 · Work of God

  • Through Jesus, God has spoken his word publicly and widely, toward a crowded shore, to people whose response was not controlled or coerced.
  • He has given the secret of the kingdom to learners, explained what they did not understand, and kept on explaining.
  • He has not hidden the cost. Through Jesus he has told his hearers in advance about the enemy who takes the word, the trouble that comes because of it, and the thorns that choke it.
  • He has begun something that looks almost like nothing, and he has pointed it toward harvest.

Who are we in light of what God has done?

3 · New Identity

  • We are recipients before we are experts — people to whom the kingdom’s secret has been given and who are still learning what it means.
  • We are hearers addressed by a living word. Our standing does not rest on quick understanding, sensory ability, or spiritual fluency.
  • We are free to sow and to harvest without being the ones who make anything grow — free to work hard and free to sleep.
  • We belong to a kingdom whose present smallness is not the measure of its future.

What wrong behavior, harmful emotions, or false attitudes result when we forget these truths?

4 · Unbelief

  • We hear without receiving, taking in Jesus’ words as information while keeping them at a distance, as though hearing and welcoming were the same thing.
  • We welcome the word while it costs nothing and let go of Jesus when trouble comes because of him, trusting our comfort more than his faithfulness.
  • We let the worries of this life, the promises of wealth, or our other desires grow until they crowd out the word, believing that money or ease can give us what only he gives. This is different from living with anxiety or depression, which calls for care, not accusation.
  • We replace trust with control — measuring the kingdom by visible results, despairing over smallness or boasting in size, or, from the other side, using God’s sovereignty as an excuse not to sow.
  • We treat the secret as status, looking down on those who do not understand and assigning soils to other people.

How would we live if we believed these truths?

5 · Faithful Response

  • We would listen to Jesus carefully and often, bringing our confusion to him rather than hiding it, expecting that more will be given.
  • We would hold on to Jesus when following him brings trouble, not treating our fear as a disqualification, but refusing to let the pressure have the final say.
  • We would name our thorns honestly — which worries have started to rule us, which promises of wealth we have believed, which desires have grown tall — and make room for the word again.
  • We would do the work in front of us and then rest, trusting God with growth we cannot see and cannot hurry.

If we lived these truths by faith, how might they overflow into the real places and relationships God has given us?

6 · Mission

  • We would speak of Jesus generously, on every kind of ground, without trying to predict who will receive the word or deciding in advance who will not.
  • We would measure faithfulness rather than size, honoring small and hidden work and refusing the triumphalism that treats growth as proof of God’s approval.
  • We would stand with people who suffer because of the word, near and far, and never read their suffering as a sign that God has let them go.
  • We would be patient with slow learners and honest questioners around us, as Jesus has been patient with us, and make sure his word can actually reach everyone among us.

Hearing the Father’s Voice

The Bible is God’s love letter to us. When we read it, we do not want accusation, fear, shame, or distance to distort what God has spoken. In this passage, the Father shows us that his kingdom comes among us like seed: given before we understand it, genuinely vulnerable in how we receive it, and alive beyond anything we can control. Here is what this passage sounds like when the Father says it to you.

My child, I gave you the secret of my kingdom before you understood it, and I did not wait for your understanding before I gave it. When my Son’s words confuse you, bring your confusion to him; he has not stopped explaining. Listen to him carefully. Do not let the worries of this life, the promises of wealth, or your other desires grow over the word he has spoken to you, and when trouble comes because of him, hold on to him.

My child, the life of my kingdom is not yours to keep alive. You may sow, and sleep, and rise, and sow again; the growing is mine. When what I am doing looks small to you, and when you feel small yourself, remember that I do not measure my kingdom by its beginnings. I bring down the tall tree and make the low tree grow tall. And if you have been looking for somewhere to rest, I am growing a place for you, with shade enough for every kind. What is hidden now is meant to be brought into the open.
