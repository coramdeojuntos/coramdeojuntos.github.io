Big Picture: What’s Happening Here?

For most of this passage, Jesus does nothing at all. Earlier in Mark he slept in the stern of a boat in a storm and stood silent before his judges. Here the stillness is death. From the moment Mark turns away from the cross, every sentence about Jesus is something other people do. Women watch him. Joseph asks for him. Pilate asks whether he is already dead, and is told that he is. Joseph takes him down, wraps him, and lays him in the rock. Two women note the place. Three come back with spices. He is the one watched, carried, laid down, and looked for, and Mark takes his time with all of it. This is a real death, and the people around it are doing what can still be done for a body.

Then a young man in a white robe speaks, and Jesus becomes the one doing things again. “He has risen! He is not here.” “He is going ahead of you.” “There you will see him, just as he told you.” The one who was carried is on the move, and he is moving toward particular people: “his disciples and Peter.” These are the friends who ran when he was arrested and the man who swore in a courtyard that he did not know him. Jesus promised this on the night he told them they would fall away. The failure came just as he said it would, and the promise is still standing.

Then Mark does something few of us would expect from an Easter story. He ends with the women trembling, fleeing, and saying nothing to anyone, because they were afraid. He does not explain it away. That ending presses on anyone who has stood at a graveside, failed someone they loved, or found that good news would not get past the fear in their throat. The passage lets all of that be real. What it tells us is who moves first. The crucified Jesus is risen and already going ahead of his disciples, before they have regrouped, before Peter has said a word, before the women have found their voices. The future of this story rests on him.

Passage Commentary

Watching from a Distance

Mark 15:40–41

Mark has just given us a centurion’s confession: “Surely this man was the Son of God!” Now he lets his attention move a little farther back, to “some women… watching from a distance.” The distance is Mark’s, and we should keep it. Nobody here is kneeling at the foot of the cross. They are close enough to see and too far away to do anything, which may be the hardest place love ever has to stand. When there is nothing else you are able to do for someone, you can still refuse to look away.

Then Mark tells us something he has not said before: “In Galilee these women had followed him and cared for his needs.” Following and serving are exactly the shape Jesus gave to discipleship. He called fishermen with “Come, follow me” (1:17), and he described his own mission as the Son of Man who “did not come to be served, but to serve” (10:45). These women had been living that shape for a long time, back in Galilee, mostly unmentioned. Their faithfulness did not appear suddenly at the cross. It had a history, and that history had a place. Mark wants us to hear the word Galilee here, because it will come back at the end.

He names three of them: Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James the younger and of Joseph, and Salome. Then he will not let those three names stand for everyone: “Many other women who had come up with him to Jerusalem were also there.” He gives none of them a backstory, and we do not need one. They had followed, they had served, and they were still there. The men who had promised to die with him were gone. The people who stayed were women whose faithfulness no one had bothered to record until now, in a world that often discounted what women saw and said. God chose them to be the first to hear that he is risen. The kingdom remembers forms of faithfulness that public history overlooks. If your own faithfulness has gone unseen, if you have served for years without anyone writing your name down, Mark has written theirs, and God has not missed yours.

A Bold Request for a Dead Man’s Body

Mark 15:42–45

The time is short. It is Preparation Day, the day before the Sabbath, and evening is coming on. Into that narrowing time Mark brings a man we have not met: Joseph of Arimathea, “a prominent member of the Council, who was himself waiting for the kingdom of God.” Mark sets those two descriptions side by side and explains neither of them. Only a chapter earlier he told us that the chief priests and “the whole Sanhedrin” looked for evidence to put Jesus to death, and that “they all condemned him as worthy of death” (14:55, 64). What Mark gives us is a man of standing, waiting for God’s kingdom, who now asks for the body of the one who began his preaching in Galilee with the words “The kingdom of God has come near” (1:15).

He “went boldly to Pilate.” Asking a Roman governor for the body of a man crucified under the charge “THE KING OF THE JEWS” (15:26) was no small thing, and the single word boldly carries all the weight Mark gives it; we need not invent penalties to feel it. Joseph spends his access, his standing, and his nerve on something that can no longer do anything for him. He is not asking for a pardon or a miracle. He is asking to bury a dead man well.

Pilate “was surprised to hear that he was already dead,” so he summons the centurion and asks. Only when he learns from the centurion that it is so does he give the body to Joseph. The centurion is the only one Mark mentions, the soldier who “stood there in front of Jesus” and watched him die (15:39). The man who called Jesus the Son of God is now the man who tells Pilate that Jesus is dead, and Mark lets both statements stand. Rome’s last act toward Jesus in this Gospel is to hear that he has died and hand over his body. Whatever the young man announces on Sunday morning, it will not be that a nearly dead man revived. Mark wants the death to be as real to us as it was to the people who carried the body.

Wrapped, Laid, and Seen

Mark 15:46–47

“So Joseph bought some linen cloth, took down the body, wrapped it in the linen, and placed it in a tomb cut out of rock. Then he rolled a stone against the entrance of the tomb.” Look at the verbs. Nearly every one of them is work done with hands: buying, lowering, wrapping, placing, rolling. This is what love and respect look like when there is no one left to talk to, only a body to care for. Mark gives it a whole verse and no commentary, and that restraint is its own kind of honor. The body in the linen is not an empty shell. It is Jesus, dead, and Joseph treats him with dignity.

The stone is the last of the verbs. Rolling it into place is what people do when something is finished. As far as anyone standing outside that tomb could tell, the story had ended about as well as such a story can end: the body released, wrapped, and closed in.

Then, almost in passing: “Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of Joseph saw where he was laid.” The watching that began at the cross has not stopped. These two women stayed long enough to learn the place, which is how love behaves around a grave. It notices exactly where. They will carry that knowledge back on Sunday morning, and it will give the young man’s words their full weight. The love that memorized the spot is the love that will be invited to look at it.

Spices at Sunrise and a Stone Already Moved

Mark 16:1–4

When the Sabbath is over, the first thing Mark shows the women doing is buying spices. Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome “bought spices so that they might go to anoint Jesus’ body.” They come expecting a body, because they watched him die and saw where he was laid. There is nothing foolish in that; it would be strange if they expected anything else. There is nothing small in it either. Anointing is a kindness for someone who can no longer thank you.

Mark has shown us this kind of love before. At Bethany a woman broke a jar of perfume over Jesus’ head, and when others scolded her, Jesus defended her: “She has done a beautiful thing to me… She poured perfume on my body beforehand to prepare for my burial” (14:6, 8). The women carrying spices on Sunday walk in the same line of love. The only difference is timing. The woman at Bethany anointed his body ahead of his death. These women come to anoint it after his death, and they are about to be overtaken by something only God could do. Their spices will go unused. Their love will not have been wasted.

“Very early on the first day of the week, just after sunrise,” they ask each other the most practical question in the passage: “Who will roll the stone away from the entrance of the tomb?” Two of them had been there when he was laid in that tomb. The question comes from people who know exactly what they are walking toward, and they keep walking. Then “they looked up” and saw that the stone, “which was very large, had been rolled away.” Who moved it goes unsaid; Mark is already taking us inside. Before anyone has said a word to them, the one obstacle they could name has already been dealt with.

The Crucified One Is Not Here

Mark 16:5–6

Inside, where they expected a body, “they saw a young man dressed in a white robe sitting on the right side, and they were alarmed.” Whatever else he is, he carries news that only God could send, and Mark keeps our eyes on what he says.

His first words are gentle: “Don’t be alarmed.” His next words are precise: “You are looking for Jesus the Nazarene, who was crucified.” He names exactly the one they came for, and he does not soften the name. The crucifixion is not left behind at the entrance to the tomb. It stays attached to Jesus in the very sentence that announces he is alive. The risen one is the crucified man from Nazareth: the one they watched die, the one Joseph wrapped, the one whose place they had seen. Resurrection does not bring in a new figure to replace him, and it is not his memory living on among his friends. It is him.

Then comes the sentence the whole passage turns on: “He has risen! He is not here.” Ever since his last cry on the cross, Jesus has been the one others acted on. Now he is the one who acts. The women came to do something for him, and they learn that what has happened is something no one could have done for him: he is no longer among the dead.

“See the place where they laid him.” The young man does not mock the laying. He points to it. The place is real, and so were the linen and the labor. The women who watched Joseph lay him there are the very ones who can recognize it. What they are shown is a place, not a person. They did not see Jesus rise. They see where he is not, and they are told why. Mark gives us both the empty place and the word that explains it, and never lets either one do the other’s work.

Word for the Disciples and Peter

Mark 16:7

“But go, tell his disciples and Peter.” The last time we saw the disciples, “everyone deserted him and fled” (14:50). The last time we saw Peter, he was in the high priest’s courtyard calling down curses and swearing, “I don’t know this man you’re talking about,” and then he “broke down and wept” (14:71–72). The first message sent out from the empty tomb is addressed to them.

And Peter is named. He could have been folded into the disciples and no one would have noticed. Instead the message singles him out. In the NIV, the only other place Mark uses the word Nazarene is that courtyard, where a servant girl said to Peter, “You also were with that Nazarene, Jesus” (14:67), and he denied it. Now the Nazarene is risen, and Peter’s name is in the message. Mark shows us no reunion, no conversation, no tears on either side. We are not told how Peter heard the news or what passed between them later. What we are given is quieter and still enough: after the denial, he is addressed by name.

“He is going ahead of you into Galilee. There you will see him, just as he told you.” Turn back to the night Jesus and his disciples went out to the Mount of Olives. “You will all fall away,” Jesus told them, quoting Scripture: “I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered.” And in the next breath: “But after I have risen, I will go ahead of you into Galilee” (14:27–28). Peter insisted he would never fall away, “and all the others said the same” (14:31). Jesus had already spoken the promise over their failure before any of them had failed. Then the failure came, all of it, just as he said. Now, on the far side of it, the promise comes back unchanged.

That is what “just as he told you” protects. The resurrection and the meeting in Galilee are not a recovery plan assembled after the disciples collapsed. The promise came first, and their failure did not cancel it.

And Galilee is where it began. It is where Jesus called fishermen away from their nets (1:16–20), and where these women had followed him and cared for his needs (15:41). The place Jesus goes ahead to is the place where they first followed. And notice where it is not: not the capital, not the temple, not the seat of the council or the governor. The risen Lord goes ahead to the region of fishermen and farmers, far from the center of power, where his work began among ordinary people. The verb is present tense: he “is going.” He is already on the way before any of them has heard the message, let alone done anything about it. Mark stops short of Galilee and leaves the promise in the tense it was given in: there you will see him.

Trembling, Fleeing, Saying Nothing

Mark 16:8

“Trembling and bewildered, the women went out and fled from the tomb. They said nothing to anyone, because they were afraid.” We should feel the jolt of that sentence. The young man said, “Go, tell,” and Mark reports that they said nothing to anyone. The gap is real, and the passage gives us no permission to close it with a smile.

Nor does it give us permission to despise them. These are the women who watched him die, who stayed to see where he was laid, and who got up early and walked toward a closed tomb carrying spices. Their fear is not the fear of people who never cared. It is the fear of people who have just met something far larger than they can hold. Mark has shown us fear in the presence of God’s power before, and it has not always meant the same thing: disciples who were “terrified” after Jesus calmed the storm (4:41), and a woman who came “trembling with fear” and then heard Jesus commend her faith (5:33–34). Trembling is not automatically unbelief. Yet their fear cannot be dissolved into simple awe, either. Mark sets the command and the silence side by side and leaves them there.

What happened next? This passage does not say. It tells us neither that their silence lasted nor that they spoke the next morning. Many Bibles, including the NIV, print a note after this verse saying that the earliest manuscripts and some other ancient witnesses do not have verses 9–20. Whatever one concludes about that, the passage in front of us closes on verse 8. The last human movement in it is flight.

It is not the only movement in the story, though. While the women run from the tomb, the risen Jesus is going ahead into Galilee. Mark leaves their response unresolved; he does not leave Jesus’ movement unresolved. The good news did not wait on them. Before they arrived, he had risen. While they ran, he was going ahead. And the promise still stood: there you will see him, just as he told you. Their fear is the last thing Mark reports. It is not the last thing that is true.

Mark 15:40–16:8 In One Movement

The passage begins with people standing still and ends with people running. At the start, everything gathers around a place: a cross watched from a distance, a body lowered, a tomb cut out of rock, a spot two women saw and remembered. It is the geography of grief, and Mark honors every step of it. Then one sentence moves the center of the story out of that place altogether: “He is not here.” From then on, the passage is about a road. The crucified Jesus is risen and on his way to Galilee, ahead of the disciples who ran and ahead of the friend who swore he never knew him, carrying a promise he made before any of them failed. The women run too, trembling and silent, and Mark lets their fear be the last thing he tells us. But by then he has shown us whose movement the future depends on. The death was real, and so was the care given to it. The failure was real, and so is the fear. And the one who was laid in the rock is already going ahead, just as he told them.

Scripture Cross-References

Zechariah 13:7; Mark 14:27–28

As he goes out with his disciples to the Mount of Olives, Jesus applies the prophetic word about a struck shepherd and scattered sheep to his own death and his disciples’ falling away, and in the same breath promises to go ahead of them into Galilee after he has risen. The scattering was foreseen, and so was the promise that answers it. Mark 16:7 is that promise, repeated after the scattering has actually happened.

Mark 14:3–9

At Bethany Jesus calls a woman’s anointing of his body “a beautiful thing,” says she prepared him “beforehand” for burial, and promises that what she did will be told wherever the gospel is preached. Love for Jesus’ body belongs inside the gospel story. The women who bring spices in 16:1 stand in that same line, and their unused spices are no rebuke to the love that bought them.

1 Corinthians 15:3–5

The gospel Paul passed on “as of first importance” is that Christ died for our sins, “that he was buried,” that he was raised on the third day, and that he appeared to Cephas, that is, Peter, and then to the Twelve. The burial Mark describes so carefully is one of the gospel’s own lines, not a footnote to it. And outside Mark’s story, the name the young man singled out in 16:7 stands first in that list of those who saw the risen Christ. Paul gives no place or detail for that meeting, and Mark leaves it unnarrated.

Romans 8:11

“He who raised Christ from the dead will also give life to your mortal bodies because of his Spirit who lives in you.” The body Joseph wrapped and the women came to anoint was raised, not left behind. Christian hope therefore includes the body, which is why caring for bodies is never wasted motion.

1 Peter 1:3–6

God has given his people “a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead,” and the same passage speaks of that hope while its readers are still grieving under various trials. Resurrection hope reaches people in the middle of grief, just as the announcement at the tomb reached women who had come with spices for the dead and left trembling.

The Shape of a Kingdom People

A people shaped by this passage will know how to stay near death without looking away. The first half of Mark’s account is almost entirely care: women who would not leave, a man who asked for a body and wrapped it, women who came back with spices. None of it could change what had happened, and none of it is treated as pointless. Churches that confess the resurrection of the body should be the last people in town to hurry past a body. They are the ones who sit with the dying, bring food to the house after the funeral, help pay for burials the family cannot afford, and keep coming back after the flowers have been cleared away. The resurrection does not make that care unnecessary. It tells us that the bodies we care for matter to God.

That care has a public edge. Jesus’ body was the body of an executed man, and it stayed in the governor’s control until someone with access asked for it. Joseph used his standing to win dignity for a man the powerful had condemned. Most of us sit on no council, but most of us have some access: a name that opens a door, a profession that can ask questions, a seat at a table where decisions get made about people who have no advocate. The passage does not ask anyone to court danger for its own sake. It does show what standing can be for. And there are still bodies the powerful would rather forget: people who die crossing deserts and seas and are buried without names, people who die in custody, people whose families cannot reach them to bury them. A people who confess the resurrection of the body will want those bodies honored and those names recovered. Like Joseph, they will use whatever access they have to ask for them.

A people shaped by this passage will also have room for failure, and real room—not probation for belonging. The first message out of the empty tomb was addressed to deserters and to a man who had denied Jesus with curses. Nobody was told to grovel first. Mark records no apology from Peter before his name is spoken. A community that has heard “and Peter” should find it very hard to make people earn their names back. Mark tells the failures plainly. But in his account the risen Jesus sets the terms, and those terms begin with being named.

And it will have room for fear. The people who first carried the resurrection news were trembling and bewildered, and for as long as Mark shows them, they were silent. A church that demands calm as proof of faith would have had no place for them, and has no place for most of us on our honest days. Witness that waits until we are fearless will wait forever. The hope here lies elsewhere: Jesus is already ahead of us while we tremble. That frees a church to be patient with the bewildered, to treat the women and men among them as full followers of Jesus without either idealizing or scolding them, and to keep telling the news in voices that are not always steady.

Gospel-Shaped Response

From this passage we can learn to move in a grace-first order. We begin with who God is and what he has done, because our identity is shaped by his character and work, not by our performance. When we forget those truths, unbelief grows in distorted emotions and patterns of life. Faithful response and mission then flow out of trust in the gospel rather than pressure to earn God’s favor.

Who is God?

1 · Character of God

  • God does not leave the crucified one where human hands laid him. He raised “Jesus the Nazarene, who was crucified,” and the cross stays in the risen one’s name.
  • God keeps his word after we have broken ours. The promise of Galilee was spoken before the disciples scattered and repeated after they did, “just as he told you.”
  • God moves first. The first word sent out from the empty tomb goes toward deserters and a denier before any of them has come back.
  • God does not scorn love for bodies. The young man points to “the place where they laid him” without a trace of contempt for the laying.

What has God done?

2 · Work of God

  • In Jesus, God entered real death: a last cry, a centurion’s answer to Pilate, linen, a tomb cut out of rock, a stone rolled against the entrance.
  • He raised the crucified Jesus, so that the place where loving hands laid him now holds only the place.
  • He sent word to “his disciples and Peter,” naming the one who had denied him.
  • He set the risen Jesus on the road ahead of his disciples, toward Galilee, where they had first followed him.

Who are we in light of what God has done?

3 · New Identity

  • We are people Jesus goes ahead of. Our following begins with his movement, not with our readiness.
  • We are named in his message after failure. Whatever our own denial looked like, “and Peter” shows how he speaks to people like us.
  • We are people whose bodies, and whose dead, matter to God, so our grief is not shameful and our care is not foolish.
  • We may be frightened carriers of news that does not depend on our steadiness to be true.

What wrong behavior, harmful emotions, or false attitudes result when we forget these truths?

4 · Unbelief

Grief, fear, and bewilderment are not themselves unbelief; the women at the tomb had all three. The distortions come when we forget what was announced there.

  • We go on tending Jesus as a treasured memory, faithfully visiting the place, with no expectation that he will act or go anywhere ahead of us. The women brought spices before they had heard the news. The distortion is settling there after the announcement has been made.
  • After failing, we decide the message could not possibly have our name in it, and we exclude ourselves before he ever has.
  • We make fearlessness the test of faith. Then we either hide our own fear as something shameful, or treat someone else’s fear as proof that they never believed.
  • Or we let fear harden into a settled silence, as though the command to go and tell had been withdrawn because we trembled.
  • We use resurrection hope to hurry past grief, bodies, or injustice, as though Sunday made the cross and the burial unimportant.

How would we live if we believed these truths?

5 · Faithful Response

  • We would stay with the dying and the grieving and keep doing the physical, practical things love can still do, even when nothing we do can change the outcome.
  • We would treat Jesus’ promise as still in force after our failures, and turn back toward where he said he would meet us.
  • We would bring our fear along with us instead of waiting to become unafraid before we move.
  • If we are the one who denied him, we would hear our own name in the message, and we would make it easier for others to hear theirs.

If we lived these truths by faith, how might they overflow into the real places and relationships God has given us?

6 · Mission

  • We would speak of the risen Jesus as news for people who have failed him, beginning with ourselves, instead of as a reward for those who never did.
  • We would use whatever access we have for the dignity of people whose bodies and lives the powerful treat as disposable: in hospital rooms, in prisons, on the street, and at funerals no one else will pay for.
  • We would carry the news even with unsteady voices, trusting that its truth does not rise or fall with our composure.
  • We would look for Jesus ahead of us in ordinary places of ordinary following, the way his disciples were pointed back to Galilee, where they had first heard his call.

Hearing the Father’s Voice

The Bible is God’s love letter to us. When we read it, we do not want accusation, fear, shame, or distance to distort what God has spoken. In this passage, the Father shows us that the crucified Jesus, laid in the tomb by loving hands, is risen and already going ahead of the very people who failed him, just as he said. Here is what this passage sounds like when the Father says it to you.

My child, I saw the hands that took my Son down from the cross, wrapped him, and laid him in the rock, and I did not despise their love. I know where you have laid the ones you love, and I know the ones you could not reach to bury, whose graves you have never seen. I have not lost track of a single one of them. I know the stones you cannot move. But listen to what was spoken at that tomb: he is not here. The Jesus who was crucified is risen. He did not wait for his friends to be brave, or ready, or even gathered before he set out. He is already going ahead.

My child, if you have run from him, or denied him, or stood trembling with good news you could not bring yourself to say, his word to you has not been withdrawn. He told his disciples where he would meet them before they failed, and he sent that word again after they did, with Peter’s name in it. I do not ask you to manufacture resurrection by your courage. You do not have to become unafraid before you follow him. Come with your fear and your grief. My Son is ahead of you, and what he has promised, he has not taken back.
