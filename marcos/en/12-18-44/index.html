Big Picture: What’s Happening Here?

For days now the temple courts have been a place of examination, and Jesus has been the one examined. Chief priests, teachers of the law and elders wanted to know by what authority he acted. Pharisees and Herodians were sent with a question about taxes, to catch him in his words. Now the Sadducees arrive with a puzzle about a woman married seven times, and a teacher of the law follows with the question every serious reader of Scripture eventually asks: what matters most? Then Mark writes a sentence that changes the room. “From then on no one dared ask him any more questions.” The examination is over, and the one who has been examined begins to say what is true.

What he says does not come as five versions of one lesson. One word corrects. One praises. One is a question left hanging in the air. One is a warning with judgment in it. And one is an act of attention so quiet that only the disciples are called over to hear it. But a single voice speaks through all of them, and Mark wants us to know whose it is. The man who tells the Sadducees they are badly mistaken, who tells a scribe he is not far from the kingdom, and who counts a poor widow’s two coins as more than the gifts of the rich, is the one David called “my Lord.” His judgments carry weight because of who he is. They are true because of where he stands when he speaks: inside the reality of the living God, who is stronger than death, who claims the whole person in love, who will not overlook religion that feeds on the vulnerable, and who sees real cost where the public eye sees almost nothing.

We all carry measures around with us. We picture what may lie beyond death with materials gathered on this side of it. We size people up by their clothes and titles, gifts by their amount, God’s work by the size of its buildings. Most of those measures are not wicked, only too small, though some of them are quietly cruel. In these verses Jesus is not scoring a debate. He is teaching his disciples how God sees. And within a few days the one teaching them will stand before a court that cannot even make its own witnesses agree (14:56–59), and hear it pass sentence on him.

Passage Commentary

A Resurrection Too Small for God

Mark 12:18–27

Mark introduces the Sadducees by the one thing he wants us to know about them: they “say there is no resurrection.” Their question deserves to be heard as a real one. They go to Moses, and to a provision meant to keep a dead man’s line from vanishing: “if a man’s brother dies and leaves a wife but no children, the man must marry the widow and raise up offspring for his brother.” Then they press that provision as far as it will go. Seven brothers, seven marriages, no children, and at last the woman dies too. “At the resurrection whose wife will she be, since the seven were married to her?”

The question rests on an assumption, and Jesus goes straight to it. It pictures resurrection as this world switched back on: the same households, the same claims, the same unfinished obligations, all standing in one room at once. If that is what resurrection means, it does look absurd. But Jesus does not tell them they have thought too hard. He tells them they have not known enough. “Are you not in error because you do not know the Scriptures or the power of God?” They have tried to build God’s future out of the arrangements of the present age, and the future will not fit.

His first answer takes apart their premise. “When the dead rise, they will neither marry nor be given in marriage; they will be like the angels in heaven.” The risen do not turn into angels. They are like the angels in the respect Jesus names: marriage is no longer how their life is arranged. He does not go on to describe the life of the age to come, and he says nothing here about what becomes of the bonds we have known. He says only enough to break the assumption that God must make tomorrow out of today’s materials.

Then he goes beneath the question they asked to the one underneath it. “Now about the dead rising,” he says, and the real issue comes into view. It was never marriage. It was whether God raises the dead at all. Jesus answers from Moses himself, from the account of the burning bush, where God said, “I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob.” In that account God speaks this name as he comes to rescue his people. He ties his own name to three men long buried and offers that bond as the reason his people can trust him to act. If death had simply ended Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, God would be naming himself by relationships he could not keep. “He is not the God of the dead, but of the living.” Death does not cancel what God has bound to himself. The hope of the dead rising rests on the faithfulness and power of the God who will not let his people go.

This is the ground the rest of the passage stands on. Before Jesus says anything about what is valuable, he says what is real. God is larger than the categories in which we try to fit his future, and his power is not limited to what experience has taught us to expect. For anyone who has stood at a grave, that is more than a debating point. Jesus does not hand mourners a floor plan of the age to come. He gives them the name of the God who holds it.

Mark’s readers also know this is not an idle argument. Three times on the road Jesus has told his disciples that he will be killed and after three days rise (8:31; 9:31; 10:34). The question the Sadducees bring as a puzzle will be answered a few chapters later at an open tomb: “He has risen! He is not here” (16:6).

The Teacher Who Is Not Far

Mark 12:28–34

A teacher of the law has been listening, and Mark gives him a detail worth pausing over: he speaks up after noticing “that Jesus had given them a good answer.” He has recognized something true, and what he asks is serious. “Of all the commandments, which is the most important?”

Jesus answers, but he begins before the command. “Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God, the Lord is one.” Love starts with a confession about who God is, and about whose God he is: our God. Because there is one Lord, not a crowd of rival gods dividing a person up among them, the love he asks for cannot be divided either. “Love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind and with all your strength.” The list is not a chart of the compartments inside a person. It is a way of saying nothing is left out.

Then Jesus gives a second commandment nobody asked for. “Love your neighbor as yourself.” The questioner wanted one answer, and Jesus will not give the first without the second. And anyone who knew where that command comes from would know how far it reaches. The same chapter of Leviticus that says “love your neighbor as yourself” goes on to say of the foreigner: “The foreigner residing among you must be treated as your native-born. Love them as yourself, for you were foreigners in Egypt” (Leviticus 19:34). The neighbor God commands us to love has always included the stranger. “There is no commandment greater than these.” Love for the one God does not float free of the people beside us, and love for our neighbors is not left to stand on its own foundation. Hold on to this pairing. The scenes that follow will show what happens to religion when both halves are broken at once.

The teacher’s reply is one of the most generous moments in these chapters. “Well said, teacher,” he says, and he repeats Jesus’ answer in his own words, and then adds something of his own: to love God this way, and to love one’s neighbor as oneself, “is more important than all burnt offerings and sacrifices.” He says it in the temple courts, of all places. He is not sweeping worship aside. He is saying what worship is for, and what no offering can stand in for.

“When Jesus saw that he had answered wisely, he said to him, ‘You are not far from the kingdom of God.’” After the correction of the Sadducees, the next verdict Jesus gives is praise, and it goes to a teacher of the law. The one who names error so plainly is just as ready to name wisdom. His words are real affirmation, and they leave a real distance. Mark does not finish the man’s story. But Mark’s readers heard Jesus announce in the first chapter that “the kingdom of God has come near” (1:15). Now a man who understands the heart of the law is standing in front of the one who made that announcement. How near is not far? Mark leaves the question open, for him and for us.

“And from then on no one dared ask him any more questions.”

The Question About David’s Lord

Mark 12:35–37

Now Jesus asks. He takes up something the teachers of the law say: the Messiah is the son of David. He does not call that false. Mark has already let a blind beggar cry out, “Son of David, have mercy on me!” and receive his sight (10:47–52), and has let a crowd bless “the coming kingdom of our father David” (11:10). The Messiah is David’s son. The question is whether that title is large enough to hold him.

“David himself, speaking by the Holy Spirit, declared: ‘The Lord said to my Lord: “Sit at my right hand until I put your enemies under your feet.”’” Jesus’ question rests on something his hearers would recognize: an ancestor does not address his own descendant as “my Lord.” Yet David, speaking by the Spirit, looks toward the one God seats at his right hand and addresses him that way. “David himself calls him ‘Lord.’ How then can he be his son?” The title is true, but it cannot contain him. The Messiah is David’s son and more than David’s son.

Jesus does not answer his own question aloud. He leaves it standing in the temple courts, and Mark tells us that “the large crowd listened to him with delight.” Delight is not the same as understanding, and it is not yet faith. But Mark’s readers have been given something the crowd lacked. This Gospel opened by naming its subject “Jesus the Messiah, the Son of God” (1:1). We know who is asking.

That is why these three verses carry so much weight. They tell us who has been speaking. The verdicts on either side of it (badly mistaken, not far, more than all the others) are not the opinions of the cleverest interpreter in Jerusalem. They come from the one David called Lord. And notice how the psalm distributes the work. The Lord is invited to sit; God himself puts the enemies under his feet. This Messiah does not seize his place. It is given to him.

Mark lets that right-hand language come back once more, at Jesus’ trial. When the high priest asks Jesus whether he is the Messiah, he answers, “I am,” and speaks of “the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of the Mighty One” (14:62). The council condemns him for it. The one whose judgments are true in these chapters will soon be judged falsely, and he will not argue his way out.

Honor That Devours

Mark 12:38–40

“Watch out for the teachers of the law.” It would be easy to hear that as a blanket warning, and Mark will not let us. A few verses ago Jesus told a teacher of the law that he was not far from the kingdom. The warning is aimed at a particular kind of teacher, and Jesus describes him precisely: men who “like to walk around in flowing robes and be greeted with respect in the marketplaces, and have the most important seats in the synagogues and the places of honor at banquets.” Having just quoted a psalm about a seat that God gives, Jesus turns to men who want the best seats they can get.

None of those things is condemned on its own. Robes are clothing, greetings are courtesy, and someone has to sit at the front. What Jesus names is a pattern: a life arranged around being honored, joined to long prayers made “for a show,” joined to something far worse. “They devour widows’ houses,” he says. Mark does not tell us how. He describes no legal scheme and no financial arrangement, and we do not need one to feel the weight of the charge. Men with the most honor were consuming the houses of people with the least protection, and making long prayers for show all the same.

This is the pattern Israel’s law had warned against from the beginning: “Do not take advantage of the widow or the fatherless. If you do and they cry out to me, I will certainly hear their cry” (Exodus 22:22–23). The verse just before it says, “Do not mistreat or oppress a foreigner, for you were foreigners in Egypt” (Exodus 22:21). The widow, the orphan, and the foreigner are the ones God names again and again, because they are the ones most easily devoured, and because he has promised to hear them. If you are one of them, if someone with more power has been consuming what little you have, Jesus has seen it, and he has named it before God.

Set that beside the answer Jesus gave only moments before. Love for the one God cannot live in a heart that is using God’s name to be admired. Love for the neighbor cannot live in a life that swallows the neighbor’s house. What Jesus exposes here is not a lapse at the edges of religion. It is both great commandments turned inside out, wearing religious clothes while it does it. That is why the sentence falls so heavily: “These men will be punished most severely.”

That judgment belongs to God. It gives no one permission to take vengeance on those they suspect. But it should make anyone who carries responsibility slow and honest. Widows’ houses are still devoured, by rent that swallows an income, by wages withheld from workers who are afraid to complain, by fees charged to families who do not know their rights, sometimes by people who pray in public and sit at the front. The honesty has to begin at home, with our own love of recognition, before it ever reaches a list of other people’s names. The honor that comes with leadership among God’s people is meant to be spent on others. When it is spent on ourselves, and especially when the vulnerable are the ones who pay for it, Jesus does not treat it as a matter of style.

Two Coins Counted as More

Mark 12:41–44

Then Jesus sits down. He takes a place “opposite the place where the offerings were put,” and he watches the crowd giving. For these few moments that is all Mark shows him doing.

“Many rich people threw in large amounts.” Mark does not sneer at them, and neither does Jesus. Their gifts were real, and they were large. Then “a poor widow came and put in two very small copper coins, worth only a few cents.” Of all the gifts Mark mentions, hers is by far the smallest.

Jesus calls his disciples to him. What he is about to say is teaching, and it is meant for them. “Truly I tell you, this poor widow has put more into the treasury than all the others.” By every public count, that cannot be right. It becomes true only when the measure changes. “They all gave out of their wealth; but she, out of her poverty, put in everything—all she had to live on.” Jesus weighs a gift by what it costs the giver, by what is left behind once it is given. The others gave from their wealth, and the wealth remained. She gave what she had to live on.

Mark tells us nothing about what she was thinking. We do not learn whether she gave in gladness or in grief, whether anyone expected it of her, what she ate that evening, or what became of her. Jesus does not tell his disciples to go and do the same, and he does not promise that the coins will come back to her. What he does is count them. Among all those large gifts, he saw the smallest and called it more, and he called his disciples over to learn to see it too. If you are poor, if what you give never shows up in anyone’s count and what you have to live on is almost nothing, this is the Lord who sits down across from you and watches. He sees what it costs you. He does not measure you by the size of the gift.

Mark will not let us look at her in isolation, though. She walks into this scene straight out of Jesus’ words about men who devour widows’ houses, and she walks out of it just before Jesus tells his disciples that not one stone of the temple will be left on another (13:2). Mark does not say that those teachers made her poor. Yet he does not let us forget what surrounds her. The gift Jesus honors goes into a house that stands under judgment, and it comes from a widow, moments after honored teachers have been accused of devouring widows’ houses. Mark sets honor and judgment side by side and does not resolve them, and we can leave them there. Her gift is not made smaller by the failures of the place that received it. The place is not excused by the gifts of the poor.

One more detail is small and hard to forget. As they leave, a disciple says, “Look, Teacher! What massive stones!” (13:1). Jesus has just called them over to look at two tiny coins, and already one of them is pointing to the biggest things in sight. Learning to see as God sees is not finished in an afternoon.

Mark 12:18–44 In One Movement

Questioned about the resurrection, the one being examined begins to say what is true. He starts at the deepest place, with the living God, whose power is not bounded by what we have seen and whose faithfulness does not end at a grave. Everything else he says in these verses comes out of that reality. He tells a thoughtful teacher he is close, and the questions stop. He asks a question that leaves the temple courts holding a Messiah larger than their categories, David’s son whom David calls Lord. He names religious honor that feeds on widows and says God will judge it. Then he sits down, watches, finds two coins in all that giving, and calls his disciples over. The passage opens with a widow who exists only as a case in an argument about the age to come, and it closes with a widow whose actual coins Jesus counts. Between the two, a whole way of measuring the world has been turned over by the one who sees as God sees.

Mark leaves the teacher’s story open, tells us nothing of the widow’s tomorrow, and lets the temple stand for now. And the Lord whose judgments are true walks out toward a court whose judgment of him will be false, and beyond it toward a tomb where the question beneath the Sadducees’ question will be answered in his own person.

Scripture Cross-References

Exodus 3:6

At the burning bush God names himself “the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac and the God of Jacob” as he comes to rescue his people from oppression. He binds his name to men long dead and offers that bond as the ground of his rescue. Jesus reads the name as a pledge death cannot cancel: the God who speaks this way is the God of the living.

Deuteronomy 6:4–5; Leviticus 19:18, 33–34

Jesus joins the confession that the Lord is one, to be loved with the whole self, to the command “love your neighbor as yourself.” The same chapter of Leviticus extends that love to the foreigner: “Love them as yourself, for you were foreigners in Egypt.” It is exactly this pairing, love of God and love of neighbor, that the teachers in 12:38–40 have broken on both sides.

Psalm 110:1

The Lord invites David’s Lord to sit at his right hand while God himself subdues his enemies. The one David addresses as Lord is greater than David, and Mark lets the right-hand language return at Jesus’ trial (14:62), where he is condemned for it.

Exodus 22:21–24

“Do not mistreat or oppress a foreigner… Do not take advantage of the widow or the fatherless. If you do and they cry out to me, I will certainly hear their cry.” Jesus’ severe sentence on those who devour widows’ houses is not a new severity. It stands in the line of the covenant God who promised to hear that cry.

1 Corinthians 15:20–28

Paul calls the risen Christ the firstfruits of those who have died. What Jesus defends in Mark 12:24–27, the dead rising by the power of God, begins in his own rising.

The Shape of a Kingdom People

A community that has listened to Jesus answer the Sadducees can stand at a graveside with real hope and real restraint together. It can say plainly that God is the God of the living and that the dead will rise by his power, without filling in the silences Jesus chose to leave. Grief does not have to be hurried or corrected. People who have lost a husband or a wife, a child or a friend, will ask questions this passage does not answer, and a church shaped by Jesus can let those questions stand while it points, patiently, to the God who holds what we cannot yet see. Our comfort does not come from our pictures of the future. It comes from the name God gave himself at the burning bush.

Such a community also refuses to separate the sanctuary from the street. The teacher of the law saw that loving God and loving the neighbor outrank every offering, and Jesus called that wisdom. That does not make worship unimportant. It means gathered praise and the treatment of people belong to one life. A church can sing well and still ask who among its members is quietly losing a home, who is being taken advantage of, and which widow no one has visited. And it will ask the same questions about its town: whose houses are being devoured, which workers are being cheated, which foreign families are being exploited because they are afraid to speak. Those questions are part of its worship, not a distraction from it. A church that loves God with all its heart and loves its neighbor as itself, foreigner included, will be found standing beside the people who are being consumed.

It will be a place where serious questions are welcome and wisdom is named wherever it shows up. Jesus did not wait for the teacher to join his followers before saying, “You are not far.” People who come with honest questions, including people we might have filed under opponents, should find in the church what that teacher found in Jesus: a real hearing, real praise for what is true, and real honesty about the distance that remains.

Its leaders will hold honor loosely and spend it for others. Jesus’ warning does not abolish teaching offices or public respect, but it does expose what they can become. Leadership in a kingdom people is accountable. It is open about money, suspicious of its own appetite for recognition, and especially careful wherever the vulnerable are involved. When harm comes to light, such a community neither hides it to protect a reputation nor answers it with vengeance. It protects the people who were harmed and entrusts final judgment to God.

And it will learn to value gifts as Jesus did. It will never use the widow to pry open wallets, never shame a large gift or celebrate a small one to make a point, and never treat poverty as a form of holiness. It will not ask the poor to prove their faith by becoming poorer. What it will do is notice what things cost: the offering no one else would record, the service no one thanks, the person whose contribution does not show up on any public count. The disciples were called over to see those things. So are we.

Gospel-Shaped Response

From this passage we can learn to move in a grace-first order. We begin with who God is and what he has done, because our identity is shaped by his character and work, not by our performance. When we forget those truths, unbelief grows in distorted emotions and patterns of life. Faithful response and mission then flow out of trust in the gospel rather than pressure to earn God’s favor.

Who is God?

1 · Character of God

  • God is the God of the living. He named himself by Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, and death did not force him to take the name back.
  • God is powerful beyond our categories. His future is not limited to what our present experience can picture.
  • God is one, and he is our God before we love him. His claim on us is whole, and he joins it to our neighbor’s claim on our love.
  • God hears the cry of those with the least protection and will judge religion that feeds on them.
  • God sees real cost. A large gift does not dazzle him, and a small one does not escape him.

What has God done?

2 · Work of God

  • In Jesus he answered a question about the dead with the promise of his own faithfulness, and then raised the one who gave the answer (16:6).
  • He has promised David’s Lord the seat at his right hand, and he himself undertakes to put every enemy under his feet.
  • In Jesus, who sat down in the temple courts and saw a gift no one else would have counted, he has shown how he sees, and he has called disciples to learn it.
  • In the Son of Man who came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many (10:45), he has shown the opposite of honor that devours.

Who are we in light of what God has done?

3 · New Identity

  • We are people whose future is held by God’s power, not by the limits of our imagination.
  • We are loved by the one Lord who calls himself our God, so our wholehearted love is an answer to him, not an entry fee.
  • We are disciples who have been called over to learn how God sees.
  • We are known by Jesus as we actually are, not by the robes, seats or amounts other people notice.

What wrong behavior, harmful emotions, or false attitudes result when we forget these truths?

4 · Unbelief

  • We build God’s future out of this world’s materials and then find it hard to believe in. Or we let our own pictures of heaven, hopeful or fearful, stand in for the God Jesus names.
  • We love God in the sanctuary and let the neighbor’s house go.
  • We crave the greeting, the seat and the title, and we learn to hide that craving inside long prayers and religious language.
  • We measure people, gifts and ministries by size, and so we admire what Jesus warns against and miss what he sees.
  • We turn Jesus’ severe words about exploitation into ammunition against others without evidence, or we hide behind self-examination when real harm needs to be named.

How would we live if we believed these truths?

5 · Faithful Response

  • We would bring our questions about death to Jesus and let him answer with who God is, even where he leaves particular questions open.
  • We would love the one God with everything we are, and let that love show up in how we treat the person next to us.
  • We would say “well said” when someone speaks wisely, even when we did not expect wisdom from that direction.
  • We would hold whatever honor we are given loosely and spend it for others.
  • We would give freely rather than under pressure, respecting real cost in ourselves and in others, and never making destitution a test of faith.

If we lived these truths by faith, how might they overflow into the real places and relationships God has given us?

6 · Mission

  • We would learn the names of the widows and others in our churches and neighborhoods who could lose a home, and stand with them in practical ways.
  • Those of us who lead would make our handling of money and influence open to others, and would treat the protection of vulnerable people as part of our calling rather than a threat to our reputation.
  • We would bring resurrection hope to grieving friends gently, without inventing details and without rushing their sorrow.
  • We would notice and honor the contributions of people the public count overlooks, at work, in church and in our cities.
  • We would meet honest questioners with the respect Jesus showed the teacher of the law, and tell them how near the kingdom has come.

Hearing the Father’s Voice

The Bible is God’s love letter to us. When we read it, we do not want accusation, fear, shame, or distance to distort what God has spoken. In this passage, the Father shows us that he is the living God, stronger than death, and that in his Son he sees what is real and what things truly cost when no one else is counting. Here is what this passage sounds like when the Father says it to you.

My child, you have learned to measure by what can be seen: who is greeted, who is seated, how large the gift, how strong the walls. I do not measure that way. When my Son sat across from the offering and watched, he was seeing as I see. You do not need a robe for me to notice you, or a seat of honor for me to know your name. If others have taken from you what little you had, I have heard your cry; I promised long ago that I would. Nothing that has cost you something is invisible to me, and no honor worn over another person’s harm deceives me.

My child, I am not the God of the dead. I bound my name to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, and the grave did not undo it. I will not let it undo what I have bound to myself in my Son, who died and was raised. I have not told you everything about the life to come, but I have told you who holds it. When your questions run out, you have not reached the edge of my power.
