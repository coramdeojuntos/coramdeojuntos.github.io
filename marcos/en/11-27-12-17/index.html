The Loved Son and What Belongs to God

Big Picture: What’s Happening Here?

The men who stop Jesus in the temple courts ask the question every institution eventually asks: who signed off on this? The day before, he overturned tables in these same courts. Now he is back, walking there openly, and the chief priests, the teachers of the law and the elders want to know what right he has and who gave it to him. It is a fair thing for them to ask. What is striking is what they receive. A question about heaven. A story about a vineyard. A coin, and a question about whose image it bears. No credential ever changes hands, and by the end the question they brought has turned around and come to rest on them.

Mark lets his readers see more than anyone in the scene is told. We were at the Jordan when heaven was torn open and a voice said, “You are my Son, whom I love” (1:11). Carry that voice into the temple courts. Listen to Jesus ask whether John’s baptism was “from heaven,” refuse to say who authorized him, and then tell of an owner who “had one left to send, a son, whom he loved.” Mark never says the story is Jesus’ answer. He sets the two side by side and lets us weigh them.

The passage is searching because it exposes several different failures without pretending they are one. Some men treat a question about God as a risk to be managed. Some tenants decide that what was lent to them will be theirs once the heir is gone. Some visitors praise Jesus’ honesty in order to trap him, pressing a question about God and Caesar into a single yes or no. Most of us will find ourselves somewhere on that list. Yet the heart of these scenes is the Son the owner loved: sent last into a long refusal, killed and thrown out, and still not defined by the verdict of those who rejected him. “The Lord has done this, and it is marvelous in our eyes.”

Passage Commentary

A Question About Heaven

Mark 11:27–33

Mark gives the leaders two questions, and they are not quite the same. “By what authority are you doing these things?” asks what kind of right Jesus has. “And who gave you authority to do this?” asks who stands behind it. “These things” most naturally reaches back to the tables overturned the day before, and perhaps to the teaching that came with them (11:15–17). The men asking are leaders with standing in that temple. Their question deserves an answer.

Jesus treats it that way. He does not wave it off; he sharpens it. “I will ask you one question. Answer me, and I will tell you by what authority I am doing these things. John’s baptism—was it from heaven, or of human origin? Tell me!” This can look like a debater’s counterpunch, yet it asks about the very thing they raised: where authority comes from. John had preached repentance and pointed past himself to “the one more powerful than I” (1:7). An honest answer about John would have carried them most of the way to the answer they wanted about Jesus. The real question on the table is whether they can recognize heaven when it acts in front of them.

Then Mark lets us overhear them. “If we say, ‘From heaven,’ he will ask, ‘Then why didn’t you believe him?’ But if we say, ‘Of human origin’ …” The sentence breaks off, and Mark finishes the thought: they feared the people, for everyone held that John really was a prophet. Notice what goes into the balance. What Jesus will say to them. What the crowd will think of them. The one thing never weighed is whether John was sent by God.

“We don’t know.” It sounds humble. In context it is a choice, the answer left standing after every true answer has been priced out. “We don’t know” can be a place to hide from what we would rather not admit.

“Neither will I tell you by what authority I am doing these things.” There is a plain fairness in this, and something more. Authority from heaven cannot be certified by men who have just declined to say whether heaven had spoken through John; any credential Jesus produced would go onto the same scales. Nor does Jesus turn away from them. The next words are “Jesus then began to speak to them.” He withholds a statement. He does not withhold himself.

A Vineyard Given, a Harvest Refused

Mark 12:1–5

The parable is spoken “to them,” to the men who have just refused to answer him. Jesus is still addressing his opponents.

It opens with the owner doing all the work. “A man planted a vineyard. He put a wall around it, dug a pit for the winepress and built a watchtower.” Jesus does not stop to decode the wall or the tower, and we need not either; together they tell us that nothing was left undone before anyone else was trusted with the place. Only then does the owner rent it out and move away. Everything the tenants will ever have in this story, they received.

Anyone who knew Isaiah’s song of the Lord’s vineyard would hear it under these opening lines. Jesus changes the song, though. In Isaiah the vineyard itself disappoints: the Lord looked for good grapes and found bad ones. Nothing in Jesus’ story suggests the vines have failed. Harvest time comes. The trouble lies with the people entrusted to tend it.

And anyone who knew Isaiah’s song would also remember what fruit the owner was looking for. Isaiah says it plainly: “he looked for justice, but saw bloodshed; for righteousness, but heard cries of distress” (Isaiah 5:7). The harvest God wants from his vineyard has always been justice and righteousness: the widow cared for, the poor not crushed, the cries of distress answered. When the owner sends for “some of the fruit,” that is the fruit he means. And the people entrusted with the vineyard are the ones with power over it. They are the leaders, the ones who decide what happens to everyone else who lives there.

“At harvest time he sent a servant to the tenants to collect from them some of the fruit of the vineyard.” Some of the fruit. The claim is modest and entirely rightful, a share of a harvest grown in soil the owner planted, behind a wall he built. The tenants seize the servant, beat him and send him away “empty-handed.” The next is struck on the head and treated shamefully. The next is killed. “He sent many others; some of them they beat, others they killed.”

Mark does not make this sequence pleasant. Real messengers bleed in it. Those sent to ask for justice are beaten, sent away empty-handed, killed. That has been the pattern for those who speak for the vulnerable ever since. The long line of servants most naturally recalls the prophets God sent to his people across generations, and Mark has already shown us what can happen to a prophet who tells a ruler an unwelcome truth (6:17–28). But look also at the one doing the sending. By any practical reckoning the owner should have stopped after the first beating, surely after the first death. “He sent many others.” It is almost an unreasonable sentence. It describes a patience that, from the outside, looks like foolishness.

One Left to Send

Mark 12:6–8

“He had one left to send, a son, whom he loved.” The story slows, as if the teller has reached the part that costs him something. The servants are spent. What remains is the one who is the owner’s own. “He sent him last of all, saying, ‘They will respect my son.’”

That hope belongs to the story, and it makes what follows more shocking. It does not mean Jesus was naive about his own road. Three times he has told his disciples that he would be killed and would rise (8:31; 9:31; 10:33–34), and the first time he named the very groups now standing before him: “the elders, the chief priests and the teachers of the law.” The owner’s hope is the measure of how wrong the refusal is.

The son walks into something heavier: a vineyard where the owner’s rightful claim has been refused again and again, and where the only question left is whether the owner’s own son will be honored.

Here the reader of Mark holds something the scene does not state. At John’s baptism a voice from heaven called Jesus “my Son, whom I love” (1:11), and on the mountain a voice from the cloud said it again, adding, “Listen to him!” (9:7). Mark gives no hint that these leaders heard either voice. We have heard both. So when Jesus asks whether John’s baptism came “from heaven,” declines to say where his own authority comes from, and then speaks of an owner’s “son, whom he loved,” the connection is hard to miss. The parable reads like the answer he withheld, though Mark leaves us to see that for ourselves.

The tenants recognize the son at once. “This is the heir. Come, let’s kill him, and the inheritance will be ours.” This is the second time in these scenes that we overhear people reasoning among themselves. The leaders in 11:31 were working out how to avoid a true answer; the tenants in 12:7 are working out how to own what was only lent. These are different wrongs, and the passage keeps them distinct. What they share is a way of reasoning that starts with the self and treats the one who has been sent as a problem to be solved. The story shows where grasping leads: want the vineyard badly enough, and the heir becomes the obstacle.

“So they took him and killed him, and threw him out of the vineyard.” Mark’s order matters. They kill him first, and then they throw his body out, as though even his corpse had no place in what they now call theirs.

The Stone the Builders Rejected

Mark 12:9–12

“What then will the owner of the vineyard do?” Jesus answers his own question: “He will come and kill those tenants and give the vineyard to others.” The owner’s patience was never indifference. The judgment is severe, and the passage does not soften it. But it is the owner’s judgment. Nothing in the story hands it to anyone else to carry out, and whoever draws a license for human violence from this verse has read it backwards.

Notice what survives the judgment: the vineyard. It is “those tenants” who lose their place, and the vineyard goes on in other hands. What the verse makes plain is that the vineyard was never the tenants’ to keep and cannot be kept by grasping. That should slow down anyone eager to cast themselves as the others. The vineyard comes to them as a gift, and whoever receives it holds it on exactly the terms the first tenants refused.

Then Jesus turns to Scripture, and to the men who knew Scripture best. “Haven’t you read this passage of Scripture: ‘The stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone; the Lord has done this, and it is marvelous in our eyes’?” The picture shifts from a vineyard to a building, and the shift is needed, because the vineyard story ended with a dead son thrown out of the vineyard. The psalm carries the story past that point. Builders are the experts; they know which stones will bear weight, and they looked at this one and threw it aside. Their verdict is overruled, and the one who overrules it is the Lord. The Jesus who predicted his rejection by these same groups also said he would “rise again” (8:31), and Mark’s last chapter will say it outright: “He has risen!” (16:6). Rejection does not get to decide who he is.

“It is marvelous in our eyes.” Of all the words spoken in this tense exchange, these are the ones that invite worship. No builder achieved this reversal, and no crowd voted for it. The Lord has done it, and the fitting response is to look and marvel.

The leaders understand exactly. “They knew he had spoken the parable against them.” Understanding a word is a different thing from receiving it. They look for a way to arrest him, a first step toward the tenants’ course, and again fear holds them back: “they were afraid of the crowd; so they left him and went away.” Twice now the crowd’s verdict has governed what they could say and do.

The Coin and What Belongs to God

Mark 12:13–17

“Later they sent some of the Pharisees and Herodians to Jesus to catch him in his words.” The owner in the parable sent servants to collect fruit. Now the leaders do some sending of their own, and what their messengers come to collect is a sentence that can be used against him.

They open with praise, and every word of it is true: “Teacher, we know that you are a man of integrity. You aren’t swayed by others, because you pay no attention to who they are; but you teach the way of God in accordance with the truth.” Mark calls it hypocrisy, and it is: praise used as bait. Yet set beside the two notices of fear that came before it, the flattery has an accidental accuracy. It describes the very freedom the men who sent them had lacked.

The question is built to catch: “Is it right to pay the imperial tax to Caesar or not? Should we pay or shouldn’t we?” The question presses for a single word, and the askers intend to use whichever word he gives.

Jesus “knew their hypocrisy.” “Why are you trying to trap me?” he asks, naming what they are doing. Then he asks them to bring a denarius, and it is they who produce it. “Whose image is this? And whose inscription?” “Caesar’s.”

“Give back to Caesar what is Caesar’s and to God what is God’s.” This is a real command. Caesar is owed something, and Jesus says so plainly. But the sentence does not end with Caesar. Beside Caesar’s claim stands God’s, and the two are not equals: God’s claim is the one every other claim answers to. Jesus lists nothing under either heading, and he offers no system. He refuses the terms of the trap and leaves his hearers holding a question heavier than the one they brought.

Read in Mark’s order, “what is God’s” lands just after a story about an owner whose claim on his own vineyard was refused, whose servants came back empty-handed, and whose son was killed for the inheritance. It is hard to hear the one without remembering the other. A few verses later Jesus will say how far God’s claim reaches: “Love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind and with all your strength” (12:30). And there is a question underneath Jesus’ question that is hard to avoid. The coin is Caesar’s because it bears his image. Whose image do human beings bear? Scripture’s first chapter answers: “God created mankind in his own image” (Genesis 1:27). Jesus does not spell this out, but his sentence points that way. Give the coin back to the one whose face is on it. And give yourself, stamped with the image of God, back to God.

Hear what that means. No Caesar owns a human being. A state may collect its taxes, but it does not own the people who pay them, and it never has the right to treat them as property, as numbers, or as less than human. Every person a government counts, detains, or deports bears the image of God, not the image of Caesar. If you have been treated by the powerful as though you belonged to them, hear Jesus: you belong to God. And if you are comfortable with the powers of your nation, hear the warning in the same sentence: Caesar is owed his coin, never a person’s worth, and never your worship.

“And they were amazed at him.” They came to catch him in his words, and they leave marveling. Mark does not say they were persuaded, or that anyone paid anything. He says only that the trap did not hold the one it was set for.

Mark 11:27–12:17 In One Movement

They came asking who had authorized him, and he answered with heaven. They weighed the cost of the truth and chose not to know; he declined to tell them, and then kept speaking, in a story they understood too well. An owner gave out a vineyard he had prepared, asked for a share of its fruit, and kept sending after his servants were beaten and killed, until he had one left: the son he loved. The tenants saw the heir and wanted the inheritance, so they killed him and threw him out. The judgment belonged to the owner, and the vineyard went on in other hands. The story did not end with the son thrown out of the vineyard, though, because the Lord takes the stone the builders rejected and makes it the one the whole building rests on. Then a coin was brought, and Jesus told them to give Caesar his due without giving him the last word. Every scene hands the leaders’ own question back to them, and to us: who has the right to make a claim on us, and how will we receive the Son?

Scripture Cross-References

Isaiah 5:1–7

Jesus’ opening details recall Isaiah’s song of the Lord’s vineyard, and the song says what fruit God wanted: “he looked for justice, but saw bloodshed; for righteousness, but heard cries of distress.” In Isaiah the vines fail; in Jesus’ story the harvest is there, and the tenants refuse the owner his share. The old song lends the parable God’s grief and names the fruit he was owed.

Psalm 118:22–23

Jesus quotes the rejected stone (Mark 12:10–11) from the same psalm the crowd sang when he entered Jerusalem (11:9). The builders make their judgment, the Lord overturns it, and the psalm calls the result marvelous.

Genesis 1:27

“So God created mankind in his own image, in the image of God he created them.” Caesar’s image is on the coin. God’s image is on every human being. That is why no Caesar can own a person.

Romans 13:7; Acts 5:29

“Give to everyone what you owe them: If you owe taxes, pay taxes.” And when rulers forbade them to speak, the apostles replied, “We must obey God rather than human beings!” Together they mark both edges of Jesus’ sentence: Caesar is truly owed something, and Caesar is never owed everything.

Romans 11:17–21

Paul warns Gentile believers, grafted in like wild branches, “do not consider yourself to be superior to those other branches… the root supports you.” Anyone tempted to hear “give the vineyard to others” as permission for superiority should read it beside this.

The Shape of a Kingdom People

The parable was aimed first at religious leaders, and Christian readers should let it land on themselves before they point it at anyone else. Churches, ministries and the people who lead them tend vineyards they did not plant: congregations, pulpits, buildings, budgets, reputations, the trust of the vulnerable. The tenants’ sentence, “the inheritance will be ours,” is rarely spoken aloud, yet it can be heard whenever those entrusted with God’s people start treating them as possessions, or treat the owner’s messengers as threats to be removed. A people shaped by this passage holds its work as a trust given for the sake of others. When someone comes asking for the fruit, whether with an honest question, an uncomfortable rebuke, or an account of how the vulnerable are being treated, such a people does not send them away empty-handed. And it remembers what fruit the owner wants. A church can have full pews, a healthy budget, and a growing reputation, and still return nothing of what God asked for if the poor, the widow, and the stranger in its town are crying out and no one is answering.

It also becomes a people that asks, before anything else, whether a thing is true. The leaders’ deliberation weighed every consequence and never reached the truth. The alternative is a kind of freedom, and it has a source: people who have heard heaven’s verdict on Jesus do not have to live by the crowd’s. That freedom lets a church be honest in public when honesty costs, set flattery aside as a tool, and refuse to use God’s name to trap or manage anyone.

In public life this passage supplies no party platform, and Christians of good faith will disagree about how its command plays out in their own time and place. What it does supply is a shape. Caesar is really owed something: taxes, honest participation, the ordinary obligations of citizens. Withdrawal is not holiness. But no Caesar, whether state, nation, party, leader, movement or church, may receive what belongs to God alone, and none may claim to speak with God’s total authority. And since every human being bears God’s image, no Caesar may treat people as its property. When a government treats some of the people within its borders as less than human, a kingdom people will say clearly, in public, that those people belong to God, and will stand beside them. A kingdom people will not let a political allegiance become the thing that owns them, and will not treat fellow believers across a political divide as though they had joined the tenants.

Finally, the judgment in this story belongs to the owner. The parable gives no one permission to punish those they consider wicked tenants. It gives Christians no ground for contempt toward Jewish people either; the tenants in view are the leaders who heard the parable that day and knew it was spoken against them, and the vineyard any of us tends came as a gift. Those who follow a Son who was killed and thrown out have no business throwing others out.

Gospel-Shaped Response

From this passage we can learn to move in a grace-first order. We begin with who God is and what he has done, because our identity is shaped by his character and work, not by our performance. When we forget those truths, unbelief grows in distorted emotions and patterns of life. Faithful response and mission then flow out of trust in the gospel rather than pressure to earn God’s favor.

Who is God?

1 · Character of God

  • God gives before he asks. He planted, walled, dug and built before anyone owed him anything, so his claim at harvest is the claim of one who has already given.
  • God is patient past what seems sensible. After beatings and killings he sent again, and then he sent the one he loved.
  • God is a Father who loves his Son, and his verdict on that Son stands when every human verdict goes the other way.
  • God’s claim is ultimate. Caesar’s claim is real, and it answers to God’s.

What has God done?

2 · Work of God

  • He has sent messenger after messenger to people who kept refusing them, and at last sent his Son into the middle of that refusal.
  • His Son stood in the temple courts among men who were looking for a way to arrest him, and kept speaking to them.
  • He allowed the builders to reject the stone, and then did what only he could do: “the Lord has done this.” The Son they killed has risen (16:6).
  • The parable tells the son’s death as the tenants’ crime. Mark has already let us hear the same Jesus say he came “to give his life as a ransom for many” (10:45). The parable does not explain how both are true, and it does not need to.

Who are we in light of what God has done?

3 · New Identity

  • We are stewards of a vineyard we did not plant. Everything we tend was prepared before we arrived.
  • We are people who have heard heaven speak about Jesus, “whom I love” and “Listen to him,” so we need not wait for the crowd to tell us who he is.
  • Our standing rests on what the Lord has done with the rejected stone, not on our skill as builders.
  • We carry Caesar’s coin and owe Caesar his due, yet we belong, all the way down, to God.

What wrong behavior, harmful emotions, or false attitudes result when we forget these truths?

4 · Unbelief

  • We treat questions about God as risks to be managed, pricing an answer before asking whether it is true, and saying “we don’t know” when we would rather not say.
  • We come to regard what God entrusted to us, whether gifts, money, influence or a place in the church, as ours, and resent anyone who asks for fruit.
  • We let the verdict of the crowd, our side or our audience weigh more than God’s.
  • We use flattering words or religious language to manage people and win arguments.
  • We negotiate God’s claim down to a portion we can control, or hand to a nation, party, leader or church the allegiance that belongs only to him.
  • In political anxiety we either demand that Jesus take our side or withdraw from public responsibility altogether.

How would we live if we believed these truths?

5 · Faithful Response

  • We would ask whether a thing is true before asking what it will cost, and say what we know about Jesus even when saying it costs us.
  • We would hold our responsibilities with open hands, glad rather than threatened when someone comes asking for the fruit.
  • We would listen to the Son and give him the respect heaven has already told us he is due.
  • We would pay what we owe to public authority honestly and without resentment, while refusing it our worship.
  • We would offer God the whole of ourselves, heart, soul, mind and strength, in answer to the Son he gave rather than as payment for his favor.

If we lived these truths by faith, how might they overflow into the real places and relationships God has given us?

6 · Mission

  • In churches and ministries we would welcome accountability, listen to those who raise uncomfortable questions, and examine our own use of religious power before criticizing anyone else’s.
  • In workplaces and public roles we would tell the truth under pressure and refuse flattery and manipulation as tools.
  • In public life we would defend the dignity of every person who bears God’s image, especially those the powerful treat as disposable.
  • In civic life we would take part as conscientious citizens, paying, serving and speaking, while refusing to let any government or movement claim what belongs to God, and treating fellow Christians who vote differently as brothers and sisters.
  • Toward those we believe are in the wrong, we would leave judgment with God and refuse retaliation, remembering that the Son we follow was the one thrown out.

Hearing the Father’s Voice

The Bible is God’s love letter to us. When we read it, we do not want accusation, fear, shame, or distance to distort what God has spoken. In this passage, the Father shows us that he gives before he asks, keeps sending when he is refused, and has vindicated the Son he loves when every human verdict went against him. Here is what this passage sounds like when the Father says it to you.

My child, everything you are tending was mine before it was yours, and I gave it gladly. I know how you hold it, some days with open hands and some days with closed ones, and I know how often you have weighed what the truth would cost before you would say it. I did not stop sending to you. I sent my Son, whom I love. Listen to him.

When people look past him or throw him aside, their verdict does not decide who he is, and the crowd’s verdict on you does not decide who you are either. Whatever the powerful have called you, you bear my image, not theirs. No one owns you but me. Give others what you owe them, honestly and without fear. But you are mine, not a portion of you but all of you, and I do not ask for less than your whole heart, because I did not give you less than my Son.
