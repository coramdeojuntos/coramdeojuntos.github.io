The Stronger One Gathers a Family

Big Picture: What’s Happening Here?

Almost everyone in this passage is reacting to the power at work in Jesus. Sick people push through a crowd to touch him, because he has already healed many. Impure spirits drop to the ground in front of him and shout a title that is perfectly true. His family hears the reports and says, “He is out of his mind.” Teachers of the law who have come down from Jerusalem do not deny that demons are leaving people; they decide he must be doing it by the prince of demons. Nobody in these verses doubts that something powerful is happening around Jesus. The question Mark keeps setting in front of us is what that power is, where it comes from, and what it is for.

Jesus answers the question himself, in the middle of the passage, and he answers with a picture: no one plunders a strong man’s house without first tying the strong man up. He does not stop to label the figures, but in the argument he is having there is little doubt who they are. The power that has held people captive is not helping Jesus; it is being overcome by him. He is the stronger one. What is remarkable is where Mark lets that strength arrive. It does not end in a display, and it does not end with Jesus taking control of anyone. It ends in a crowded room, with people sitting around him, and Jesus looking at them and saying, “Here are my mother and my brothers!”

Between the lake and that room there is a mountainside, and on it is the phrase that quietly holds the passage together. Jesus “called to him those he wanted,” and he appointed twelve “that they might be with him” — and then that he might send them out. His first purpose for them is not a task but his company, and the task grows out of it. This passage is full of different ways of being near Jesus: pressing against him, falling at his feet, coming to take charge of him, coming down to pass judgment on him, sitting around him and listening. Mark does not arrange these on a ladder, and he does not turn a person’s place in a room into a verdict on their soul. But he does let us see the difference between getting close to Jesus’ power and being called by Jesus to be with him.

Passage Commentary

Pressing Toward Him, Falling Before Him

Mark 3:7–12

The chapter has just ended one scene with a plot to kill Jesus (3:6). The next thing Mark tells us is that Jesus “withdrew with his disciples to the lake.” He does not go alone, and he does not go far enough to be forgotten. “A large crowd from Galilee followed,” and then people arrive from much farther off — “from Judea, Jerusalem, Idumea, and the regions across the Jordan and around Tyre and Sidon.” Mark gives the reason in one plain clause: they came “when they heard about all he was doing.” News about Jesus has gone out in every direction at once. Look at the map Mark draws. Judea and Jerusalem to the south, Idumea farther still, the lands across the Jordan, and Tyre and Sidon on the coast, beyond Israel’s borders, where foreigners lived. People are crossing every boundary to reach him. And Mark lets us feel the contrast. In the same days that some are planning Jesus’ death, others are walking a long way just to reach him.

Why so many come is no mystery. “He had healed many, so that those with diseases were pushing forward to touch him.” Mark does not sneer at these people. They were in pain, and they were reaching for the one person who had helped others like them; nowhere in the scene does Jesus tell them they came for the wrong reason. Yet Mark is honest about the pressure too. Jesus has his disciples keep a small boat ready “to keep the people from crowding him.” Mercy and limits sit in the same sentence. Jesus does not pretend that a crush of bodies is harmless, and he does not let himself be used up. Mark reports that he had healed many. He does not say that everyone who touched him was cured, and he does not turn what happened by that lake into a promise about every illness since. What he shows us is a Jesus toward whom suffering people could run.

Then Mark turns to a very different kind of nearness. “Whenever the impure spirits saw him, they fell down before him and cried out, ‘You are the Son of God.’” The title is true. Mark’s Gospel opens with it (1:1), and at the Jordan the Father’s own voice spoke it (1:11). The impure spirits are not mistaken; they are simply not on his side. They fall down, but falling down is not trusting. They are subdued, not devoted. And Jesus will not accept their announcement: “he gave them strict orders not to tell others about him.” It is enough to see that correct words about Jesus, spoken by those who oppose him, are not what he is after. Notice, too, that Mark keeps two groups distinct in these few lines, as he does elsewhere: people “with diseases” and “impure spirits” are not the same, and nothing in the passage invites us to read one into the other. Jesus has silenced the spirits who know his name. In the next scene he chooses who will speak for him.

Called to Be With Him

Mark 3:13–19

“Jesus went up on a mountainside and called to him those he wanted, and they came to him.” The initiative is all his. He goes up, he calls, and the ones he calls are the ones he wanted. Their part is to come. Then, from among them, “he appointed twelve.” The number is not incidental. Twelve recalls the twelve tribes of Israel, and Jesus choosing twelve signals God gathering his people again around the one he has sent — a few chosen not for their own sake but so that they could call many others. It is a sign of hope for Israel, not a notice that Israel has been set aside.

Mark gives three purposes for the Twelve, and the first is the most arresting: “that they might be with him.” Before a single sermon, before any authority is handed over, Jesus appoints these men to his own company. Then come the other two: “that he might send them out to preach and to have authority to drive out demons.” Those are not tasks Jesus devised to keep them busy. They are his own work. Earlier Mark summarized Jesus’ ministry in nearly the same terms: he “traveled throughout Galilee, preaching in their synagogues and driving out demons” (1:39). The Twelve are not sent to build something of their own. They are drawn into what he is already doing, and they are drawn in by first being drawn to him. The order is not a timetable, as though companionship were a training course to finish before the real work begins. Being with him is where the sending comes from and where it keeps coming home. When Mark later shows the apostles returning from their mission, they gather “around Jesus” and tell him everything (6:30).

Set this beside the scene by the lake. The impure spirits know exactly who Jesus is, and they are silenced. These twelve will understand little for a long while — Mark is frank about how much they miss — and they are the ones he will send to preach. What qualifies a messenger is not correct information about Jesus. It is being called by him and being with him.

The authority he gives them is real, and it is borrowed. It is authority “to drive out demons” — not authority over people, not a rank above the crowd, and not a power that works apart from him. Later in Mark some of them will try to free a suffering boy and fail, and Jesus will tell them, “This kind can come out only by prayer” (9:29). Later still, when two of them ask for places of honor, he will tell them all that among his people whoever wants to be great “must be your servant” (10:43). Whatever authority flows from Jesus remains his, and it remains shaped like him.

Then come the names. Simon, “to whom he gave the name Peter.” James and John, to whom “he gave the name Boanerges, which means ‘sons of thunder.’” Mark does not explain the name, and we do not need to invent a temperament to go with it. Then Andrew, Philip, Bartholomew, Matthew, Thomas, James son of Alphaeus, Thaddaeus, and Simon the Zealot — two Simons, two Jameses, a pair of brothers — and at the end, “Judas Iscariot, who betrayed him.” Mark tells us the ending at the very beginning, and he offers no motive. Jesus called this man too. The whole list is a plain reminder that being with Jesus began with his call, not their accomplishment. For the disciples who later deserted him — and for Peter, who denied him — that call proved stronger than their failure. Near the end of the Gospel, “everyone deserted him and fled” (14:50), and when a servant girl told Peter, “You also were with that Nazarene, Jesus,” he denied it (14:67–68). Yet the message at the empty tomb is “go, tell his disciples and Peter” (16:7). Their failure did not erase the call Jesus had given them.

No Room to Eat, and a Family Alarmed

Mark 3:20–21

“Then Jesus entered a house, and again a crowd gathered, so that he and his disciples were not even able to eat.” Here is the ordinary texture of life with him: a house full of people and no meal. Mark reports it; he does not hold it up as the measure of devotion. When the same thing happens later — so many people coming and going “that they did not even have a chance to eat” — Jesus says to the apostles, “Come with me by yourselves to a quiet place and get some rest” (6:31). Being with Jesus includes his care for tired bodies. The missed meal is something that happened to them, not a standard they were meant to keep.

“When his family heard about this, they went to take charge of him, for they said, ‘He is out of his mind.’” Those are their words, not Mark’s judgment on Jesus, and we are not invited to speculate about anyone’s mind — his or theirs. Nor does the verse fault a family for noticing that someone they love is not eating. What it shows is concern reaching for control. Faced with a ministry that seems to them to have spilled past reasonable boundaries, they set out to take hold of it and manage it. Mark gives no sign of malice here, and he will not let us treat this the way he treats the accusation that follows. But it is a way of being near Jesus that tries to take charge of him instead of being led by him, and many of us know that impulse from the inside.

How Can Satan Drive Out Satan?

Mark 3:22–27

Then the visitors from Jerusalem speak. “The teachers of the law who came down from Jerusalem said, ‘He is possessed by Beelzebul! By the prince of demons he is driving out demons.’” Notice what they do not dispute. They do not deny that demons are leaving people. They grant the evidence and rename its source. Their charge has two parts — one about Jesus himself, one about the power behind what he does — and together they make the gravest accusation in the passage. Mark is precise about who makes it: these teachers of the law, down from Jerusalem. He is not describing Jewish people in general or religious people in general. Later in the same Gospel another teacher of the law will hear Jesus say to him, “You are not far from the kingdom of God” (12:34).

“So Jesus called them over to him.” On the mountainside he “called to him those he wanted.” Now he calls his accusers. He does not wave them away or answer them from a distance; he brings them near and reasons with them “in parables.” “How can Satan drive out Satan? If a kingdom is divided against itself, that kingdom cannot stand. If a house is divided against itself, that house cannot stand. And if Satan opposes himself and is divided, he cannot stand; his end has come.” This is not a general saying about getting along. It is an argument aimed squarely at their explanation. If Satan were the one expelling Satan’s forces from people, his own rule would be tearing itself apart. Their theory cannot account for what they have seen.

Then the picture: “In fact, no one can enter a strong man’s house without first tying him up. Then he can plunder the strong man’s house.” Jesus does not name the strong man, or the one who ties him up. In the argument he is making, the picture is clear enough: the power that has held people in bondage is not Jesus’ ally. It is being overcome, and Jesus is the one overcoming it. What the teachers of the law called collusion was conquest. And these are not evenly matched rivals trading blows. Jesus is the stronger one, and every person set free in Mark’s story bears witness to it. Isaiah had asked, “Can plunder be taken from warriors, or captives be rescued from the fierce?” and answered, “Yes, captives will be taken from warriors… your children I will save” (Isaiah 49:24–25). If you are held by something you cannot break, a fear, a bondage, a power over your life that is stronger than you, Jesus is stronger than it. He came to plunder that house and set its captives free.

This is Jesus describing his own work, not handing out a technique. The verse gives no formula for tying up Satan, and it gives no permission to see the strong man in the faces of people we disagree with — Jesus, after all, said it to his accusers, having called them close to hear it. His strength is also stranger than we might expect. Before Mark’s Gospel is finished, the one who ties up the strong man will himself be tied: “So they bound Jesus, led him away and handed him over to Pilate” (15:1). His strength sets others free, and in the end he spends it by giving his life for them (10:45).

Every Sin, Every Slander, and One Warning

Mark 3:28–30

His next words are spoken to the same men who have just accused him. “Truly I tell you, people can be forgiven all their sins and every slander they utter.” It is hard to overstate how wide that is. All their sins. Every slander. Not long before, teachers of the law had heard Jesus forgive a paralyzed man and thought, “He’s blaspheming!” (2:7). Now, standing among men who have just slandered him, he says that slander can be forgiven. “Can be” matters — forgiveness is offered, not automatic — but the offer is not limited by the size of the sin.

Then comes the warning, and it must not be softened. “But whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit will never be forgiven; they are guilty of an eternal sin.” These are the gravest words in the passage, spoken by the same Jesus who has just opened mercy so wide. He is not contradicting himself. He is telling the truth about a danger.

And Mark tells us exactly what prompted it. “He said this because they were saying, ‘He has an impure spirit.’” That sentence is the key Mark himself hands us, and it keeps the warning from drifting loose. Mark’s story began with the Holy Spirit coming down on Jesus at his baptism (1:10), and it is that Spirit’s power at work when Jesus frees people. These men were faced with that work — people released from what had held them — and called the power behind it impure. The danger Jesus names is exactly this: looking at the Spirit’s liberating work in Jesus and calling it evil. Mark does not tell us how long a person must persist in it or where precisely the line lies, and it is wiser to leave those questions where he leaves them than to fill them in with confident guesses. What he gives us is the accusation, and Jesus’ answer to it.

Many readers have been frightened by these verses, some of them for years, so it matters what the passage is actually describing. It does not describe a frightened person, a thought that arrives unbidden, a season of doubt, an illness of body or mind, or a sentence spoken in pain. It describes teachers of the law, faced with people being set free, declaring that the one freeing them had an impure spirit. Mark gives no test to run on our own hearts or anyone else’s, and we should not invent one. What he gives us is Jesus, speaking both the breadth and the warning face to face to the very men who stood in danger. The warning is serious, and the mercy is real, and both come from the same mouth. Whatever fear these verses stir belongs in his presence, not at a distance from him.

Here Are My Mother and My Brothers

Mark 3:31–35

“Then Jesus’ mother and brothers arrived. Standing outside, they sent someone in to call him.” Mark picks up the family thread he began in verse 21. They remain outside and send word in. Inside, “a crowd was sitting around him,” and that small detail changes the feel of the passage. By the lake the crowd was pushing toward him; here a crowd is seated around him. The picture has moved from pressure to presence.

The message reaches him: “Your mother and brothers are outside looking for you.” Jesus answers with a question — “Who are my mother and my brothers?” — and then Mark gives us one of the gentlest motions in his Gospel. “He looked at those seated in a circle around him and said, ‘Here are my mother and my brothers!’” He looks before he speaks. All through this passage others have been putting names on Jesus: Son of God, out of his mind, possessed by Beelzebul, one who has an impure spirit. Now Jesus is the one naming, as he named Simon and the sons of Zebedee on the mountain, and the name he gives the people around him is family.

Then he widens it. “Whoever does God’s will is my brother and sister and mother.” Sister has entered the sentence, though no one mentioned sisters at the door, and “whoever” opens it to anyone. This is not a door closing on Mary and her sons; the word “whoever” does not shut them out but makes room for them. Nor is Jesus teaching contempt for family. Elsewhere in Mark he rebukes a tradition that let people declare what might have helped their parents “devoted to God” and then do nothing for them, and he holds to the command, “Honor your father and mother” (7:9–13). What he refuses here is to let even the closest natural claim take charge of his mission. What he creates is a family that does not rest on blood: people joined to him, and to one another, by the will of God.

Hear what that means for anyone whose family is far away. If you have left your mother on the other side of a border, if your children are growing up in a country you cannot reach, if you have been separated from the people who should be at your table, Jesus looks at the people seated around him and says, “Here are my mother and my brothers!” His family is meant to be yours. And it is meant to be a real family: people who show up, share food, stand with you in court, and keep your birthdays.

The phrase “does God’s will” is not decoration. Jesus means real obedience, lived out, and this passage offers no belonging that leaves people as they were. Across this passage, Jesus’ initiative comes first: he calls before the Twelve come, appoints before they preach, and announces forgiveness even to men who are slandering him. So when he describes his family by what they do, obedience is not payment for admission. It is the family resemblance of people living under his call and mercy. And the one who says this will live it first, at a cost none of them could imagine, praying in Gethsemane, “Yet not what I will, but what you will” (14:36).

Mark 3:7–35 In One Movement

From a lakeshore to a mountainside to a house with no room to eat, Mark lets us watch the stronger one at work and shows us what his strength is for. It draws suffering people toward him and brings hostile spirits down at his feet, where he silences them. It calls the people he wants, first to be with him, and then sends them into his own work of preaching and setting people free, with an authority that is never theirs apart from him. It is misread from two directions — by a family that sets out to take charge of him, and by teachers of the law who call the Spirit’s work in him impure — and Jesus answers the gravest misreading with the truth: Satan is not empowering him; the strong man is being overcome. Even then, speaking to his accusers, he opens forgiveness wider than they could have imagined and warns them honestly of the one danger that stands against it. And then, among people seated around him, he looks at them and calls them his mother and his brothers, and widens the circle to anyone who does God’s will. Mark does not tell us that the strong man’s house and this circle are the same picture. He sets them in one passage so that we see both at once: Jesus is strong enough to overcome what enslaves, and he uses that strength not to seize people but to call them near.

Scripture Cross-References

Mark 1:9–11

At the Jordan the Spirit descends on Jesus, and a voice from heaven says, “You are my Son, whom I love; with you I am well pleased.” The title the impure spirits shout in 3:11 was first the Father’s word of love, and the Spirit the teachers of the law call impure in 3:30 is the Spirit who came down on the beloved Son.

Isaiah 49:24–25

“Can plunder be taken from warriors, or captives be rescued from the fierce? But this is what the LORD says: ‘Yes, captives will be taken from warriors.’” The strong-man saying stands in the line of this promise. The God who rescues captives from the fierce has come in Jesus to do it.

Genesis 3:15

“He will crush your head, and you will strike his heel.” Right after humanity’s first rebellion, God promises that evil will be overcome by one who is himself wounded in the struggle. The strong-man saying belongs to that long hope.

Hebrews 2:11

“Both the one who makes people holy and those who are made holy are of the same family. So Jesus is not ashamed to call them brothers and sisters.” Mark 3:34 shows that happening in a room, with Jesus looking around at the people seated near him.

Romans 8:15–16

“The Spirit you received does not make you slaves, so that you live in fear again; rather, the Spirit you received brought about your adoption to sonship. And by him we cry, ‘Abba, Father.’” The Spirit whose work in Jesus the teachers slandered is the Spirit who brings people into God’s family as children, not slaves.

The Shape of a Kingdom People

A people shaped by this passage will begin where Jesus began with the Twelve: with him. Churches are good at sending, and they should be; the Twelve were appointed to preach and to set people free. But Mark puts “that they might be with him” first, and a community that forgets it will slowly start measuring people by what they produce. Being with Jesus — in prayer, in Scripture, in worship, in the ordinary company of his people — is not the warm-up for real ministry. It is the relationship real ministry comes from and returns to. That also means taking bodies seriously. Jesus kept a boat ready against the crush, and later he told weary apostles to come away and rest. A church that praises exhaustion as proof of faithfulness has taken the wrong lesson from “not even able to eat.”

It will also be a people careful with authority. Jesus shared real authority with the Twelve, and it was authority over demons, not over people — authority that could fail, that depended on prayer, and that Jesus defined, when they began to jockey for position, as service. No pastor, elder, or movement inherits Jesus’ own authority by holding an office. In this passage the reflex to take charge belongs to those who misunderstood him. A community that follows Jesus will make it safe to question its leaders, will never treat disagreement with its leaders as rebellion against God simply because those leaders hold office, and will watch for the moment when care begins to turn into control.

It will be a people who speak carefully about evil and about those who oppose them. Jesus called his accusers over and reasoned with them. He named no human being as the strong man, and neither should we — not political opponents, not other churches, not the neighbor who thinks we are wrong. A church shaped by these verses will not treat illness, disability, or mental distress as signs of a spirit; Mark keeps disease and impure spirits distinct, and so should we, gladly receiving ordinary medicine and counsel alongside prayer. It will make room for people who come simply because they are hurting, without looking down on them as “the crowd.” And it will handle the warning about the Holy Spirit the way Mark handles it: never as a threat to silence questions, never as a label for outsiders, always tied to the accusation Mark says prompted it, and always spoken beside the breadth of forgiveness Jesus announced first.

And it will be a family. “Here are my mother and my brothers” is one of the kindest things Jesus ever says about his people, and a church should feel like that sentence: brothers, sisters, and mothers who share life and share obedience to God’s will. For people whose families are far away, absent, or unsafe, that kinship is a real gift and not a consolation prize. In churches full of immigrants, refugees, and people separated from their families by borders, “Here are my mother and my brothers” is not a metaphor. It is a description of who will sit with them at Christmas, who will pick up the children from school, and who will be in the waiting room. But it is a gift, not a takeover. Jesus taught no contempt for mothers and brothers, and he held people to honoring their parents. A church that urges people to cut off safe relatives, sets leaders up as substitute parents, or demands loyalty in the name of “family” has turned his words upside down. The circle around Jesus is held together by the will of God, freely done, and the word over its door is “whoever.”

Gospel-Shaped Response

From this passage we can learn to move in a grace-first order. We begin with who God is and what he has done, because our identity is shaped by his character and work, not by our performance. When we forget those truths, unbelief grows in distorted emotions and patterns of life. Faithful response and mission then flow out of trust in the gospel rather than pressure to earn God’s favor.

Who is God?

1 · Character of God

  • God is stronger than every power that holds people captive, and his strength sets people free rather than taking control of them.
  • God wants people near him. On the mountainside Jesus “called to him those he wanted,” and the first thing he wanted was that they be with him.
  • God’s mercy is wide — “all their sins and every slander.” He is also truthful, and he warns plainly about calling his Spirit’s good work evil.
  • God gathers a family, and he is the one who says who belongs to it.

What has God done?

2 · Work of God

  • In Jesus the stronger one has come; impure spirits fall before him, and the hold of evil on people is being broken.
  • He called ordinary, named people — Judas among them — to be with him, and sent them into his own work of preaching and setting people free.
  • He announced forgiveness to men who were slandering him, and he told them the truth to their faces.
  • He looked at the people seated around him, called them his mother and brothers, and opened that family to whoever does God’s will.

Who are we in light of what God has done?

3 · New Identity

  • We are wanted before we are useful. The first thing Jesus gave the Twelve was his company.
  • We are sent people, carrying a message that belongs to Jesus and serving under his authority rather than our own.
  • We are people to whom Jesus announces forgiveness for “all their sins and every slander,” and we receive it from his hand rather than from our own estimate of ourselves.
  • We are brothers and sisters and mothers to one another in his family, and we remain sons and daughters who honor the families God first gave us.

What wrong behavior, harmful emotions, or false attitudes result when we forget these truths?

4 · Unbelief

  • We trade being with Jesus for being useful to him, and begin measuring our worth — and other people’s — by output, stamina, or visible results.
  • We try to take charge of Jesus, fitting him into what seems reasonable to us instead of letting him lead.
  • We look at God’s work of setting people free and explain it away, especially when it unsettles our standing or our categories.
  • We turn borrowed authority into control over others, or treat people who oppose us as enemies to be labeled rather than people to be called near.
  • We treat belonging to his family either as something to be earned or as something that asks nothing of us.

How would we live if we believed these truths?

5 · Faithful Response

  • We would come when he calls and stay near him, making room in ordinary days for prayer, Scripture, worship, and his people — not as a warm-up for service but as life with him.
  • We would go where he sends, speaking of him as people who have been with him and relying on his authority rather than the force of our personalities.
  • We would take Jesus’ words about forgiveness as spoken to us, and if the warning in these verses troubles us, we would bring that trouble to him and to his word rather than to tests of our own making.
  • We would do God’s will together, gladly and imperfectly, as a family resemblance rather than a payment.
  • We would accept our limits — food, sleep, rest — as part of following a Lord who kept a boat ready and called his apostles away to rest.

If we lived these truths by faith, how might they overflow into the real places and relationships God has given us?

6 · Mission

  • We would make room for people who come to Jesus because they are hurting, neither despising them as “the crowd” nor promising them outcomes he has not promised.
  • We would speak about Jesus as people who have been with him, carrying good news rather than borrowed volume.
  • We would refuse to demonize those who oppose us, and follow Jesus in calling people near to hear the truth.
  • We would offer real kinship to the lonely, the estranged, and the overlooked, while encouraging honor and care toward parents and safe relatives.
  • We would care for bodies and minds with mercy, welcoming ordinary medical and mental-health care, without spiritualizing illness.

Hearing the Father’s Voice

The Bible is God’s love letter to us. When we read it, we do not want accusation, fear, shame, or distance to distort what God has spoken. In this passage, the Father shows us that his Son is stronger than the evil that holds people captive, and that he uses that strength to call people to himself and gather them as family. Here is what this passage sounds like when the Father says it to you.

My child, I sent my Son into a world where people were held by what they could not break, and he is stronger than all of it. He did not come to seize you or to manage you. He came to call you to himself, the way he called those he wanted on the mountain, and the first thing he wants for you is that you would be with him. Whatever he sends you to do will come from being near him, and it will be his work, not a weight you were meant to carry alone. When you are worn out, he still says, come away and rest.

My child, hear how wide my Son’s forgiveness is: all sins, every slander. Do not carry your fears away from him; bring them to him. And if your family is far from you, if borders or distance or death have emptied the chairs at your table, look at the room where he sat and said, “Here are my mother and my brothers!” I have given you a family in him. You are not alone in this land. He gathers his family close to himself, not at arm’s length, and you are in that circle.
