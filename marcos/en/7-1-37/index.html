Big Picture: What’s Happening Here?

Mark 7 is crowded with words. There are words handed down by the elders about how to wash before eating. There is a word from Isaiah and a word from Moses. There is a single religious word, Corban, spoken over money, that leaves a father and mother without help. There is Jesus calling a whole crowd to listen, disciples asking what he meant, a hard saying in a house near Tyre and a mother’s answer to it, and a word spoken close to a man who cannot hear: Ephphatha, “Be opened!” By the end, a crowd that has been told to keep quiet cannot stop talking. Hear the chapter that way and a question surfaces beneath all of it: whose word actually governs a life?

The chapter opens with Jesus’ disciples measured against “the tradition of the elders” and closes with people saying of Jesus, “He has done everything well.” In between, Jesus does not argue as a man who has left Israel’s Scriptures behind. He argues from them. His complaint is not that his opponents are religious, or that they keep inherited customs, but that they have found a way to honor God with their lips while setting aside what God actually said. Then he goes further than any washing could go. What defiles a person, he says, rises from within, from the heart, and the list he gives spares no one who reads it, including his own disciples, who need the whole thing explained.

Then the chapter travels, and mercy travels with it. A Greek woman near Tyre, a foreigner with no claim on Israel’s teacher, falls at his feet for her little daughter and goes home to find her child free. A man in the Decapolis who is deaf and can hardly talk is brought by others, drawn aside, touched, and opened. The people who thought they were clean are told the truth about their hearts. The people everyone assumed were outside find Jesus coming toward them. Mark is not only showing us what Jesus can do. He is asking whether Jesus’ word has been received, whether we have truly heard it, or have only learned to say true things about him.

Passage Commentary

Washed Hands and a Distant Heart

Mark 7:1–8

The Pharisees and “some of the teachers of the law who had come from Jerusalem” gather around Jesus. These are serious people giving serious attention to a teacher everyone is talking about, and what they notice is a small thing: some of his disciples eating “with hands that were defiled, that is, unwashed.” Mark stops to explain the custom. This is ceremonial washing, “holding to the tradition of the elders,” extended to hands after the marketplace and to “cups, pitchers and kettles.” Nobody here is arguing about germs. The question is religious: what does it take to come to an ordinary meal in a way that honors a holy God?

Their question sounds reasonable enough. “Why don’t your disciples live according to the tradition of the elders instead of eating their food with defiled hands?” Jesus’ answer is not polite, and it does not begin with his own words. “Isaiah was right when he prophesied about you hypocrites.” Then he quotes Israel’s prophet: “These people honor me with their lips, but their hearts are far from me. They worship me in vain; their teachings are merely human rules.” The sharpest words in this exchange come from inside Israel’s own Scriptures. Jesus is not standing outside that tradition throwing stones. He is standing in the middle of it, reading Israel’s prophet to people who knew him well, and the charge has lost none of its edge for churches since.

Notice what the heart is in Isaiah’s line. It is not a warm center of sincerity that makes everything else acceptable. It is the person at the core, the place of loving and wanting and choosing, and it can be far from God while the lips keep speaking correctly. That is the unsettling thing. The lips are saying the right things. What Isaiah and Jesus both expose is distance: the words have become a place to stand instead of a way toward God.

Then comes the sentence the whole controversy turns on: “You have let go of the commands of God and are holding on to human traditions.” The argument began with what was on the disciples’ hands. Jesus turns it to what is in theirs. Every religious community holds something; the question is what it has let go of in order to hold it. Mark will later show us a teacher of the law to whom Jesus says, “You are not far from the kingdom of God” (12:34), so this is not a verdict on a whole class of people. It is a verdict on a pattern, and the pattern is at home in churches too. Communities cannot live without customs, and many are wise. But any of them can quietly trade places with the word it was meant to serve.

A Gift to God That Closed a Hand

Mark 7:9–13

Jesus now gives an example, and it is not about washing. “You have a fine way of setting aside the commands of God in order to observe your own traditions!” The irony is heavy. Fine is the one thing this way is not.

“For Moses said, ‘Honor your father and mother,’ and, ‘Anyone who curses their father or mother is to be put to death.’” Jesus reaches back to the law God gave Israel, to a command about the most ordinary obligation there is, and the severe line beside it shows how much weight Moses gave to the way children treat the people who gave them life. Then comes the contrast the whole chapter is built around: “Moses said … But you say.” Two words stand side by side. One is God’s. The other is a ruling that has grown up beside it.

The ruling concerns a declaration. If anyone declares that what might have helped their father or mother is Corban, Mark stops to translate, “that is, devoted to God,” “then you no longer let them do anything for their father or mother.” Someone had something that could have helped their parents. They spoke a holy word over it. And the word worked. The help was closed off, and the teachers held it closed: you no longer let them. What makes the example so searching is that it sounds like devotion. No one is robbing anyone or cursing a parent. Something has been given to God, and in the giving a person has found a sacred reason not to give it where God said it belonged.

“Thus you nullify the word of God by your tradition that you have handed down.” To nullify a word is not to deny it. The command to honor parents stayed in force on paper. People could still recite it. It simply stopped governing anything, because another word had been placed between it and life. That is the danger Jesus names: religious devotion becoming a shield against costly love. It is always easier to give to God in a way that costs us nothing toward the people in front of us. And it is always the vulnerable, the aging parent, the neighbor without means, who pay for that kind of devotion.

Jesus ends with a small sentence that reaches a long way: “And you do many things like that.” He does not list them. He lets us look.

What Rises From Within

Mark 7:14–23

“Again Jesus called the crowd to him and said, ‘Listen to me, everyone, and understand this.’” The dispute with the visitors from Jerusalem becomes an announcement for everyone. What he is about to say is not a technicality for experts. It is the truth about being human, and he wants all of them to hear it. “Nothing outside a person can defile them by going into them. Rather, it is what comes out of a person that defiles them.”

Then he goes indoors, and his disciples ask him “about this parable.” They have been with him through storms and feedings and still need help, and Jesus does not hide his frustration: “Are you so dull?” But he answers. His sharpness is not a door closing; it is a teacher refusing to let his students settle for less than understanding. Food “doesn’t go into their heart but into their stomach, and then out of the body.” What passes through the body never reaches the place where a person loves, chooses and wants.

Mark adds a note of his own: “In saying this, Jesus declared all foods clean.” The note is striking, because the law God gave Israel had itself distinguished clean from unclean foods. This is not a man who has just defended Moses turning around to treat Moses as a mistake. A declaration carries only as much weight as the right of the one who makes it. The elders’ tradition could add to God’s law; it could not speak for it. Mark presents Jesus as one who can.

Then Jesus says what does defile. “For it is from within, out of a person’s heart, that evil thoughts come—sexual immorality, theft, murder, adultery, greed, malice, deceit, lewdness, envy, slander, arrogance and folly. All these evils come from inside and defile a person.” Take the list whole, because it refuses to be used as a weapon. It holds the sins a court would prosecute and the sins polite company barely notices: murder beside greed, adultery beside envy, theft beside a quiet lie about a colleague, and at the end arrogance and plain folly. No reader gets to hand it to someone else.

And notice what is not on it. Bodies are not on it. Illness is not on it. Disability is not on it. Ancestry is not on it. Where you were born, what language you speak, what food is on your plate, whose people you come from: none of it makes anyone unclean before God. Anyone who has been treated as dirty or dangerous because of who they are can hear this as Jesus’ own verdict. What defiles is moral evil, and it comes from the same heart Isaiah said could be far from God, the heart in every one of us.

This is the hardest news in the chapter, and it undoes every strategy for fixing ourselves from the outside. If the problem came in through the hands, washing could deal with it. If it came from the wrong food or the wrong company, avoidance could deal with it. But Jesus locates it at the source, and a source cannot be cleaned by rinsing what flows out of it. Even sincerity will not do, because sincerity rises from the same place. The disciples in that house are not being told to try harder at having better hearts. They are being told the truth about a problem only God can reach. Mark will not leave that problem hanging, but for now he lets it stand.

Crumbs Under the Table

Mark 7:24–30

“Jesus left that place and went to the vicinity of Tyre. He entered a house and did not want anyone to know it; yet he could not keep his presence secret.” Word about Jesus had reached people from around Tyre and Sidon earlier (3:8). Now it reaches one particular woman.

“In fact, as soon as she heard about him, a woman whose little daughter was possessed by an impure spirit came and fell at his feet.” Mark gives her no name, but what he gives he gives carefully: “The woman was a Greek, born in Syrian Phoenicia.” She is a foreigner, a woman, and a mother with a child in bondage, every one of which put her at the edge of anyone’s idea of who belonged near Israel’s teacher. She had no standing to claim and no one to speak for her. Mark does not describe her feelings. He describes what she does. She comes, she falls at his feet, and then: “She begged Jesus to drive the demon out of her daughter.” An impure spirit had her girl, and the mother could not break it.

Then Jesus answers, and his answer is hard. “First let the children eat all they want,” he told her, “for it is not right to take the children’s bread and toss it to the dogs.” There is no honest way to make this sound soft. It is a saying about a household table where the children are fed first and the bread is theirs, and it uses a word, dogs, that no one wants spoken over them. In this setting it is hard to hear “the children” without thinking of Israel, to whom God’s promises had come first. Jesus does not treat God’s dealings with Israel as a mistake. We have what he said, and we have what happened next.

What happened next was her reply. “Lord,” she replied, “even the dogs under the table eat the children’s crumbs.” Look at what she does with his words. She calls him Lord. She does not dispute first. She does not ask for the children to go hungry. She steps inside his own picture and finds a place in it, under the table, where crumbs fall, and asks only for what the table can spare. In Mark’s story that is not a foolish hope. Twice in these chapters, crowds eat bread Jesus has broken until they are satisfied, and broken pieces are gathered afterward (6:42–43; 8:8). She believes there is so much mercy at his table that a crumb of it is enough for her daughter.

“For such a reply, you may go; the demon has left your daughter.” Jesus himself honors her answer. What he commends is not cleverness used as a lever. It is faith that takes his word seriously, stays within it, and trusts that there is mercy enough at his table even for her. But the reply does not do the delivering. Jesus announces that the demon has left, without going to the house, seeing the girl or touching her. “She went home and found her child lying on the bed, and the demon gone.” Distance did not limit him. The order of first was not erased, and in this encounter it did not become a locked door.

Stay with where this story ends, because the last word spoken over this foreign mother was not the hard one. It was “the demon has left your daughter.” If you have had words spoken over you that no one should hear, if you have been made to feel like someone with no right to the table, notice that this woman came anyway, and that Jesus sent her home with her child free. And if you have ever been tempted to think mercy is scarce and must be guarded from outsiders, notice how much there was at his table: enough for the children, and enough for her.

“Be opened!”

Mark 7:31–37

“Then Jesus left the vicinity of Tyre and went through Sidon, down to the Sea of Galilee and into the region of the Decapolis.” This is the region where, some chapters earlier, a man Jesus had freed went about telling “how much Jesus had done for him” (5:20). Now “some people brought to him a man who was deaf and could hardly talk, and they begged Jesus to place his hand on him.” Mark names no spirit here and attaches no fault. The man cannot hear, and he can hardly speak.

What Jesus does next is strange, and it should stay strange. “After he took him aside, away from the crowd, Jesus put his fingers into the man’s ears. Then he spit and touched the man’s tongue.” Taking him aside spares him being a spectacle, and the actions that follow are ones a man who cannot hear could feel and see. Fingers in his ears. A touch on his tongue. Then: “He looked up to heaven and with a deep sigh said to him, ‘Ephphatha!’ (which means ‘Be opened!’).” None of this is a technique. In the previous scene Jesus freed a child he never saw; here he puts his own fingers in a man’s ears. The same Lord uses entirely different means, which tells us the power was never in the method. And the deep sigh tells us the one who heals is not detached from the one he is healing.

This is the second time in the chapter Mark has stopped to translate a word for us. The first was Corban, “devoted to God,” a religious word spoken over money that closed a hand to a father and mother. This one is spoken to a man. “At this, the man’s ears were opened, his tongue was loosened and he began to speak plainly.” In a chapter about whose word governs, the two stand side by side. One closed a hand in God’s name. The other, spoken by God’s Son, opened a man’s ears and loosened his tongue.

The healing is good. Ears that hear and a tongue that speaks plainly are gifts, and Jesus gave them fully. Bodies matter to him; he would not have put his fingers where he did otherwise. But this man was not less a person before his ears opened, and his worth did not begin in that moment. The people who brought him already counted him worth bringing. And the crowd’s words sound very like Israel’s old hope that one day the ears of the deaf would be unstopped, a sign of what God has come to do.

“Jesus commanded them not to tell anyone. But the more he did so, the more they kept talking about it.” Mark has already shown what followed when a healed man spread the news against Jesus’ explicit word: Jesus “could no longer enter a town openly” (1:45). Jesus decides when he is to be announced. Their talk overran his word.

And yet what they said was true. “People were overwhelmed with amazement. ‘He has done everything well,’ they said. ‘He even makes the deaf hear and the mute speak.’” It is hard to improve on that sentence. Their wonder is real, and what they say stands. But the chapter that began with lips honoring God while his command was set aside ends with lips praising Jesus while his command is set aside. Mark lets us notice what the whole chapter has been teaching: saying true things about Jesus, even glad and astonished things, is not yet the same as letting his word govern us.

Mark 7:1–37 In One Movement

Everyone in Mark 7 is speaking, and the chapter keeps asking which word will rule. Human words can honor God and still set his command aside; they can even call themselves devotion. Beneath them, Jesus says, lies a heart that no washing reaches, because the evil comes from there. Against all of it stands Jesus’ own word. It holds God’s command in place when a pious ruling tries to slip out from under it. It tells the truth about what is within us and refuses to call anyone unclean for their body or their birth. It keeps the order of first and still sends a foreign mother home to a free daughter. It comes, in Jesus’ own hands, close enough to touch a man’s ears before telling them to open.

And Mark knows that the problem Jesus named in the heart is not solved by teaching alone. Nothing that enters us from outside can defile us, Jesus said; but it will take something from beyond us to make us clean. The same Jesus who said “Be opened!” would give his life “as a ransom for many” (10:45), and would himself cry out a word Mark again translates for us: “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” (15:34). The one who opened others was not spared, so that what rises from within us could be met by a love that did not come from us at all. The crowd was right: he has done everything well. To receive him is to let his word govern us.

Scripture Cross-References

Exodus 19:4; 20:12

Before God gave the command to honor father and mother, he reminded Israel that he had carried them “on eagles’ wings” and brought them to himself. The commands came to people already rescued. Corban managed God’s word instead of living inside it, and Jesus’ defense of Moses is a defense of the order grace itself set.

Zechariah 3:1–9

Joshua the high priest stands before the angel of the Lord in filthy clothes, and the remedy is not a washing he performs. The angel commands that his filthy clothes be taken off and tells him, “See, I have taken away your sin, and I will put fine garments on you.” Then the Lord promises, “I will remove the sin of this land in a single day.” Mark 7 locates our defilement where no washing can reach; Zechariah shows the cleansing only God provides.

Isaiah 35:4–6

Isaiah tells people with fearful hearts that their God will come to save them, and then pictures that day: “Then will the eyes of the blind be opened and the ears of the deaf unstopped. Then will the lame leap like a deer, and the mute tongue shout for joy.” The crowd’s words in the Decapolis sound like that hope arriving.

Romans 1:16

The gospel is God’s power for salvation “first to the Jew, then to the Gentile.” Both of Mark 7’s words are there, first and the nations. The Tyre encounter holds that order and that mercy together, and leaves no room for pride at the table.

James 1:27

The religion God accepts is “to look after orphans and widows in their distress.” Set beside Corban, it names what pure devotion actually looks like: not a holy word that closes a hand, but care for the vulnerable.

The Shape of a Kingdom People

A church shaped by this chapter will be grateful for its inherited practices and unafraid to test them. No community lives without customs: ways of praying together, ordering a week, giving, keeping feasts, raising children in the faith. Many of them carry the wisdom of people who loved God before us. But Mark 7 teaches us to ask a sharper question than How old is this? The question is What is this being used to avoid? Jesus’ remark, “And you do many things like that,” is an invitation for every congregation to look for its own Corban: the budget that is generous to programs and closed to the neighbor in need, the ministry calendar with no room for an aging parent, the devotion that has quietly become a reason not to visit, call, help, or speak up. The test is not whether a practice is ours but whether God’s word still governs it.

Such a people will let Jesus’ whole list search them, together. It is always tempting for a community to linger on the sins it already condemns in others and hurry past greed, envy, slander, arrogance and folly, which are so often at home in religious circles. A kingdom people reads the list whole and stands under it together. And they will refuse to call unclean what Jesus did not: bodies, illness, disability, ancestry, nationality, the food on someone else’s plate. In a world quick to call whole groups of people dirty, dangerous, or less than human, a church that has heard Mark 7 cannot speak that way, and cannot stay silent when others do.

The Tyre encounter asks something particular of those of us who came to Israel’s God from the nations. We are guests at a table whose promises came to Israel first, and the right posture for a guest is gratitude, not superiority. We are not clean insiders dispensing charity from above; we are people who were once outside and have been fed. That changes how such a people welcomes. Their tables become places of mercy for people who do not know whether there is room for them: the foreign family, the newcomer with the accent, the mother who has come a long way for the sake of her child. A church like that becomes a sign to its neighbors of the kind of table Jesus keeps, one where mercy is not scarce and does not need to be guarded from outsiders.

The last scene shapes how a kingdom people cares for bodies. Some unnamed people brought a man to Jesus and begged on his behalf. Communities can do that for one another, bringing people toward Jesus without turning them into projects or exhibits. Jesus took the man aside and reached him through touch and sight; a church can learn from that a care that pays attention to how a person actually receives, and that has no need of an audience. Such a church will rejoice in healing when God gives it, without treating Deaf or disabled members as illustrations or as people waiting to become full members once they are fixed. They already belong. And when it speaks of Jesus in public, as it must, it will want its words to be true and its life to answer to him.

Gospel-Shaped Response

From this passage we can learn to move in a grace-first order. We begin with who God is and what he has done, because our identity is shaped by his character and work, not by our performance. When we forget those truths, unbelief grows in distorted emotions and patterns of life. Faithful response and mission then flow out of trust in the gospel rather than pressure to earn God’s favor.

Who is God?

1 · Character of God

  • God speaks, and his word is good. The command to honor father and mother came from the God who had already carried his people to himself, and he defends it against a holy-sounding evasion.
  • God is not satisfied with lips. He wants the heart that Isaiah said could drift far away, not because he despises our words, but because he wants us.
  • God keeps his promises in their order, and his mercy is not confined by that order. The children are fed first, and a foreign mother goes home to a free daughter.
  • God does not call anyone unclean for their body, their birth, or their people. What he names as defiling is the evil of the heart, in all of us.

What has God done?

2 · Work of God

  • In Jesus, God’s word came in person: defending his command, telling the truth about the heart, and explaining it to people who were slow to understand.
  • Jesus freed a daughter he never touched and touched a man he might have healed from anywhere, and no one watching both could mistake his power for a method.
  • Jesus gave his life as a ransom for many (10:45), meeting the defilement that rises from within us with a cleansing no washing could provide.
  • Jesus rose (16:6), and in him the hope that opened ears in the Decapolis is pledged for everything God will make new.

Who are we in light of what God has done?

3 · New Identity

  • We are people addressed by a word we did not invent, and our lives are both answerable to it and held by it.
  • Our uncleanness is real, but it is not the last thing said about us. What we could not wash away, God has dealt with in his Son.
  • Those of us from the nations are guests at a table whose promises came first to Israel. We sit there by mercy, not by rank, and so does everyone else.
  • Our dignity does not rest on how well we hear, speak, or fit in. It rests on being known and reached by Jesus.

What wrong behavior, harmful emotions, or false attitudes result when we forget these truths?

4 · Unbelief

  • We honor God with our words while quietly avoiding what he has commanded, and we mistake the words for obedience.
  • We use religious devotion as a shield against costly love, finding holy reasons not to help the people God has put in our care.
  • We locate evil mainly outside ourselves, in other groups, other nations, other people’s plates, instead of in the heart Jesus described.
  • We assume mercy is scarce and must be guarded from outsiders.
  • We read his list selectively, lingering over other people’s sins and passing quickly over our own greed, envy, slander, arrogance and folly.
  • We speak accurately, even gladly, about Jesus while resisting what he tells us to do.

How would we live if we believed these truths?

5 · Faithful Response

  • We would let God’s word judge our inherited practices, keeping with gratitude the ones that serve it and letting go of the ones that have begun to replace it.
  • We would help the people God has given us to help, especially where a pious excuse is close at hand.
  • We would bring our hearts to Jesus instead of trying to scrub them, praying through his whole list honestly and trusting what he has done for us more than what we can do for ourselves.
  • We would come to him boldly for the people we love, as the mother did, trusting that even a crumb of his mercy is enough.
  • We would praise him freely, and then do what he says.

If we lived these truths by faith, how might they overflow into the real places and relationships God has given us?

6 · Mission

  • In our families, we would honor parents in costly, practical ways, without pretending that honor requires anyone to accept harm.
  • In our churches, we would keep asking what our customs are serving, and open what we have closed in God’s name.
  • At our tables, we would make room for people who do not know whether there is room for them, especially those from other nations and backgrounds.
  • In public, we would refuse any speech that calls people unclean, dangerous, or less than human because of who they are or where they come from, and we would stand with those who are spoken of that way.
  • With Deaf and disabled neighbors and members, we would offer care that attends to how each person actually communicates and receives, without spectacle and without making belonging wait for a cure.

Hearing the Father’s Voice

The Bible is God’s love letter to us. When we read it, we do not want accusation, fear, shame, or distance to distort what God has spoken. In this passage, the Father shows us that his Son tells the truth about our hearts without abandoning us there, that he calls no one unclean for who they are, and that his mercy is not stopped by distance or kept at arm’s length from our bodies. Here is what this passage sounds like when the Father says it to you.

My child, true words come easily to your lips, good words, even words about me, while your heart can be somewhere else entirely. I am not fooled by them, and I am not driven away by what my Son has told you lives within you. I do not expose your heart to drive you away. I did not send him to improve your outside. I sent him to reach what no washing could reach, and he gave his life to reach it.

My child, you may have been told that you do not belong at my table, that your people, your language, your body, or your story make you unclean. That is not my word over you. My mercy is not scarce. In my Son, even a crumb from my table is abundance, and in him you are no stranger at that table but my own child. Whether your ears hear clearly or not, whether words come easily to you or not, wherever you were born, you are mine. This is my Son, whom I love. Listen to him!
