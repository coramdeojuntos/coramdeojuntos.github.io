More Than a Teacher

Big Picture: What’s Happening Here?

Twice in this passage someone calls Jesus “Teacher,” and both times the word arrives at the edge of death. The first time it comes from men in a boat that is filling with water: “Teacher, don’t you care if we drown?” The second time it comes from people who have arrived from a house where a girl has died: “Why bother the teacher anymore?” Neither sentence is an insult. A teacher is a good thing to have, and Mark has just spent a whole day by the lake showing us how Jesus teaches. But in each scene the title is joined to a judgment about Jesus: in the boat, whether he cares; on the road, whether there is any point asking more of him. A teacher can explain the kingdom from a boat; what can a teacher do about a boat that is going under? A teacher might have helped a sick girl; what can a teacher do for a dead one? Between those two verdicts Mark sets the question that governs everything here: “Who is this?”

He answers by bringing Jesus into four places where human help has run out, and he is careful not to make them the same place. A boat is being overwhelmed faster than the men in it can manage. A man lives among tombs, and no one was strong enough to subdue him. A woman has spent twelve years and everything she had on doctors, and she is worse. A father is told there is no longer any point in asking. Weather is not a demon. Bleeding is not possession. Death is not simply a more serious illness. What these scenes share is not one kind of trouble but one person walking into each of them, and in each, something no one else could move answers to his word.

Yet if all we carried away from these chapters were Jesus is very powerful, we would miss how Mark tells it. The power keeps taking the shape of attention. Jesus asks a tormented man his name. He sends him home with the word mercy to describe what happened. He stops in a pressing crowd to find the one person who touched him on purpose, and he calls her “Daughter.” He takes a dead girl by the hand, and then he tells her parents to give her something to eat. The one the wind and waves obey is also the one who thinks to tell a family to feed their child.

One more thing needs saying at the start, because this passage is so full of rescues. Every scene here ends with someone delivered. Mark is showing us who Jesus is; he is not handing us control over how our own storms, illnesses, and losses will end. Faithful people still die at sea, still live with illnesses no treatment reaches, still bury their children. What this passage offers is not a formula for the outcome but a person to trust when the outcome has passed out of our hands: a Lord who is far more than useful while life stays manageable.

Passage Commentary

From “Teacher” to “Who Is This?”

Mark 4:35–41

“That day when evening came.” Mark will not let us forget which day it is. Since morning Jesus has been sitting in a boat, teaching the crowd on the shore about seed and soil and a kingdom that grows in ways no one can see (4:1–34). Now, at evening, the same boat carries him away. “Leaving the crowd behind, they took him along, just as he was, in the boat.” The teacher of the parables has become a passenger. The crossing was his idea: “Let us go over to the other side.” And the other side, we will soon learn, is foreign country.

The squall is real. “The waves broke over the boat, so that it was nearly swamped.” Mark gives us no room to treat the danger as an overreaction. These men were in genuine trouble, and they were in it because they had set out when Jesus told them to go. Obedience did not exempt them from the weather. Meanwhile Jesus “was in the stern, sleeping on a cushion.” Mark offers no explanation. What Mark does tell us is what the disciples concluded from it.

“Teacher, don’t you care if we drown?” The question is more than a cry for help. It is a question about his heart. From where they stood, a sleeping Jesus looked like an absent Jesus, and an absent Jesus looked like an indifferent one. That is a very human conclusion, and Mark does not hide that the disciples reached it. But notice where they took it: to him. Whatever their fear had concluded, they carried it to the stern and woke him.

He got up and spoke to the weather: he “rebuked the wind and said to the waves, ‘Quiet! Be still!’” There is no prayer here, no appeal to a higher name, no visible struggle, only a command of the kind you give to something under your charge. “Then the wind died down and it was completely calm.” Earlier, in the synagogue at Capernaum, people watched him command an impure spirit and asked one another, “What is this? … He even gives orders to impure spirits and they obey him” (1:27). Here the disciples ask something larger: “Who is this? Even the wind and the waves obey him!” The question has moved from what to who.

Between the calm and that question comes a question of Jesus’ own: “Why are you so afraid? Do you still have no faith?” He asks it after the rescue, not before. He did not wait for their faith to be in order before he saved them. And his question does not condemn fear as a feeling; it presses on what their fear had decided about him, that because he was asleep he did not care. The word still suggests they had seen enough of him by now to trust him. Then Mark adds the strangest line in the scene: “They were terrified.” The calm did not end their fear; it moved it. Before, they were afraid of the sea. Now they are terrified in the presence of the one the sea obeys. The storm was dangerous. The man in the stern is greater. And he is in their boat.

No One Strong Enough

Mark 5:1–13

The boat reaches the far shore, “the region of the Gerasenes,” most likely largely Gentile country. “When Jesus got out of the boat,” before anything else happens, a man comes from the tombs to meet him. The crossing that nearly drowned the disciples ends at a graveyard, face to face with a man no one had been able to restrain or subdue. Notice what Jesus has done. He has crossed a storm-filled lake into foreign territory, and the first person he meets there is the one everyone on that shore had given up on: chained, shut out, living among the dead. Jesus went through the storm to get to him.

Mark lingers over this man’s condition, and each detail is a record of what failed. “No one could bind him anymore, not even with a chain.” He had often been chained hand and foot; he tore the chains apart and broke the irons. “No one was strong enough to subdue him.” Chains are what people reach for when they have run out of other ideas. They can contain a person; they cannot restore one. If you have been chained, locked away, or shut outside the life of your community, kept at a distance because no one knew what else to do with you, Jesus crossed the lake for someone exactly like you. And inside all that strength was a man in anguish: “Night and day among the tombs and in the hills he would cry out and cut himself with stones.” Mark is not describing a monster. He is describing a human being in torment, living among the dead.

Mark also tells us plainly what was wrong: this man had “an impure spirit.” We should let Mark say what he says, and not quietly exchange his account for a modern diagnosis he never gave. But we must be just as careful in the other direction. Mark tells us what was happening to this man because he knew it and said so. He hands us no list of symptoms for identifying anyone else. In the lives of our neighbors, self-harm, unusual strength, isolation, and cries in the night are reasons for care, often for skilled medical care, and never evidence that someone is demonized. This story is about this man, and about the one who came ashore to meet him.

“When he saw Jesus from a distance, he ran and fell on his knees in front of him.” Then comes a shout: “What do you want with me, Jesus, Son of the Most High God? In God’s name don’t torture me!” Just before this, on the lake, the disciples were asking who Jesus is. Here the answer is shouted, by a hostile voice that knows exactly whom it faces and wants nothing to do with him. Knowing Jesus’ true title is not the same as trusting him. This recognition comes with dread and bargaining, not with faith. Mark then explains the shout: Jesus had already said, “Come out of this man, you impure spirit!” The one who spoke to the wind has spoken again, and now it is the spirit that pleads.

Jesus asks, “What is your name?” The answer comes back: “My name is Legion … for we are many.” Legion was a word from the vocabulary of armies, but Mark supplies its meaning himself, and that is where we should stay: many. The man’s own voice has been crowded out by we. We have no need to turn the name into a coded message about Rome, and the herd that follows does not give us a headcount.

Then the spirits beg, first “again and again” not to be sent out of the area, then to be sent into a large herd of pigs feeding on the nearby hillside. “He gave them permission.” The spirits come out and go into the pigs, and the herd, “about two thousand in number,” rushes down the steep bank into the lake and drowns. Mark does not tell us why Jesus allowed it, and an honest reader leaves that question open rather than hurrying to close it. The loss was real: animals dead, and very likely a livelihood gone with them. What Mark does let us see is the difference between what the spirits’ presence did to a herd and what Jesus’ word did for a man.

Asked to Leave, Sent to Tell

Mark 5:14–20

The herdsmen run to the town and the countryside, and people come out to see. When they arrive, Mark gives them, and us, three plain descriptions. The man is “sitting there, dressed and in his right mind.” He is sitting, where he had roamed the tombs and the hills. He is dressed, among people. He is in his right mind, which is Mark’s way of describing a restored man, not a category for sorting people into the whole and the unwhole. This is what Jesus’ authority looked like when it had done its work: not a spectacle but a man able to sit quietly among his neighbors. His worth did not begin in that moment; he was no less a man in the tombs. But now he could live among people again.

Then comes the last clause of Mark’s sentence: “and they were afraid.” They are not afraid of the man anymore. They are afraid of what has happened to him. The eyewitnesses tell the story again, “and told about the pigs as well,” and the people “began to plead with Jesus to leave their region.” Mark does not tell us why. Perhaps the loss of the herd weighed on them; perhaps a power that could do this was simply too much to have nearby. Mark leaves their hearts to God, and so should we. What we can see is that the same power that calmed the lake can be felt as a threat to the way things are, and that one response to it is to ask Jesus to go. He goes. He does not force his presence on people who plead with him to leave.

As Jesus is getting into the boat, the man who had been demon-possessed begs to go with him. It is a beautiful request. He is asking for exactly what the Twelve had been appointed for, “that they might be with him” (3:14). And Jesus says no. “Go home to your own people and tell them how much the Lord has done for you, and how he has had mercy on you.” For this man, following Jesus means staying. The refusal is not a rejection. It is a commission, shaped to him: his home, his own people, his own story.

Notice the word Jesus chooses for what happened in the tombs. He does not say, Tell them how strong I am. He calls it mercy. Then Mark tells us what the man did: he “began to tell in the Decapolis how much Jesus had done for him.” Jesus speaks of what the Lord has done; Mark reports the man telling what Jesus had done. Mark sets the two sentences side by side and adds no comment. Jesus does not announce his divine identity in so many words, and we should not put words in his mouth. But the correspondence is striking, and it belongs with the question asked in the boat. Who is this? Someone whose work Mark can describe in the same breath as the Lord’s.

“And all the people were amazed.” Mark does not say the region believed, and we should not say it for him; amazement is not the same as faith. Still, the region that asked Jesus to leave is not left without a witness. And this man’s commission is his own. Before the chapter ends, Jesus will give a household the opposite instruction.

So Jesus Went with Him

Mark 5:21–24

Jesus crosses back, and a large crowd gathers around him by the lake. Out of it comes “one of the synagogue leaders, named Jairus.” Mark gives us his name and his role and nothing else: not his income, not his earlier opinion of Jesus, not his standing in town. What he shows us is a father on the ground. “When he saw Jesus, he fell at his feet.” A man who shared in the leadership of his synagogue is at Jesus’ feet for the reason any parent would be. “My little daughter is dying. Please come and put your hands on her so that she will be healed and live.” Mark says he “pleaded earnestly.” There is no bargaining in it, and no pretense.

This is the second time in the passage someone has gone down before Jesus, and there will be a third. The man from the tombs fell on his knees, and what came out of him was dread. Jairus falls at his feet in desperate hope. The woman will fall at his feet trembling. It is the same posture held by three very different hearts. Mark is not turning kneeling into a technique. He is showing us how many different kinds of people end up at Jesus’ feet.

“So Jesus went with him.” Before any healing happens, the first thing Jesus gives this father is his company on the road. The crowd comes too. “A large crowd followed and pressed around him,” so closely that in a moment no one will be able to say who is touching him.

Found in the Crowd and Called Daughter

Mark 5:25–34

“And a woman was there.” Mark could have kept moving toward Jairus’s house. Instead he stops to give us a history in four short clauses, and each one is a door closing. She “had been subject to bleeding for twelve years.” She “had suffered a great deal under the care of many doctors.” She “had spent all she had.” And “instead of getting better she grew worse.” Mark does not name her condition, and we should not name it for him. He does not say why the doctors failed. He simply tells the truth about one woman’s twelve years of suffering, expense, and decline, and he does not soften it. This is not a verdict on medicine. It is the honest record of a woman for whom every available help had run out, and who had been made poor by her illness. Many people know that story: the bills that took everything, the treatment that cost more than they had, the getting worse anyway. Jesus stopped the whole procession for a woman who had spent all she had. And a world where the sick must spend all they have and still grow worse is not a world his people can be comfortable with.

“When she heard about Jesus,” when news of him had reached her, “she came up behind him in the crowd and touched his cloak, because she thought, ‘If I just touch his clothes, I will be healed.’” Her plan is small and hidden: come from behind, touch, and go. She asks for nothing out loud. And “immediately her bleeding stopped and she felt in her body that she was freed from her suffering.” Mark lets us feel it from inside her, in her own body, before anyone else knows.

Then Jesus stops. “At once Jesus realized that power had gone out from him.” Mark does not tell us how that works, and it is wise not to fill in what he leaves out. He tells us instead what Jesus did: “He turned around in the crowd and asked, ‘Who touched my clothes?’” The disciples’ reply is perfectly sensible. People are crowding against him on every side, so how can he ask who touched him? “But Jesus kept looking around to see who had done it.” He has an urgent errand, a desperate father beside him, and a crowd pressing in, and still he will not let this touch stay anonymous. Her healing has already happened. What he is looking for now is her.

“Then the woman, knowing what had happened to her, came and fell at his feet and, trembling with fear, told him the whole truth.” Here is fear again, but this fear brings her forward instead of sending Jesus away. And he does not correct her trembling. He says, “Daughter.” It is the only time in Mark’s Gospel that Jesus calls anyone by that word. A woman who came hoping to be healed without being noticed is given a family word, in front of everyone. Mark does not tell us all that the word meant for her life afterward, and he does not need to. Jesus has not only stopped her bleeding. He has spoken to her as his own.

“Your faith has healed you.” We should not flinch from that sentence. Jesus says it, and he means it. But look at what her faith actually was. It was not a technique, and it was not a force she aimed at him. It was a reaching toward Jesus himself after everything else had failed. It may have been imperfectly understood, but it was aimed at the right person. Jesus names it faith, and he does not let it end with a hidden touch. He brings her face to face with him and adds his word to what her body already knew: “Go in peace and be freed from your suffering.” Her faith received what Jesus gave; it did not control him. And Mark gives us no warrant here to explain anyone’s unhealed suffering as a shortage of faith.

The whole truth she told was hers to tell, in that moment, to him. The story does not require every suffering person to lay out their private history before a crowd. What it shows is Jesus making room for this woman to be known by him. Nor is her story merely a delay in someone else’s. For twelve years her life had been interrupted. Here Jesus interrupts everything for her.

“Why Bother the Teacher Anymore?”

Mark 5:35–43

“While Jesus was still speaking, some people came from the house of Jairus, the synagogue leader. ‘Your daughter is dead,’ they said. ‘Why bother the teacher anymore?’” Here is the second teacher, and the second verdict. The messengers are not being cruel. They are being practical in the worst hour of a family’s life. A teacher’s help, they reason, had a limit, and the limit has been reached. Mark does not say that Jesus’ stop for the woman cost the girl her life, and he does not tell us when she died. Too late is how it looked from the road, to a father who had waited while Jesus stopped for someone else.

“Overhearing what they said, Jesus told him, ‘Don’t be afraid; just believe.’” Jairus has not asked for anything; Jesus speaks first. These words are for this father, at this moment, with this news still hanging in the air. They are not a rule that grief is unbelief, and they are not a command to feel calm. They ask him not to let the messengers’ verdict be the last word about Jesus, and to keep trusting the one who is still walking toward his house. Mark records no reply from Jairus. We do not know what he felt; we know only that he went on with Jesus. And honesty requires saying what this scene does not say. Many parents have trusted Jesus through a child’s dying and have not received their child back in this life. “Just believe” was a word of relationship spoken to a father, not a lever that moves outcomes.

Jesus lets no one follow him except Peter, James, and John. At the house there is “a commotion, with people crying and wailing loudly.” The grief is real, and Mark does not hurry past it. Jesus goes in and says, “Why all this commotion and wailing? The child is not dead but asleep,” and they laugh at him. They know death when they see it, and Mark wants us to know it too: the girl is dead. Jesus is not correcting a mistaken diagnosis. He is speaking about what her death is in his presence, something he can wake her from.

He puts them all out and takes the child’s father and mother and the three disciples in with him. Then comes the smallest possible scene for the largest thing that happens in this passage. “He took her by the hand and said to her, ‘Talitha koum!’ (which means ‘Little girl, I say to you, get up!’).” Mark keeps the words Jesus spoke and translates them for us, and we can receive his translation as it stands. There is a hand and a sentence, and no struggle and no ritual. “Immediately the girl stood up and began to walk around (she was twelve years old).” The woman had suffered for twelve years, and the girl had lived for twelve. Mark lets us notice the match without explaining it, and we can leave it there: two daughters, placed side by side.

“At this they were completely astonished.” Then come two instructions, and both are surprising. First, “He gave strict orders not to let anyone know about this.” That is the opposite of what he told the man across the lake, and Mark does not explain the difference, so we need not force one. Second, and last of all, he “told them to give her something to eat.” The greatest act in the passage ends with a practical instruction about a meal. The girl is not turned into a symbol. She is a twelve-year-old whose parents are told to feed her. She has come back to ordinary life, the kind of life that will one day end again, so her rising is not yet the final defeat of death. It is a sign, in one house, of who has authority over death.

Mark 4:35–5:43 In One Movement

“Who is this?” The disciples ask it on a calm lake at night, and Mark spends the rest of the passage answering. He does it by taking Jesus wherever human help has run out: a boat filling with water, a graveyard where chains had failed, a woman whose doctors and money were gone, and a house where the only thing left to do was mourn. Each time, something no one else could move answers to Jesus: wind and waves, a legion of spirits, a flow of blood that would not stop, and death itself. And each time, the authority comes as mercy with a face. There is a man sitting clothed among his neighbors and sent home to tell of mercy, a trembling woman called Daughter and sent away in peace, and a girl lifted by the hand and given something to eat. Twice people call him “Teacher” at the edge of death: once questioning whether he cares, once concluding there is no point in bothering him further. Mark lets both judgments be spoken, and then overturns what they assume about him. He does not do it by explaining why Jesus slept or why he stopped. He does it by showing who Jesus is. Fear runs through every scene and never means just one thing. It questions his care, bows in awe, asks him to leave, and trembles its way to his feet. What Jesus asks for is not the absence of fear but trust in him, a trust that keeps going with him when the report says there is nothing left to ask. That trust does not buy the ending. Those who follow Jesus will still face storms that do not calm, illnesses that no treatment reaches, and graves that stay closed in this life. But they do not face them with a teacher whose help has run out. They face them with the Lord whose authority reaches past the end of every human help, and whose authority keeps taking the form of mercy.

Scripture Cross-References

Psalm 107:28–29

“Then they cried out to the LORD in their trouble… He stilled the storm to a whisper; the waves of the sea were hushed.” Israel’s Scriptures credit God with quelling storms. Mark quotes none of them. He simply lets the disciples watch the wind and the waves obey Jesus and ask who he is, and that question grows heavier the more of Israel’s Scriptures a reader brings into that boat.

Jonah 1

Here too there is a boat and a storm. A man is asleep while others fear they will perish, the sea grows calm, and the sailors are more afraid afterward than they were before. Mark does not mention Jonah, and the resemblance is a suggestive comparison rather than something Mark states. But the difference is what matters. Jonah tells the sailors that the only way forward is to throw him overboard. Jesus gets up and speaks to the wind and the waves.

Mark 3:27

“No one can enter a strong man’s house without first tying him up.” Jesus said this when he was accused of driving out demons by the prince of demons. In the tombs, people had tried to bind the man himself, and “no one was strong enough to subdue him.” Jesus does not put chains on the captive. He deals with what held him, and the spirits that held him beg him for terms.

1 Corinthians 15:26

“The last enemy to be destroyed is death.” Jairus’s daughter truly rose, but she rose into ordinary mortal life, and those who belong to Christ still grow sick, grow old, and die while they wait for that final victory. Her rising is a sign of who holds authority over death, not a promise that every grave will open now.

Revelation 21:4

John’s vision of the end promises that God will wipe away every tear and that death, mourning, crying, and pain will be no more. In Mark 5 a woman’s suffering ends and a house full of wailing receives its daughter back, one person at a time. The vision describes a day when that will be true for all of God’s people together. Until then, Jesus’ rescues are glimpses of that day. They are not guarantees that it has already arrived in every life.

The Shape of a Kingdom People

The disciples carried their worst conclusion straight to Jesus: “Teacher, don’t you care if we drown?” He answered with a calm sea and then a hard question of his own. A community shaped by this passage makes room for that kind of honesty. People should be able to say aloud, among other believers, It looks as if he is asleep, without being told that their fear proves they have no faith. Jesus did press the disciples about what their fear had concluded about him. He did not treat fear itself as a disqualification, and later in this same passage he called a trembling woman “Daughter.” Mark will later show Jesus himself “deeply distressed and troubled” in Gethsemane (14:33). Distress is not the same as unbelief. What a kingdom people learns to resist is the final verdict, the conclusion that he does not care, and not the anguish that brings that question to him.

Jesus crossed to the other side, and the first person he met there was a man living among the tombs, beyond the power of chains and irons to restrain. Whatever those restraints were meant to accomplish, they had not restored him. Chains are containment, not restoration. A kingdom people will refuse two opposite mistakes. We will not explain away what Mark tells us about this man, and we will not take his story and use it to label our neighbors. People who harm themselves, hear voices, or live with psychiatric illness are not illustrations for sermons about spiritual warfare. They are neighbors to be loved, patiently and for the long run, often with the help of skilled doctors and counselors. What this story does ask of us is where our care should aim: at a person sitting among us, clothed and at home with their own people, and not merely kept at a safe distance. And we cross toward such people as Jesus did, without any trace of superiority.

In the crowd, Mark is honest that treatment can fail, that it can cost everything, and that a person can grow worse under care. That honesty does not make medicine faithless. It does make a church pay attention to people who have spent all they have: the family without insurance, the worker who cannot take a day off to be seen, the immigrant afraid to go to the clinic. A kingdom people will help carry those costs and open those doors. A church can pray for the sick and support good medical care in the same breath. It can grieve with those whose treatment has failed without blaming them. It can stay beside people whose illness is chronic, the ones whose lives are not a dramatic story and whose suffering may go on for years. Mark gives us no warrant here to explain unhealed suffering as deficient faith. The healing Jesus gave this woman was good, and we can rejoice in every healing still. But she was also called Daughter, and the people in our churches who are still ill, still in pain, still waiting, are no less daughters and sons for it.

Jairus’s house was full of wailing, and Jesus went in anyway. A kingdom people honors grief. We do not hurry the bereaved toward calm, and we do not promise them what Jesus has not promised us, that if they believe enough, the one they lost will come back now. What we can offer is presence, the kind of ordinary care this passage ends with, someone making sure there is food on the table, and the hope that death is not beyond the reach of Jesus’ word, even though its final defeat is still to come.

On the far shore, Jesus left behind a witness. The restored man was not sent to put on a show. He was sent home to tell “how much the Lord has done for you, and how he has had mercy on you.” In this passage, testimony is a person telling their own people about mercy. Not every story is for broadcasting, though: the same chapter contains both “go home … and tell them” and “not to let anyone know.” Part of maturity is learning which is being asked of us, without turning either into a rule. Nor should any of us imagine that we carry Jesus’ authority the way he carries it. Mark soon describes the Twelve sent out with real but delegated authority (6:7–13), and a little later he describes them failing (9:18). Ministry shaped by this passage is dependence on Jesus, not command over storms and spirits. The one who crossed the lake to the tombs is still the Lord. We are the ones he sends, and sometimes the ones he tells to go home.

Gospel-Shaped Response

From this passage we can learn to move in a grace-first order. We begin with who God is and what he has done, because our identity is shaped by his character and work, not by our performance. When we forget those truths, unbelief grows in distorted emotions and patterns of life. Faithful response and mission then flow out of trust in the gospel rather than pressure to earn God’s favor.

Who is God?

1 · Character of God

  • God is Lord over what no human strength can master: wind and waves, hostile spirits, long illness, and death. In Jesus that lordship travels in a small boat with frightened people.
  • His authority is joined to mercy. When Jesus sent a freed man home, the word he gave him for what had happened was mercy.
  • He attends to persons, not only to problems. In a crowd pressing on every side, Jesus kept looking for one woman.

What has God done?

2 · Work of God

  • In Jesus, God calmed a storm for disciples before he addressed their faith; the rescue came first.
  • He set free a man no one could subdue, and returned him, seated, clothed, and in his right mind, to his own people.
  • He healed a woman whose every other help had failed, called her Daughter, and sent her away in peace.
  • He went on to a house where death had been announced, took a girl by the hand, raised her, and told her parents to feed her.

Who are we in light of what God has done?

3 · New Identity

  • We belong to a Lord whose authority does not end where ours does, so our standing with him does not depend on life staying manageable.
  • We are daughters and sons whom he addresses, not cases to be processed in a crowd.
  • We may come to him trembling. Fear does not cancel belonging, and our worth is not measured by whether our bodies or minds have been mended yet.
  • We are witnesses of mercy, each sent to particular people and places.

What wrong behavior, harmful emotions, or false attitudes result when we forget these truths?

4 · Unbelief

  • We reach a settled verdict that Jesus does not care because his care is hard to see, and we let that verdict harden into distance from him.
  • We treat Jesus as a useful teacher, worth consulting while things are manageable but not worth bothering once they are not.
  • We ask him to leave when his presence unsettles what we are used to controlling.
  • We turn faith into a technique for securing outcomes, and then we blame ourselves or others when the outcome does not come.
  • None of this means that fear, grief, anxiety, illness, or unanswered questions are unbelief in themselves. The disciples were pressed about a conclusion they had reached about Jesus, and a trembling woman was called Daughter.

How would we live if we believed these truths?

5 · Faithful Response

  • We would bring our own version of “don’t you care” to Jesus rather than carry it away from him.
  • We would keep trusting Jesus himself when the report says there is nothing left to ask, without turning that trust into a claim on a particular ending.
  • We would come forward, trembling if need be, and receive his word of peace.
  • We would obey the call that is actually ours, whether that means going with him or going home.

If we lived these truths by faith, how might they overflow into the real places and relationships God has given us?

6 · Mission

  • We would tell our own people, plainly, how much the Lord has done for us and how he has had mercy on us, as testimony rather than spectacle.
  • We would move toward people others have given up on, and stay near those whose help has run out (the chronically ill, those in psychiatric distress, the bereaved) without diagnosing them or promising them outcomes.
  • We would offer ordinary, bodily care, such as meals, clothing, and a place to sit among us, as small signs of the day when God wipes away every tear.
  • We would serve in dependence on Jesus, remembering that his authority is his own, and that anything entrusted to us is delegated, limited, and never a substitute for him.

Hearing the Father’s Voice

The Bible is God’s love letter to us. When we read it, we do not want accusation, fear, shame, or distance to distort what God has spoken. In this passage, the Father shows us that his Son is more than a teacher for manageable days: he is the Lord whose authority reaches past the end of human help, and whose authority keeps taking the form of mercy toward particular people. Here is what this passage sounds like when the Father says it to you.

My child, you have called my Son “Teacher,” and he is. But when the water came over the side, and when the report came that there was nothing left to ask, you were never left with only a teacher. The wind and the waves obeyed him. And his authority has never been cold. He crossed a storm to reach a man everyone had given up on. He stopped in a pressing crowd for a woman who had spent all she had, and called her Daughter. He took a dead girl by the hand. When his care is hard for you to see, do not let what you cannot see become your verdict about him.

My child, I have not placed the endings of your storms and sorrows in your hands, and I do not ask you to pretend you are not afraid. Come to my Son trembling, if that is how you come; trembling does not keep you out. Keep trusting him when you cannot see how this will end. Your worth to me is not measured by whether your body or your mind has been mended yet, or by what you have left in your hands. Death is not beyond his word, and one day I will wipe every tear from your eyes.
